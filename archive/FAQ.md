# FAQ — Objections, Edge Cases, and Honest Answers

Every other file in this repo describes the system working. This one is for
when it doesn't: the objections that are actually good, the failures that are
actually common, and the questions the rest of the repo is too enthusiastic
about itself to answer straight.

Where a criticism lands, it's conceded below. A plan that can't survive its
own objections was never a plan — it was an intention with formatting.

---

## Doubts about the whole enterprise

### Isn't this just an elaborate form of procrastination?

Sometimes, and the risk is structural rather than incidental. Planning is the
most pleasant part of learning: it produces the sensation of progress at zero
cost, with no possibility of failing at it. Rereading `03-spine.md` feels like
work and isn't.

The test is arithmetic. Count the hours you have spent inside this repo
against the artifacts you published this year. If that ratio is worse than
one to five, you are decorating rather than studying. The countermeasure is
already in the system — nothing gets checked off without a published
artifact (`01-principles.md`, principle 3), and the weekly log makes the gap
visible in weeks instead of years.

Practically: if you are reading this file before you have published anything,
stop reading. Open `03-spine.md` to year 1, pick the first domain, get the
survey book. The plan is finished. The reading isn't.

### Isn't a seventy-year plan absurd? I don't know what I'll want in year 30.

You don't, and neither does the plan — which is why it is built the way it
is. `03-spine.md` calls itself "a default sequence, not a contract." The
README calls the phase files forecasts and tells you to revise them every few
years.
Almost nothing here is a prediction about your preferences.

What is actually fixed is infrastructure: twenty-five hours a week, a daily
repetition habit, the artifact rule, tiers with concurrency caps, an annual
review empowered to rewrite any of it, and a slot held permanently open for
fields that don't exist yet (`04-frontier.md`). Those hold regardless of what
you want in year 30, because they describe how you learn rather than what.

Concede the real cost, though: a seventy-year document invites you to treat
it as binding, and people do. The failure isn't wanting something different
decades in — it's wanting something different and not editing the file.

### Isn't breadth just shallowness with better branding?

Take the case for specialization at full strength, because it is strong.
Specialists win on nearly every measurable outcome: income, citations,
promotion, hiring, prizes, and the odds of doing work that changes a field.
Depth compounds inside a domain the way nothing else does — the tenth book
in a field reads five times faster than the first, and that advantage is
only available to someone who read the nine. Most self-described polymaths are
people with a wide reading list and no results, and they are tedious.

The repo does not dispute any of that; it builds the spike first.
`03-spine.md`'s reordering rule 5 is "the spike always wins," and "a polymath
with no spike is an audience member" is the harshest sentence in the repo
about its own reader.

What breadth actually buys is combinatorial: 44 domains make 946 pairs, and
the interiors of fields are crowded while the edges are not
(`resources/bridges.md`). But that payoff is lumpy, unmeasurable, and may
never arrive for you specifically. The honest version is: specialize, then
add breadth on top, and admit that part of your reason is that it's a life
you would rather live.

### What if I'm not smart enough for some of these domains?

Split the question by tier, because the honest answers differ.

T3 literacy — stating a field's core questions, following an expert, knowing
where its canon is — is not ability-gated for anyone who can read this file.
It is gated by prerequisites, by the wrong first book, and by the wrong mode.
What almost always reads from inside as "not smart enough" is arriving at
genetics without statistics, or neuroscience without biology, which is
exactly why `03-spine.md` publishes its prerequisite chains instead of just
its order. Fix the sequence before you conclude anything about yourself, and
try a second survey and a different mode before you conclude it twice.

T1 is different, and pretending otherwise would be a lie. The top of research
mathematics, theoretical physics, and a handful of other fields is closed to
most people permanently, including most people who work extremely hard. That
is fine. The plan asks for three or four masteries out of forty-four domains,
and nothing anywhere specifies which three.

### Doesn't AI make memorizing and learning all this pointless?

The objection is serious and gets partly conceded. For retrieval of facts it
is largely right, and the repo already routes around this rather than arguing
with it — `resources/fundamentals.md` treats working with AI as the single
largest multiplier on breadth in the whole system, precisely because it makes
orientation cheap.

What survives is the difference between what you can think *with* and what
you can look up. Retrieval is a separate act you have to initiate; knowledge
you hold participates in every thought without being asked. You cannot
recognize that a question is worth asking in a field you know nothing about,
you cannot notice when an answer is wrong, and verification — the actual
skill, since an unverified model answer is a rumor — requires holding enough
of a domain to know where confident nonsense tends to live.

Don't overclaim it. That argument rests on judgment staying hard to automate,
which is a bet about a decade rather than a law of nature. If the bet loses,
the breadth was still worth having for reasons that were never instrumental.

### Isn't this a recipe for burnout?

It is designed against burnout and it is now much closer to causing it, and
that concession comes first because the hour budget moved and the honest
answer moved with it. Twenty-five hours a week is not a hobby's worth of
time. It is a part-time job, run for seventy years, on top of whatever else
your life contains. At ten hours a week the design margin was real; at
twenty-five it is thin, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling the plan
rather than describing it.

What still protects you: principle 8 treating sleep and exercise as
infrastructure rather than as competition for study time; the spine's
alternation rule, which pairs a hard prerequisite-chained domain with a free
one every year specifically so there is something to read on a bad week; a
curiosity budget that is unplanned and demands no artifact; and the fact
that three domains a year does not mean three at equal pressure in the same
week — one runs hot, the others are held warm.

Where it goes wrong is arithmetic. Twenty-five hours means twenty-five hours
total, and it only exists if the rest of your life is arranged to leave it
there. Inside a demanding degree or job it usually isn't, and then the
spine's own instruction applies: cut the ledger and run fewer domains a year
— the professional program *is* the spike. Most burnout here comes from
stacking the agenda on top of a full life instead of inside it, and a larger
budget makes that mistake larger rather than safer.

The other mechanism isn't hours at all. A schedule you are permanently behind
on costs more than the studying does. That's the last question in this file,
and it's the more common killer.

## Falling behind and breaking

### I've done nothing for six months. Is it over?

No, and the shape of the recovery matters more than the fact of it. Do not
audit the six months. Do not build a catch-up schedule. Do not wait for
January.

The repo's rule is deliberately tiny: any lapsed track restarts at the next
weekly log, at half size if necessary. So this week, open the log, write one
row, and do one deliberate hour of one thing. That is the entire recovery
procedure, and anything more elaborate is a way of not starting.

`phases/decade-1-foundations.md` names the actual failure mode, and it isn't
the six months — it's "silent habit death": a streak that breaks in a hard
season and never restarts. Broken streaks are normal across seventy years and
the schedule carries slack for exactly this. What kills agendas is that
restarting requires admitting the lapse, so it gets deferred, and the
deferral compounds. Make the restart cheap enough that pride isn't involved.
Half size counts. An empty week logged as empty counts.

### I keep starting domains and not finishing them.

Diagnose before you resolve, because "try harder" has already failed. Three
causes cover most cases. First, the artifact was never named — a domain with
no specific thing that must exist by December has no ending, so it can only
be abandoned, never finished. Second, you stacked two or three hard
prerequisite-chained domains in the same year, which is the exact thing the
alternation rule exists to prevent and which three-a-year makes much easier
to do by accident. Third, the survey book was wrong for you and you concluded
the field was.

Then apply the structural fix: no new domain starts until the current
artifact is published. Three a year does not repeal that — it means one
domain at full pressure while the others are held warm, in sequence, not
three simultaneous starts in January. That is a real constraint and it will
feel bad in month four, which is the point — `02-map.md` says the caps hold
"even when it hurts."

Concede the remaining possibility. Some people genuinely prefer beginnings,
and if that is you, no amount of insight will change it; only the constraint
above will.

### I hate the domain I'm on. Can I skip it?

Yes — "order is yours" is in `02-map.md`, and rule 2 of the spine's
reordering rules says opportunity beats sequence. But separate two things
first, because from inside they feel identical. Difficulty at week three is
normal and is where the learning actually happens; most literacy passes are
unpleasant somewhere in the middle. Genuine dislike survives a change of book
and a change of mode.

So run that test before deciding: a different survey, and one of the
non-textual modes from `resources/modes.md` — a museum, a person, a dataset,
the thing in your hands. Fields are often hated in one channel and liked in
another.

If you still hate it, defer rather than delete. Reordering rule 4: a domain
that won't start after two attempts is information, twice-deferred moves to a
later decade, four-times-deferred belongs in your anti-goals. Write the
deferral into the annual review, so that it's a decision instead of a drift.

### I fell behind by five years. Do I restart the plan?

No, and the spine anticipates you by name: "Falling five years behind the
sequence is not failure; it's the schedule working as designed." Seventy
years for 44 domains is generous on purpose, and the slack exists to absorb
illness, a demanding program, a child, a move, a job that ate everything.
The larger hour budget makes the slack larger, not smaller: five years is
now a third of the ledger's whole length rather than a sixth of it, which
sounds worse and is in fact the same fifteen domains.

Practically, nothing needs to happen except arithmetic. Open the spine at the
domain you are actually on and continue — the sequence's value is the
prerequisite order, not the year numbers attached to it. The ledger completes
around year 20 instead of year 15, still inside Decade 2. Then reread the
second half of `03-spine.md` and notice how little that changes: mastery,
re-foundation, promotion, the frontier slot, and transmission are the work of
decades 3 through 7, and none of them care what year the ledger closed.

Resist the restart instinct generally. Restarting is a way of making the past
not count, and it costs you everything you actually learned.

### Life blew up — illness, a death, a divorce, a job loss. What happens to the plan?

The plan yields, completely, and it does not ask to be informed. There is no
version of this repo in which a curriculum outranks a crisis, and any
accounting you do during one is effort spent where you cannot spare it.

If you want a floor, make it ten minutes of repetition a day, because it
protects years of accumulated work at almost no cost and can be done badly.
If even that goes, it goes.

Two honest notes. First, recovery time is not a lapse to apologize for in the
next review — write it down as a fact, the way you would write down a year
spent on a thesis. Second, some events end tracks permanently: a language you
will not return to, a craft your hands can't do now, a spike that belonged to
a life you are no longer living. Closing those deliberately at a review is
the discipline principle 7 describes; drifting out of them silently is not.
Grieving them is allowed. The map doesn't shrink; your relationship to parts
of it does.

## Changing your mind

### I want to change my mastery spike in year 12.

Legitimate, expensive, and usually the wrong framing.

Timing first: this decision belongs in an annual review, not in the week you
had it, for the same reason `04-frontier.md` refuses promotions in the moment
of excitement. Then ask what is actually happening, because pull toward a new
field and push away from the current one feel identical and have completely
different remedies. A bad manager is not a reason to spend six years
retraining.

Now the framing correction. `01-principles.md`'s π-shape says a second spike
is supposed to arrive right about now. Most people who want to change spikes
in year 12 actually want to *add* one, and adding is the stronger move,
because original work comes from holding two competencies few people hold
together. A swap throws that away and buys nothing back.

If it really is a swap, price it honestly: T1 costs five to ten years, so
starting in year 12 means mastery around year 20. That's survivable. Just
don't pretend the first twelve years transfer less than they do. Method,
taste, and having gone deep once all carry across.

### I want to add a domain that isn't on the map.

Add it. `02-map.md` says the ledger is open at the bottom, and it means it: a
map that can't grow is a map that expires. Two checks before you write the
row.

First, is it a field or a tool? Nothing with a version number belongs on the
map (principle 5). If it looks like a tool but might be a paradigm shift
wearing a tool's clothes, that is exactly what `04-frontier.md`'s triage
exists for — apply test 1, does it change what can be *asked*, and take your
time over it.

Second, it enters at T3 like everything else, with no exemption for
enthusiasm. Write it into your copy of `02-map.md`, give it a year in the
spine with an actual prerequisite argument rather than a slot at the front,
and let the literacy artifact decide whether it earns more.

If it's new to the world rather than merely new to you, that's the frontier
slot's job, and there is a whole file about how to tell.

### I no longer believe in one of the map's premises.

Then name it in writing and edit the file. Principle 7 asks you twice a year
to review the system itself, and this is that. The repo is a working
document, not scripture.

But know which premises are load-bearing before you pull one out. Roughly
decorative: the specific 44 domains, the sequencing, the decade themes, most
of the equipment advice. Structural: artifacts as the only thing that earns a
checkmark, tiers as budgets with concurrency caps, fundamentals before
territory, and the review cycle itself.

Drop a decorative premise freely — that's maintenance. Drop a structural one
and you should be able to say what replaces it. Remove the artifact rule, for
instance, and you have removed the only unfakeable measurement in the system;
what's left is counting hours, which measures nothing and flatters everyone.

Disagreement recorded and acted on is the system working. Disagreement that
shows up as quietly doing less is the failure mode this repo exists to
prevent.

### My field is being automated away.

This is the scenario the second spike and the frontier slot were built for,
which is cold comfort if it's happening now.

Get specific first, because "field" is too coarse a unit. Ask what
`04-frontier.md`'s first triage question asks: is the thing changing what
your field can *ask*, or industrializing answers it already produced? The
routine tier of a field being automated is common and survivable. The
questions changing is a re-foundation — which is a scheduled, normal
operation in this system rather than an emergency.

Then the concrete moves. Run a re-foundation pass on the part that moved,
using material published in the last three years. Lean the second spike
toward wherever your field's judgment is still scarce. Check the fundamentals
table in `02-map.md`, because those rows transfer across any field boundary
and are the reason relearning is fast.

Honestly: retraining decades in is hard, and nothing here makes it painless.
What the plan gives you is a person who has voluntarily learned twenty
unfamiliar fields from scratch, on a schedule, with a method that
demonstrably worked.
That is the actual transferable asset, and most people facing the same shock
don't have it.

## Practical mechanics

### How do I know when a literacy pass is actually done?

There is a stated bar and an unfakeable one.

Stated (`resources/README.md`): one survey work, one canonical work, and one
published artifact of one to three thousand words — what the field asks, its
biggest ideas, what surprised you, what you'd study next, and what you still
don't understand.

Unfakeable: you can explain the domain's five to eight big ideas to someone a
level below you, without notes, and survive one follow-up question. The
follow-up is the whole test. Fluent output you can't defend is the exact
failure `resources/fundamentals.md` warns about.

A third check runs for free afterward. `05-current.md` calls it placement:
when a story touches the domain, can you name the sub-field, the standing
questions, and why this is or isn't surprising? If not, the pass either never
took or has decayed — which is a re-foundation signal, not a verdict on you.

If you fail these, more hours in the same book will not fix it. A different
mode will.

### What if I can't afford the equipment?

Most of it you shouldn't buy anyway, and `resources/kit.md` argues that at
length. Borrow first: tool libraries and library "libraries of things,"
makerspaces at thirty to a hundred and fifty a month, university surplus
sales where professional microscopes and optics go for a tenth of new,
community college shop and lab courses, astronomy clubs with loaner
telescopes. A month with a borrowed object usually reveals that you wanted
the practice rather than the thing.

Then the genuinely free layer. A library card is named there as the
highest-leverage item in that whole file by an enormous margin, and
interlibrary loan reaches almost anything. The software that matters costs nothing —
Anki, LaTeX, R, Stellarium, PsychoPy, IMSLP. So do the flagship courses: MIT
OpenCourseWare, Open Yale, the Feynman Lectures, CORE Econ, Smarthistory,
3Blue1Brown.

Concede the remainder honestly. A few things cost real money — a chemistry
lab course, a language tutor — and those are precisely where the feedback
loop lives, so they're what to spend on. Defer a domain rather than fake it.

### I have less time than the default.

Most people do, and the default moved a long way up — twenty-five hours a
week, about 1,300 a year. So do the arithmetic and accept its answer,
instead of thinning every track to a fifth, which is how people end up with
nine things all failing at once.

The default funds three T3 domains a year and closes the ledger near year
15. Ten hours a week is about 500 a year: one or two domains, ledger
somewhere around year 30, and every structural feature of the plan intact.
Four hours is about 200 a year, which is the constrained fork in
`TIMELINE.md` — one domain a year, the ledger closing in the forties, a
finished map with decades left to use it. All three are good outcomes. The
one that isn't is running the 25-hour table at 20% and calling it the same
plan, because then nothing finishes and the log tells you so for years
before you believe it.

Say plainly what the smaller budgets cost, since the fork pages are
cheerful about it: they buy the same map and much less depth. Promotions to
working depth begin around year 6 at the default because there are hours
left over after the ledger; at four hours a week there are none, and the
plan becomes a breadth plan with one spike rather than a breadth plan with
three or four masteries in it.

Cut, don't thin. The order to protect: spaced repetition and the weekly log
first, because they cost fifteen minutes a day and five minutes a week and
they are what makes everything else retainable; then the spike, which always
wins; then one ledger domain; then the curiosity budget, which is not
optional because it's the canary. The frontier slot shrinks to one deliberate
afternoon a year, and current awareness to the annual tier — which
`05-current.md` argues is the highest-value tier anyway.

### Should I do this publicly or privately?

Publish the artifacts; keep the log and the review private. That split is the
whole answer, and the reason has nothing to do with audience.

Publishing works because it forces a piece to be finished and legible to a
stranger, which is exactly what the artifact is supposed to prove. "I
understand it" is unfalsifiable; a thing you shipped is not. The effect
survives having no readers at all — a page nobody visits still disciplines
the writing.

The costs are real. An audience quietly selects for what plays: domains that
sound impressive, opinions that travel, artifacts written for reception
rather than as evidence of work. It also makes public beginner-hood
expensive, and beginner-hood is something this agenda needs you to stay
comfortable with for seventy years.

The log and the review are where you're honest about drift and failure, and
honesty degrades under observation. Nobody needs your weekly rows.

### How do I handle domains where I can't get lab or archive access?

There are more routes than most people check, and the resource files name
them: community college lab courses for chemistry — the spine's year 5 says
to just take one, it's cheap and it's the honest route — archive reader's
cards, which are usually free and want only ID and proof of address,
volunteer archaeology and field schools that genuinely take amateurs,
hospital or hospice volunteering and EMT certification for medicine,
university open days, and citizen science with real scientific value such as
eBird, iNaturalist, and Zooniverse. Public data replaces a surprising amount
of lab access: GenBank, SDSS SkyServer, FRED, CourtListener.

Concede the rest. For a few domains the wall is real and permanent — law's
licensing wall is the clearest, clinical medicine the other — and the repo
already says the ledger pass there is literacy and nothing more. Read the
actual court opinions instead of books about them, then stop, and be exact
with yourself about what you have. A reading pass is not a practitioner's
knowledge. That difference is only embarrassing if you hide it.

### What if I don't have a full seventy years?

The tiers, the artifact rule, the caps, and the method don't depend on the
length of the runway — nothing in `01-principles.md` or `02-map.md` assumes
all seventy years are available. What changes is arithmetic, so do it plainly
rather than feeling vaguely late.

At the current pace the ledger is no longer what a short runway threatens.
Three domains a year closes it near year 15, so with fifty-five years or with
forty you have the whole structure: the map covered once well inside the
first two decades, then mastery, integration and transmission landing roughly
as written, just compressed at the back. What a short runway costs is depth,
because T1 is five to ten years and you can only stack so many of those. With
forty years, plan for two masteries rather than four and stop apologising for
it. With twenty-five, stop pretending you're at year 1: run the ledger and the
promotions concurrently from the start, cut the ledger to the domains you
actually want since the 44 were never sacred, and read the second half of the
plan directly — promotion of what already pulls, re-foundation of what's gone
stale, the frontier slot, transmission.
`phases/decade-5-reinvention.md` describes your situation better than decade 1
does.

Two advantages a standing start lacks: a spike already exists, and you read
much faster in everything adjacent to it. One thing that refuses to compress:
a craft and a language are time-on-instrument, and no accumulated judgment
buys those hours back.

## The long game

### What if I die before finishing?

Then you die partway through a curriculum, like every person who has ever had
one. There is no other available outcome — the plan runs to year 70, and
`03-spine.md` closes by telling you to write the next sketch anyway, because
there was never a finish line to cross.

The value was never held in escrow against completion. What you can think
with in year 20 is yours in year 20. The artifacts you published in Decade 1
existed and were read. The people you taught were taught. This is a plan
built to deliver continuously and to be interrupted at any point with most of
the value already banked — artifacts from year 1, teaching from year 3, a
body of work a stranger can evaluate by year 10. A ledger with thirty-one
boxes checked is not a failed seventy-year plan. It is thirty-one domains you
understood, inside a life that had them in it.

One practical concession, and it's the only one this question needs.
`phases/decade-7-long-view.md` says to hand things over concretely — books,
tools, notes, collections placed with named people while you can still
explain them. Don't schedule all of that for the final years.

### How do I keep this from becoming a source of guilt?

Concede the risk first, because it's structural: checkboxes, streaks, a
seventy-year schedule, and tables with blank cells are a guilt engine, and
shipping one without saying so would be irresponsible.

Several countermeasures are already load-bearing. The curiosity budget
answers to nothing and requires no artifact, and if it hits zero the repo
reads that as the canary rather than as discipline. An empty week logged as
empty is a valid entry. Lapsed things restart at half size. Quitting a line
of study deliberately at a review is described as discipline, not failure.

Add one distinction the repo implies but never states: guilt comes from an
unrevised plan you are silently failing, never from a plan you revised. So
the annual review has exactly two legitimate outputs for anything undone —
recommit with a structural change, or edit the file. "Still on the list,
still not done, no comment" is the state that rots.

And keep a real exit available. If a year of this produced guilt and no
artifacts, delete the schedule and keep three things: daily repetition, the
weekly log, one finished artifact a year. That's most of the value at a tenth
of the machinery, and it's a version you will still be running in year 40.
