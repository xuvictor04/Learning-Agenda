# Textbooks

Where `CATALOGUE.md` says a gap exists, this file says what to read instead.

Every entry here exists because **no adequate free course was found**, not
because a book seemed nice. If a good free course exists, it's in the
catalogue and it wins — a course gives you a sequence, a pace, and someone
else's judgement about what matters. A book gives you none of those. That's
the cost you're paying in this file, and it's the reason these are
substitutes rather than the default.

**Author and title only.** No chapters, no page numbers, no editions unless
the edition genuinely matters. The contents page does that work better than
I can, and a wrong reference costs more than a missing one.

**Free** marks a book that is legally free online — public domain, open
access, or an open-licensed press. Everything unmarked has to be bought or
borrowed. Interlibrary loan reaches most of them; university library
guest cards reach nearly all.

---

## How to work a textbook without a course

The transcript's rule is *problem sets are the course*. A textbook has no
grader, so the rule has to be replaced rather than dropped:

1. **Do the end-of-chapter problems**, and check them. Many textbooks
   publish odd-numbered answers; some have student solution manuals sold
   separately. Where none exists, the discipline has to come from writing
   your answer out in full before you look anything up.
2. **A textbook without an artifact is reading, not study.** Same rule as
   the courses. Written summary, worked problem set, translated passage,
   drawn plate, dataset analysed — one per book.
3. **Read the survey before the canon.** The pattern that fails most often
   here is opening Bourdieu or Foucault or Marx with no framing text first.
   Every section below names the survey and the canon separately for that
   reason.
4. **Two books beat one.** Almost every gap below lists a primary text and
   a complement, because a single author's blind spot goes invisible when
   they are your only source. A course has a syllabus committee behind it;
   a book has one person.

# Languages

## Latin

The largest gap in the catalogue. Nothing free teaches Latin as a course,
and this is normal — most classicists learned from a book.

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *Lingua Latina per se Illustrata: Familia Romana* | Hans Ørberg | Entirely in Latin from the first line, comprehensible from context. The best-loved route in, and the one that produces readers rather than parsers. Pair with *Latine Disco*, the student companion, and the *Exercitia* workbook |
| *Wheelock's Latin* | Frederic Wheelock, rev. Richard LaFleur | The grammar-translation standard. Slower to reading fluency than Ørberg, faster to grammatical precision. The free *Wheelock's Latin* answer keys and forums are extensive |
| *A New Latin Syntax* | E.C. Woodcock | The reference you graduate to when the introductory book stops explaining |
| *New Latin Grammar* | Allen & Greenough | **Free** (public domain, Perseus and elsewhere). The reference grammar everyone actually uses |

**Then read.** Dickinson College Commentaries (**free**) and Geoffrey
Steadman's commentaries (**free**) both give running vocabulary and notes on
real texts — Caesar, Ovid, Vergil, Cicero. Reading with a commentary is the
whole second half of learning the language. Perseus (**free**) supplies
parsed text for anything they don't cover.

## Greek, past the introductory course

Muellner's 118 lessons are in the catalogue and take you through the
beginning. After that:

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *Athenaze* | Balme & Lawall | The Ørberg-adjacent route — continuous narrative, reading-first |
| *Greek: An Intensive Course* | Hansen & Quinn | The other tradition. Brutal pace, complete coverage, the standard for people who need Greek fast |
| *Greek Grammar* | Herbert Weir Smyth | **Free** (public domain, Perseus). The reference grammar |

## Sanskrit, Pali, and the textual languages

Named in the catalogue as absent at advanced level.

- *Devavāṇīpraveśikā* — Robert Goldman & Sally Sutherland Goldman, for
  Sanskrit; the standard American university text
- *The Sanskrit Language* — Walter Maurer, a gentler alternative
- *A Sanskrit Grammar for Students* — Arthur Anthony Macdonell (**free**,
  public domain)

# History

## Historiography

No free course exists. The discipline's self-examination is entirely a
reading list, which is convenient — historiography was always more argued
in books than taught in lectures.

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *The Pursuit of History* | John Tosh | The textbook. Sources, method, the uses and abuses of the past. Start here |
| *What Is History?* | E.H. Carr | The famous argument that the historian's own position is inseparable from the history. Short and combative |
| *The Historian's Craft* | Marc Bloch | Written in the Resistance and unfinished when he was executed. The most humane book about how evidence works |
| *In Defence of History* | Richard Evans | Carr and the postmodernists answered, from a working historian who had to defend the discipline in court |
| *The Houses of History* | Anna Green & Kathleen Troup | A reader — each school of thought introduced, then a specimen text. The fastest way to see what Marxist, Annales, cultural, and gender history actually do differently |

**Artifact:** re-read something from the transcript's history courses and
write out which school its lecturer belongs to, from internal evidence.

## Latin America

Free courses are effectively absent. The book literature is excellent.

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *Born in Blood and Fire* | John Charles Chasteen | The best single-volume narrative. Written to be read, not consulted |
| *Modern Latin America* | Skidmore, Smith & Green | The country-by-country reference companion to Chasteen's narrative |
| *Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest* | Matthew Restall | Short, corrective, and the fastest way to unlearn the received story |
| *Open Veins of Latin America* | Eduardo Galeano | A polemic, not a history, and named as one. Read it for what a generation of Latin American readers took from it, with the survey already in hand |

## Africa

Same situation, same solution.

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *Africans: The History of a Continent* | John Iliffe | The standard single-volume history, organised around population and the struggle to make a hostile continent habitable |
| *History of Africa* | Kevin Shillington | The more conventional textbook — chronological, illustrated, easier as a first pass |
| *Africa since 1940: The Past of the Present* | Frederick Cooper | Decolonisation and after, by the historian who reframed the field |
| *Africa and Africans in the Making of the Atlantic World* | John Thornton | African agency in the Atlantic system, against the older passive-victim framing |

## The Middle East

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *A History of the Arab Peoples* | Albert Hourani | The classic long view, from the rise of Islam to the twentieth century |
| *A History of the Modern Middle East* | Cleveland & Bunton | The teaching textbook — clearer structure, better maps, less literary |
| *The Modern Middle East: A History* | James Gelvin | The best on the region as a product of its encounter with global capitalism and the modern state |
| *The Venture of Islam* | Marshall Hodgson | Three volumes, difficult, and the most ambitious account of Islamic civilisation written in English. Take it after one of the surveys, not first |

## South Asia

Served free only by NPTEL, at low production quality.

- *A Concise History of Modern India* — Barbara & Thomas Metcalf
- *A History of India* — Burton Stein, rev. David Arnold, for the longer span
- *The Idea of India* — Sunil Khilnani, for what the republic was meant to be

## Rome

Kagan's Greek course has no Roman equal, free or otherwise.

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *SPQR* | Mary Beard | The best modern one-volume Rome, and honest about what the evidence can't tell us |
| *The Roman Republic* | Michael Crawford | Compact, scholarly, the standard short account of the Republic |
| *The Fall of the Roman Empire* | Peter Heather | The end, argued as contingency and external pressure rather than decadence |

# Law

The largest structural gap after Latin. No free 1L sequence exists, and no
free doctrinal constitutional law course taught by a constitutional law
professor. What follows reconstructs the first year from books.

**Two free resources make this workable.** **CALI's eLangdell Press**
publishes free, openly licensed law casebooks across most 1L subjects, and
Harvard's **Open Casebook** (opencasebook.org) hosts free casebooks
including Fisher's copyright material. Cornell's **Legal Information
Institute** (**free**) supplies the actual text of statutes, the U.S. Code,
and annotated Supreme Court opinions. Between them you can read primary law
without buying anything.

## Learning to read law at all

- *The Bramble Bush* — Karl Llewellyn. What law school is trying to do to
  your reading, said out loud
- *An Introduction to Legal Reasoning* — Edward Levi. Short, and the
  clearest account of how a rule moves from case to case
- *Thinking Like a Lawyer* — Frederick Schauer

## The 1L subjects

The **Examples & Explanations** series is the honest recommendation here.
It is written for students to self-study alongside a casebook, it states
the doctrine plainly and then works problems, and it is the closest thing
to a course a book can be.

| Subject | Book | Author |
|---|---|---|
| Civil procedure | *Civil Procedure: Examples & Explanations* | Joseph Glannon |
| Torts | *The Law of Torts: Examples & Explanations* | Joseph Glannon |
| Contracts | *Contracts: Examples & Explanations* | Brian Blum |
| Property | *Property: Examples & Explanations* | Barlow Burke & Joseph Snoe |
| Criminal law | *Understanding Criminal Law* | Joshua Dressler |
| Constitutional law | *Constitutional Law: Principles and Policies* | Erwin Chemerinsky |

Chemerinsky is the substitute for the missing course, not a supplement to
it — it is the treatise American law students actually use to understand
constitutional doctrine, and it covers the standard syllabus the National
Constitution Center's material only gestures at.

**Pair the constitutional reading with** *America's Constitution: A
Biography* by Akhil Reed Amar — the document read clause by clause as a
historical artifact rather than a doctrinal source.

## Jurisprudence

- *The Concept of Law* — H.L.A. Hart. The century's central work of
  analytic legal philosophy
- *Law's Empire* — Ronald Dworkin. The reply
- *The Nature of the Judicial Process* — Benjamin Cardozo (**free**, public
  domain). A sitting judge on how he actually decides

## International law, past what Leiden covers

The catalogue names treaty law, law of the sea, and trade law as
uncovered.

- *Brownlie's Principles of Public International Law* — ed. James Crawford.
  The reference
- *International Law* — Malcolm Shaw. The more teachable alternative
- *Modern Treaty Law and Practice* — Anthony Aust
- *The Law of the Sea* — Churchill, Lowe & Sander
- *The Law and Policy of the World Trade Organization* — Van den Bossche &
  Zdouc

# Psychology and medicine

## Clinical and abnormal psychology

The catalogue calls this a genuine hole. Books can supply the knowledge;
nothing supplies the supervised hours, and no book should be mistaken for
clinical training.

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *Abnormal Psychology: The Science and Treatment of Psychological Disorders* | Kring & Johnson | The standard course text. Disorder by disorder, with the evidence for each treatment stated rather than assumed |
| *Abnormal Psychology: An Integrative Approach* | Barlow & Durand | The alternative standard, organised around the biopsychosocial model |
| *Cognitive Behavior Therapy: Basics and Beyond* | Judith Beck | What the most-evidenced therapy actually consists of, session by session |
| *Science and Pseudoscience in Clinical Psychology* | ed. Lilienfeld, Lynn & Lohr | The critical apparatus. Read it alongside, not after — a lot of clinical practice does not survive it |

**The DSM-5-TR** is the diagnostic reference and should be consulted rather
than read. Know that its categories are contested from inside the field —
the NIMH's RDoC framework (**free**, on the NIMH site) is the main
alternative and is worth reading for the argument.

## Pharmacology

Absent from the free landscape at professional depth, and the catalogue
says so. This is a genuine textbook subject and always has been.

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *Basic & Clinical Pharmacology* | Katzung | The standard course text. Mechanism first, then therapeutics |
| *Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics* | ed. Brunton et al. | The reference. Not a book to read through; a book to own |
| *Rang & Dale's Pharmacology* | Ritter et al. | The British standard, better written than Katzung and stronger on receptor theory |
| *Lippincott Illustrated Reviews: Pharmacology* | Whalen | The review book. Diagram-driven, built for retention, and the right companion to spaced repetition |
| *An Introduction to Medicinal Chemistry* | Graham Patrick | Why the molecule looks the way it does. The bridge from 5.07SC to the drug |

**Prerequisite reality:** pharmacology on top of biochemistry and
physiology is a subject. Pharmacology without them is memorisation with
nothing underneath it. Take 5.07SC and a physiology course first.

## Anatomy, without a cadaver

The dissection cannot be substituted. The visual knowledge partly can.

- *Color Atlas of Anatomy* — Rohen, Yokochi & Lütjen-Drecoll. Photographs
  of actual dissections rather than illustrations, and the closest a book
  gets to the lab
- *Atlas of Human Anatomy* — Frank Netter. The illustrated standard;
  clearer than reality, which is both its strength and its limit
- *Gray's Anatomy for Students* — Drake, Vogl & Mitchell. The text, as
  against the atlases

# Social sciences

## Post-1970 social theory

SOCY 151 stops around Weber. Everything since is books.

**Start with a reader**, not a primary text: *Contemporary Sociological
Theory* (ed. Calhoun et al.) gives you the excerpt and the framing
together, which is the difference between reading Bourdieu and bouncing off
him.

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* | Erving Goffman | The most readable major work in the tradition, and the one that changes how you see a conversation |
| *Outline of a Theory of Practice* | Pierre Bourdieu | Habitus, field, capital. Hard prose, foundational apparatus |
| *Distinction* | Pierre Bourdieu | Taste as class, demonstrated with data. The more approachable of the two |
| *Discipline and Punish* | Michel Foucault | Power as productive rather than repressive. The single most cited book on this list |
| *The Constitution of Society* | Anthony Giddens | Structuration — the attempted resolution of the structure-agency problem the whole tradition circles |
| *Reassembling the Social* | Bruno Latour | Actor-network theory, and an argument that most of the above asks the wrong question |
| *Black Feminist Thought* | Patricia Hill Collins | Standpoint theory and the case that the canon's view from nowhere was a view from somewhere |

## Qualitative sociology and the inequality tradition

Chetty's course gives you quantitative mobility and none of the
ethnographic literature.

- *Evicted* — Matthew Desmond
- *Unequal Childhoods* — Annette Lareau
- *Sidewalk* — Mitchell Duneier
- *$2.00 a Day* — Kathryn Edin & H. Luke Shaefer
- *Writing Ethnographic Fieldnotes* — Emerson, Fretz & Shaw, for the method
  rather than the findings

## Comparative politics

Thin to absent as lecture courses.

**The textbook:** *Principles of Comparative Politics* — Clark, Golder &
Golder. Formally rigorous, and the one that treats the field as a science
rather than a tour of countries. *Essentials of Comparative Politics* by
Patrick O'Neil is the lighter alternative.

**The canon**, which is where the field actually lives:

| Book | Author |
|---|---|
| *Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy* | Barrington Moore |
| *States and Social Revolutions* | Theda Skocpol |
| *Making Democracy Work* | Robert Putnam |
| *Patterns of Democracy* | Arend Lijphart |
| *Seeing Like a State* | James C. Scott |
| *Why Nations Fail* | Acemoglu & Robinson |

## International relations theory, past realism

The catalogue names the absence of a constructivist or critical
alternative to Van Evera.

- *International Relations Theories: Discipline and Diversity* — ed. Dunne,
  Kurki & Smith. The survey, one chapter per school, each by a partisan
- *Social Theory of International Politics* — Alexander Wendt. The
  constructivist statement
- *Bananas, Beaches and Bases* — Cynthia Enloe. Feminist IR, and the book
  that asks where the women are in a discipline that never noticed
- *Orientalism* — Edward Said. Postcolonial theory's founding text

## Human geography

Close to absent from free courses. Penn State's open courseware is
geospatial technique, not the discipline.

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *Human Geography: Places and Regions in Global Context* | Knox & Marston | The survey textbook |
| *Geographic Thought: A Critical Introduction* | Tim Cresswell | The discipline's own history and arguments — the historiography of geography, effectively |
| *Place: An Introduction* | Tim Cresswell | The central concept, unpacked. Short |
| *Space and Place* | Yi-Fu Tuan | Humanistic geography's classic |
| *For Space* | Doreen Massey | The theoretical end of the discipline |
| *Social Justice and the City* | David Harvey | Where geography meets political economy |

## Economic history

A genuine gap, not a search failure.

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *Global Economic History: A Very Short Introduction* | Robert Allen | Ninety-odd pages, the whole question of why some places got rich. The best door in existence |
| *The Lever of Riches* | Joel Mokyr | Technological creativity as the engine, across two millennia |
| *A Culture of Growth* | Joel Mokyr | The follow-up argument: why Europe, and why then — as a matter of ideas and institutions |
| *The Great Divergence* | Kenneth Pomeranz | The California School's answer, and the book that made the field argue about Europe's uniqueness again |
| *Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance* | Douglass North | The institutional framework the whole modern field runs on |
| *The Rise and Fall of American Growth* | Robert Gordon | One country, 1870 to now, in detail, with the case that the great growth was a one-time event |
| *Globalizing Capital* | Barry Eichengreen | The international monetary system as history |
| *Manias, Panics, and Crashes* | Kindleberger & Aliber | Financial crisis as a recurring pattern rather than a series of accidents |

## Heterodox economics

The catalogue flags that every free economics course comes from the
empirical American mainstream, and that the absence distorts.

- *Economics: The User's Guide* — Ha-Joon Chang. Nine schools of economics,
  each given its own best case. The right first book here
- *Capital, Volume I* — Karl Marx (**free**, public domain). Read with a
  companion; David Harvey's lecture series on it is free online
- *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy* — Hyman Minsky, for the
  post-Keynesian position
- *Individualism and Economic Order* — F.A. Hayek, for the Austrian one
- *The Value of Everything* — Mariana Mazzucato

# Business

## Strategy

No free course. This is the most book-served subject in the file — strategy
was built in books and articles, not classrooms.

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *Competitive Strategy* | Michael Porter | The five forces, and the book that made strategy an analytical field |
| *Competitive Advantage* | Michael Porter | The value chain — the follow-up, and the more useful of the two in practice |
| *Economics of Strategy* | Besanko, Dranove, Shanley & Schaefer | The rigorous textbook. Strategy derived from microeconomics rather than asserted |
| *Good Strategy / Bad Strategy* | Richard Rumelt | The corrective. What a strategy is, and why most documents called one aren't |
| *The Innovator's Dilemma* | Clayton Christensen | Why good management kills good companies |

## Organizational behaviour

| Book | Author | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| *Organizational Behavior* | OpenStax | **Free**, open-licensed, and a complete course text. Start here — it costs nothing and covers the syllabus |
| *Organizational Behavior* | Robbins & Judge | The commercial standard if you want the fuller treatment |
| *Organizational Culture and Leadership* | Edgar Schein | Culture as something with a structure that can be examined, not a vibe |
| *A Primer on Decision Making* | James March | How organisations actually decide, from the person who spent a career finding out that it isn't rationally |
| *Organizations* | March & Simon | The foundational text. Bounded rationality applied to institutions |

*Principles of Management* (OpenStax) is **free** and covers the general
management survey the catalogue also lacks.

# Writing and studio work

These two share a problem: what's missing isn't information, it's critique.
A book can carry the curriculum but not the response. Say that plainly and
plan for human feedback separately.

## Advanced writing

- *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* — Joseph Williams. Already read;
  noted here because it is the spine of this list and everything else is
  built against it
- *Clear and Simple as the Truth* — Francis-Noël Thomas & Mark Turner.
  Prose as a set of stances toward reader and subject. The most
  intellectually serious book on writing
- *Several Short Sentences About Writing* — Verlyn Klinkenborg
- *Draft No. 4* — John McPhee. Structure, from the person who thinks about
  it hardest
- *Steering the Craft* — Ursula K. Le Guin, for narrative specifically

## Drawing and studio practice

Three of these are structured curricula with assignments, which makes them
closer to courses than most things in this file.

- *The Natural Way to Draw* — Kimon Nicolaïdes. A scheduled curriculum with
  exercises and a stated number of hours. Follow it as written
- *Charles Bargue Drawing Course* — the plates are widely available free
  and are the atelier tradition's actual sequence
- *The Practice and Science of Drawing* — Harold Speed (**free**, public
  domain)
- *Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain* — Betty Edwards, for absolute
  beginners, and effective for the narrow thing it does
- *Color and Light* — James Gurney, once you can draw
- *Classical Drawing Atelier* — Juliette Aristides, for the full
  academic method

**Critique still has to come from people.** Local community college studio
courses, life-drawing sessions, and atelier open workshops are the
substitute, and they are in the catalogue.

## What no book fixes

Stated so the file doesn't overclaim. Textbooks substitute for missing
courses. They do not substitute for:

- **Wet lab work.** Protocol books exist — *Molecular Cloning* is the
  canonical one — and reading a protocol is not running it. Community bio
  labs and community college lab courses remain the answer
- **Cadaveric dissection.** Rohen's photographic atlas is the closest a
  book gets, and it isn't close
- **Clinical hours.** Licensure-gated by statute. No reading changes this
- **Studio critique.** See above
- **Supervised feedback on writing**

Those five stay in the gap list, and always will.
