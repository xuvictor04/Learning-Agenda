# Kit — Equipment & Materials

Reading about a field and touching it are different kinds of knowing. You can
hold an accurate account of stellar magnitudes and still not know what
Andromeda looks like in cheap binoculars; you can describe a dovetail and be
unable to cut one. Equipment converts a passive domain into a practice domain,
and practice is what this repo rewards.

So buying is encouraged, with one failure mode in view: the telescope in the
closet with the dew cap still bagged, the pristine chisel set still wearing its
factory grind. Gear bought as a *substitute* for practice is the commonest way
to spend money on learning and get nothing, because buying feels like starting
and it isn't.

---

## The constraint is no longer money

This file used to be written for someone counting dollars. It isn't any more.
The working assumption now is **at least $3,000 a year for equipment, and
$3,000 is a deliberate lowball** — a floor, not a cap. Discretionary income is
higher than that and rises over time. Take the floor as the number below which
the plan is being under-resourced, not the number to squeeze under.

**Books, courses, tuition and other direct learning materials are budgeted
separately and do not come out of this line.** Do not talk yourself out of a
textbook because you bought a microscope. Those are different accounts, and
the reading account is the one that should never be rationed.

The one deliberate exception is the next section. Instruction, access and
travel *do* sit inside this budget, even though they look like learning
materials, because putting them here forces the comparison that matters: this
tutor or this telescope, this field school or this lathe. That comparison is
the whole point of the file, and it disappears if they're on separate lines.

And the agenda is now seventy years long — years 1 to 70, ages 20 to 90. At
$3,000 a year that is a **lifetime capital allocation of well over $200,000**,
before any increase. That reframes everything. This is not a starter-kit
document. It is a plan for equipping seven decades of practice, in which most
of the durable goods you will ever own get bought once, used for thirty or
forty years, maintained, and eventually handed on.

### What actually binds

When money was the constraint, the discipline was *can I afford it*, and the
failure mode was overspending. At this budget that discipline does nothing —
you can afford almost everything in this file, in the same year if you want
to. **The binding constraint is attention.** There are only so many hours, and
every object you own claims some of them: to learn, to set up, to maintain, to
store, to feel guilty about.

So the question is no longer *can I afford this*. It is **will this actually
get used** — and the failure mode shifts from overspending to *accumulating
capability you never exercise*. A shelf of unused good equipment is worse than
a shelf of unused cheap equipment. It cost more, yes, but the real damage is
that it bought a false sense of progress: the room looks like a laboratory,
the person in it has learned nothing, and the evidence of competence sitting
in plain sight makes it harder to notice.

### The rules

**Anything you buy must have a scheduled first use.** A date, in the calendar,
before the order is placed. If you cannot find two hours in the next fortnight
for the first session with the thing, don't order it yet — you have just
discovered that you want the idea of the practice rather than the practice.
This single rule will kill more bad purchases than any price limit, and at
this budget it is the *only* filter with teeth left.

**Buy the cheap version first — where you don't yet know your requirements.**
The original rule stands and is still usually right: use the cheap one until
its limits genuinely annoy you, on a job you were trying to finish, and let
that annoyance tell you which of the expensive version's features you actually
need. But it is not universal, and pretending it is has costs. **Where the
cheap version teaches bad habits or simply doesn't work, buy the good one
immediately.** In this file that means: temperature-controlled soldering
irons, chef's knives and the means to sharpen them, sharpening equipment
generally, telescope mounts, microphones, and paper and pigment in the visual
arts. Each is flagged where it appears. The test is whether the cheap version
fails *informatively* — a cheap plane teaches you what a good plane does; a
cheap soldering iron teaches you that you're bad at soldering, which is a lie.

**Buy the thing used weekly before the thing used once.** A $20 kitchen scale
beats a $400 telescope, and that holds even though the telescope is more
interesting — and it holds harder at a high budget, because a high budget is
exactly what tempts you into the once-a-year object.

**Borrow before you buy.** You can use expensive equipment without owning it:
libraries with a "library of things", makerspaces, tool libraries, community
college shop and lab courses, university extension programs, astronomy clubs
with loaner gear. Money makes this *more* attractive, not less, because
membership fees stop mattering and what you are really buying is a month of
finding out whether you want the object at all.

**Prices below are approximate USD ranges and they drift.** Orders of
magnitude, not quotes — said once so it isn't hedged on every line.

## Spend on access and people before objects

This is the most important section in the file, and at a real budget it is the
one that changes outcomes most.

At $3,000 a year, the highest-return purchases are usually not equipment. They
are **feedback and access** — the two things principle 1 says skill actually
requires and the two things a shelf of tools cannot provide. A telescope will
show you Saturn. It will not tell you that your observing notes are useless,
that you're star-hopping inefficiently, or that the thing you think is seeing
is actually collimation. Only a person does that, and people are purchasable.

Compare honestly. **$2,000 of objects** buys a very good telescope, or a
research-grade microscope, or a full hand-tool kit. **$2,000 of access** buys a
weekly language tutor for eight months, or a two-week archaeological field
school, or a semester of community college ceramics plus a year of makerspace
membership. In almost every year of a seventy-year plan, the second column
teaches more — because it comes with correction, deadlines, and other people
who will notice if you don't show up. Objects have none of those properties.

### Tutors and coaches

**$25–80/hour** typical, **$60–200/hour** for genuinely expert instruction;
online language tutoring runs **$10–30/hour** and is the best value in this
entire document. A weekly hour is **$1,300–4,000/yr**.

This is the only mode in the whole repo with real-time feedback — the
tightest possible loop, the thing principle 1 is built around. Where it earns
its cost:

- **A language tutor**, weekly, from month one. Nothing else in language
  acquisition comes close; see `languages.md`. Do not self-study a language
  for two years and then hire someone to fix your accent.
- **An instrument teacher**, weekly or fortnightly. Self-taught technique
  compounds errors that take years to unpick, and the guitar and piano are
  both instruments where a beginner cannot hear their own faults.
- **A maths tutor for a hard stretch** — real analysis, measure theory, the
  first serious proof course. Not permanently: hire one for the eight weeks
  where you're stuck, which is where self-study actually fails.
- **A coach for anything physical** — a lifting coach, a swim coach. Principle
  8 makes the substrate part of the plan; form errors under load are exactly
  the kind of thing you cannot self-diagnose.

Buy blocks, not open-ended arrangements. Six sessions, a specific target,
then decide.

### A paid editor

**$300–1,500 per pass** depending on length and depth; substantive editing
runs higher than copy-editing. Once or twice a year, on a real piece.

Writing is a fundamental in this repo, and it is the fundamental where the
feedback loop is worst: nobody in your life will tell you your prose is
flabby. An editor will, with line-level evidence, and the effect carries into
every artifact you write afterwards. One good developmental edit teaches more
about writing than a shelf of books on writing, and this is the single most
underrated line item here.

### Live courses with feedback

- **Community college lab and shop courses** — **$150–600 a semester**.
  Chemistry, welding, ceramics, machining, electronics. Supervised access to
  equipment you shouldn't buy, plus an instructor who corrects errors you
  cannot see. The best value per dollar in the file after tutoring.
- **University extension and continuing education** — **$300–1,500 a course**.
- **Summer schools and institutes** — **$1,000–4,000** for one to three
  weeks, plus travel. Mathematics, linguistics, philosophy, writing.
- **Field schools** — **$1,500–5,000** for two to six weeks: archaeology,
  ecology, marine biology, geology field camps. These are transformative and
  chronically overlooked by self-directed learners because they feel like
  something students do. They are open to adults, they teach method rather
  than content, and three weeks of excavation or transect survey does what no
  amount of reading does.
- **Language immersion programs** — **$800–3,000** for two to four weeks
  including homestay. The step change in `languages.md` that self-study
  cannot produce.
- **Artisan and craft workshops** — **$200–1,500** for a weekend to a week.
  Blacksmithing, timber framing, letterpress, bookbinding, boatbuilding.
  Concentrated hands-on instruction with tools you'd otherwise buy and
  misuse.

### Memberships and access

- **Museum memberships** — **$50–150/yr**, and the argument is repetition:
  twenty short visits to look at four things is how you learn to see.
- **Community bio labs** — **$50–150/month** for benches, autoclaves, PCR,
  and the supervision that makes them safe.
- **Makerspaces and hackerspaces** — **$30–150/month** for laser cutters, CNC,
  welding, and more valuably the people who will show you how to use them.
- **Tool libraries** and library "libraries of things" — **free to $50/yr**.
- **Botanical gardens and arboretums** — **$40–100/yr**, and the good ones run
  courses in plant identification, propagation and taxonomy.
- **Professional society memberships** — **$50–300/yr**, often far less for
  associate or amateur grades. Astronomical, geological, ornithological,
  mycological, historical, mathematical societies all take non-professionals.
  What you're buying is journals, meetings, field trips, and a room full of
  people who know more than you.
- **An alumni library account** — **$50–200/yr** and worth every dollar for
  journal access. This is what alumni associations are actually for.

### Conferences and seminars

**$200–1,500** registration, plus travel. Amateur and regional meetings are
cheaper and often better for a learner than flagship conferences.

Go as a non-academic. Nobody checks. Amateur astronomy conventions, regional
history conferences, mycological forays, linguistics summer meetings, open
sessions of professional societies — you will understand a third of it in year
one, which is the correct proportion, and you will calibrate what the frontier
of a field actually sounds like, which no textbook conveys.

### Travel as field equipment

**$1,500–6,000** for a substantial trip; a great deal less if you go slowly
and stay put.

Travel is field equipment for geography, anthropology, art, architecture and
languages, and should be budgeted as equipment rather than leisure. The
variable is not distance but duration: three weeks in one place, navigating
badly in a language you're learning, teaches what three countries in three
weeks does not. Attach it to something — a field school, a language program,
a specific set of buildings, a specific archive — and it converts from
tourism into study.

## Universal — cross-domain

**A library card.** Free, and the highest-leverage item in this file by an
enormous margin: books, interlibrary loan reaching almost anything, and the
part most people never discover — remote access to JSTOR, newspaper archives,
often O'Reilly. If you buy nothing else here, get this today.

**A notebook system.** $20–40/yr. Not one notebook — a system: a carry notebook
for capture, a bound one for working problems, one place they get processed.
The paper doesn't matter; capture within reach does.

**Index cards.** $10 for hundreds. Cards you wrote beat decks you downloaded,
and physical cards reorder on a table in a way a document resists.

**A whiteboard.** $25–80. Thinking with your arm instead of your wrist changes
what you can hold in view. A2 or larger, or wall paint.

**A reading stand**, $20–40, which holds a book open at eye height so you can
write while reading — the difference between reading and studying.
**Noise-cancelling headphones**, $100–350, only if your environment is genuinely
noisy. **An e-reader**, $100–200, worth it for volume reading and public-domain
canon, useless for diagrams and equations. **A duplex laser printer/scanner**,
$150–300, justified by annotation; inkjets are a false economy past occasional
use.

**When budget isn't the constraint.** Put the money into the desk, the chair
and the light, in that order — **$400–1,200 for a good task chair, $300–800
for a sit/stand desk, $150–400 for proper task lighting**. This is the least
glamorous upgrade in the file and the one with the largest total effect,
because it is applied to every hour of every domain for seventy years, and
principle 8 is not decoration. A **second large monitor** ($200–500) is worth
it for anything involving code, data or two documents at once. A **whiteboard
wall** rather than a whiteboard is worth it and costs almost nothing. What is
*not* worth it: headphones above about $350 (the returns are audiophile, not
cognitive), premium notebooks, and any note-taking software that charges
monthly. The pen does not matter. It has never mattered.

## Formal

Almost free, and honestly so. Paper, pencil, patience.

**A proof notebook.** $10, hardbound, kept separate — the record of your own
failed attempts is the most useful document you will own here.

**A calculator.** Skip it. A graphing calculator is a $120 answer to a question
Python answers better and free, unless an exam mandates one.

The real equipment is software, all free: **LaTeX**, which forces precision;
**Anki**, the spaced-repetition infrastructure principle 2 asks for; a computer
algebra system — **SageMath** or SymPy — for checking and exploring; and
**Lean** with mathlib for formalization, the most brutally honest feedback loop
in mathematics.

**When budget isn't the constraint.** Mathematics is the cluster where money
buys nothing, and it is worth sitting with that fact for a moment because it
is the clearest evidence for this file's central claim. There is no $5,000
version of understanding measure theory. The only purchases that help are a
**tutor for the weeks you are stuck** ($40–100/hour, and transformative at
exactly the right moment) and, marginally, **compute** — a machine that runs
your simulations without a coffee break, or rented cloud time at $50–300 for a
project, if you get into serious numerical work. An **e-ink tablet** ($400–700)
is pleasant for problem sets and changes nothing. Buy the tutor.

## Physical

**Binoculars before a telescope. 10x50, $90–180.** The most useful advice in
this file. A beginner with 10x50s sees more, more often, than a beginner with a
$200 department-store telescope, because the cheap scope is optically poor,
shakes on its tripod, and is hard to aim — so it stops being used. Binoculars
are grab-and-go and show the Moon's terminator, Jupiter's moons and Andromeda.

**Then a Dobsonian reflector, 6–8 inch, $350–700.** The standard first real
telescope for forty years, because it spends your money on the only thing that
matters: aperture. Aperture is light-gathering area, and it sets how faint an
object you can see and how much detail resolves. Magnification is merely a
choice of eyepiece — any scope can be pushed to 600x, and a bad one will show
you a large blurry nothing, so **any advertisement leading with magnification
is aimed at people who don't know this.** A Dobsonian is a tube on a rocker
mount: nothing to fail, all the budget in the mirror.

**The mount is the exception to buying cheap.** A cheap tripod or a wobbly
equatorial head makes a good telescope unusable — every touch sets off a
ten-second shake, focusing becomes a fight, and the instrument goes in the
closet. If you buy a mount at all, buy a rigid one. The Dobsonian is
recommended partly because its mount problem is already solved.

Never point an optical instrument at the Sun without a proper full-aperture
solar filter over the front. Eyepiece-mounted "sun filters" — still sold — can
crack from concentrated heat while your eye is at the lens. This is the one
irreversible injury in this file.

**Star charts.** A planisphere is $12 and teaches the sky's rotation in a way
an app cannot, because you turn it yourself. **Stellarium** is free.

**Weather instruments.** Thermometer and aneroid barometer, $40–90; a home
station with anemometer and rain gauge, $120–300. Pressure falling ahead of a
front stops being a textbook sentence and becomes something you watched happen.
Log the readings — the log is the point.

**A rock and mineral hardness kit.** $25–50: Mohs picks, a streak plate, a
magnet, dilute hydrochloric acid for the carbonate fizz test. Geology is a
field identification discipline first, and identification is a procedure you
perform with your hands.

**A spectroscope**, $15–40 handheld — point it at a sodium street lamp and
quantized energy levels stop being an assertion. **A Geiger counter**, $120–350,
optional and memorable: background radiation turns out to be constant, audible,
and different in your basement than on an aeroplane. Safe test sources are
uranium glass and thoriated welding electrodes.

**When budget isn't the constraint.** Astronomy is one of the few domains
where serious money genuinely buys capability, so be precise about which
money.

- **Premium eyepieces**, $150–600 each. The most reliably worthwhile upgrade
  in amateur astronomy and the one beginners skip. Wide apparent field and
  long eye relief change what an evening feels like, and they carry across
  every telescope you will own for the next fifty years. Buy two or three,
  not a set.
- **A tracking or GoTo mount**, $800–2,500. Worth it — an object that stays
  in the field while you study it is a different activity from one you nudge
  every twenty seconds, and it makes showing the sky to other people possible.
- **An apochromatic refractor**, $1,500–4,000. Beautiful, and honestly not the
  upgrade most people think. For visual deep-sky work a large Dobsonian at the
  same price shows dramatically more, because aperture still wins. The apo
  earns its cost for planetary detail, for wide-field imaging, and for being
  portable enough that you actually take it out — which is a real argument,
  not a small one.
- **A hydrogen-alpha solar telescope**, $700–2,500. Worth it and
  underappreciated: prominences and filaments in daylight, no dark sky
  required, usable from a city on a lunch break. If light pollution is
  limiting you, this is the purchase.
- **Astrophotography**, $2,000–10,000 for camera, guiding, filters and
  software. Be warned that this is not an upgrade to astronomy; it is a
  different hobby, mostly conducted indoors, and it competes with observing
  rather than deepening it. Choose deliberately.
- **Larger aperture**, $1,200–3,000 for a 12–16 inch Dobsonian. Worth it only
  if it fits in your car and your back, tested honestly. A 16-inch that stays
  in the garage is worse than an 8-inch in the boot.

For geology, a **petrographic microscope with polarizing stage** ($600–2,500
used) turns rock identification into mineralogy and is genuinely worth it if
earth science reaches T2. A **research-grade weather station** ($600–1,500)
mostly buys precision you have no use for — the cheap one already taught you
the physics.

## Living

**A real microscope.** The gap between a toy and an instrument is enormous and
easy to see once you know the words. A toy has plastic optics, a fixed stage,
and "1000x" on the box. A usable **compound microscope** has *achromatic
objectives* (4x, 10x, 40x, usually a 100x oil-immersion), a mechanical stage
with coaxial controls, an Abbe condenser with iris, and glass throughout. New,
the usable entry point is **$200–400**; below that you are buying a toy
whatever the listing claims. Better: buy used. **University surplus sales and
lab liquidations** put professional scopes on the market at $150–400, and a
thirty-year-old professional instrument beats anything new at that price.

**Slides.** A prepared set ($30–60) calibrates your eye. Making your own is the
skill: blanks and coverslips ($20 per hundred), methylene blue and iodine
($15). Pond water on day one.

**A dissection kit**, $20–40, because anatomy is spatial knowledge and diagrams
flatten it. **A 10x doublet or triplet loupe**, $15–40, non-negotiable for
botany, since plant keys ask about hairs and floral parts you cannot otherwise
see. **A plant press**, $40–80 bought or $20 built from plywood, straps and
newspaper.

**Field guides for your actual region.** $20–30 each, and regional beats
comprehensive — a guide covering your country is thinner, so you actually key
things out. Peterson Field Guides and the Audubon Society guides are the
standard American series; Sibley for North American birds; Collins for Britain
and Europe. Buy the one whose range map includes your house.

**Binoculars for birding** — the same pair as astronomy, though 8x42 is the
birder's preference for the wider, steadier field.

**A fermentation setup.** $30–60: wide-mouth jars, airlock lids, a scale, pH
strips, salt. Applied microbial ecology at kitchen scale, failing
informatively.

**Soil test kits.** $15–30 home; better, send a sample to your agricultural
extension service for $15–30 and get a real lab report. One lab result is worth
ten home kits.

**On home chemistry, be clear-eyed.** Reagents, glassware and apparatus are
regulated differently by jurisdiction, sometimes strictly, and the hazards —
fumes, thermal runaway, incompatible mixtures — are not learned by improvising.
The legitimate routes are better anyway: a proper hobby chemistry set from an
established educational supplier, a community college lab course, or for the
biology side a **community bio lab** with benches, autoclaves and PCR at
$50–150/month. Take the course; it costs less than the glassware.

**When budget isn't the constraint.**

- **A used research-grade compound microscope with phase contrast**,
  $800–2,500. Worth it, and the specific feature to buy is **phase contrast**,
  not more magnification — living unstained cells become visible, which is the
  difference between looking at prepared slides and looking at biology. DIC
  optics cost several times more and are not worth it outside real research.
- **A stereo (dissecting) microscope**, $200–800, and this is probably the
  better *first* upgrade rather than the second compound scope. Insects,
  flowers, fungi, circuit boards, mineral grains — the working magnification
  for most of what you'll actually pick up outdoors.
- **A trinocular head and camera adapter**, $150–500. Worth it, because
  photographs are how microscopy becomes a record instead of an evening.
- **Quality field optics**, $800–2,500 for premium binoculars. The gain over a
  $150 pair is real — edge sharpness, low-light performance, and the fact that
  your eyes aren't tired after two hours. But be honest: a **$300–500** pair
  captures most of that, and the last thousand dollars is refinement. A
  **spotting scope** ($500–2,000) is the bigger capability jump for shorebirds,
  raptors and anything at distance.
- **A serious herbarium setup, an incubator, a centrifuge, a PCR kit** —
  don't. Use the community bio lab. This is the clearest case in the file where
  membership beats ownership, because the equipment needs maintenance,
  consumables and a room, and the lab already has all three.

## Mind

Mostly free, and the equipment that isn't free is mostly disappointing.

**EEG hobbyist kits.** $200–1,000. Real, and genuinely limited: few channels,
dry electrodes, poor signal-to-noise, enormous artifacts from blinking. You can
watch alpha rhythm appear when you close your eyes, which is a legitimate
thrill. You cannot do neuroscience with it.

**Browser experiments.** Free. **PsychoPy** and **jsPsych** run real
reaction-time, Stroop and psychophysics tasks on yourself and anyone you can
recruit. Watching your own data come out noisy teaches more about the field's
methods problem than any chapter about it.

The real equipment for psychology is **access to participants** and **R**,
which is free and is the environment the field reasons in.

**When budget isn't the constraint.** Spending more here buys almost nothing,
and this is the honest answer rather than a hedge. A $3,000 research-grade EEG
does not fix the problem, because the problem is that you have no lab, no
participants, no ethics approval and no comparison condition. What *does*
scale: **paid participants** ($10–20 each, or $100–500 for a small online
sample through a recruitment platform), which turns a demo into a study, and a
**statistics tutor** for the eight weeks where your analysis is wrong in a way
you cannot see. For linguistics, a **good microphone** is the one object worth
buying properly — see the note under Meaning & expression, and buy the good one
first, because cheap microphones make recordings you cannot analyse.

## Human & social

**An archive reader's card.** Usually free; ID, proof of address, a short
registration. The first time you handle a document nobody has requested in
eleven years, history stops being a genre.

**A scanner, or a phone scanning app.** Free to $200. The phone app is fine;
the discipline that matters is recording the citation in the same moment, not
the resolution.

**Subscriptions worth real money.** One serious newspaper of record,
$150–400/yr — read daily it is a slow course in politics, economics and
geography at once. **JSTOR** you should not pay for: get it through your
library card or an alumni association, which is what alumni associations are
actually for.

**Maps and atlases.** Topographic sheets from your national mapping agency
(USGS quadrangles, Ordnance Survey Explorer), $10–20 each, for places you walk.
For the desk, the *Times Comprehensive Atlas of the World* ($150–250) is the
reference standard and the *National Geographic Atlas of the World* ($100–170)
the cheaper alternative. A wall map you see daily beats a better one you shelve.

**Travel is the field equipment** for geography and anthropology, and should be
budgeted as equipment rather than leisure. The variable is not distance but
duration: three weeks in one place, navigating badly in a language you're
learning, teaches what three countries in three weeks does not.

**When budget isn't the constraint.** The money goes to **archive travel** —
$1,000–4,000 for a week or two working in a collection that exists in one
building — and that is a real capability purchase, not a holiday. An **overhead
book/document scanner** ($400–900) is worth it once you're doing archival work
in volume, because it changes how much you can capture in a day, which is the
actual limiting factor in a reading room. **Paid research database access**
($200–800/yr for genealogical, newspaper or legal databases) is worth it for a
specific project and a waste on subscription autopilot. **Buying rare books and
antique maps** is collecting, not learning; it is a fine thing to do with money
and it should be honestly accounted as such rather than smuggled into the
education budget. Ownership of a first edition teaches nothing that the reprint
doesn't.

## Meaning & expression

**A real instrument, bought used.** Instruments hold value and improve with age
when wooden. A decent **acoustic guitar** with a solid top is $150–400 used,
$250–450 new; below that the action is usually so high that beginners quit and
blame their fingers. A **weighted-key digital piano**, 88 keys with hammer
action, is $500–900 new and $300–500 used — weighted is the specification that
matters, because unweighted keys build a touch you must later unlearn. Spend
$50–100 on a setup or tuning either way; a badly set-up instrument is exactly
the tool-maintenance failure `made-applied.md` warns about.

**A metronome**, $20–30 mechanical or free as an app — the mechanical one gets
used because it is sitting there. **Sheet music** is free at IMSLP, which holds
essentially the whole public-domain repertoire.

**A microphone, bought properly the first time.** $100–250 for a decent
large-diaphragm condenser or dynamic plus an audio interface, and this is one
of the buy-good-immediately categories. A $25 microphone doesn't produce a
worse recording of your playing or your speech; it produces a recording in
which you cannot hear the thing you were trying to evaluate, which defeats the
entire purpose of recording yourself. Recording your own practice is one of the
highest-feedback tools available to a self-taught musician or speaker.

**A drawing kit.** $40–70: graphite HB through 6B, vine and compressed
charcoal, a kneaded eraser, a blending stump. Then the part people get wrong —
**cheap paper ruins the experience.** Copier paper buckles, won't take an
eraser, and makes competent work look bad, which is demoralizing at precisely
the wrong moment. A sketchbook at 90–150 gsm is $15–25.

**Watercolours.** $60–120: a small **artist-grade** pan set (student grade is
the false economy — weak, muddy pigments), three brushes, and **100% cotton
paper at 300 gsm**, which does more than anything else to determine whether the
result looks like a painting.

**Printmaking.** $50–100 for linocut: blocks, gouges, a brayer, water-based
ink, a baren. No press — a wooden spoon works. Cutting in reverse and in flat
value changes how you see every image afterwards.

**A museum membership.** $50–150/yr, and the argument is repetition: twenty
short visits to look at four things is how you learn to see, rather than one
exhausting four-hour march.

**For film study**, a projector ($400–900) or a good large screen, plus
physical media. Streaming crops, recompresses and alters colour; if you are
studying film rather than watching it, the disc is the primary source.

**When budget isn't the constraint.**

- **A real acoustic piano.** $2,000–8,000 for a good used upright, more for a
  grand, plus $150–250 twice a year for tuning and a room that can take the
  weight and humidity. Worth it, with a genuine caveat: a good weighted digital
  is entirely adequate for learning the notes, and the upgrade buys touch,
  resonance, and — the real argument — the fact that an acoustic piano in the
  room gets played on the way past, while a digital one under a cover does not.
  If you will not tune it, don't buy it.
- **A better guitar or a step-up violin**, $1,500–5,000. Worth it *after* your
  technique is good enough to hear the difference, which is usually year three
  or four, not year one. Buying it early is the classic substitution error.
  These instruments hold value and are effectively lifetime purchases.
- **A printmaking press**, $600–2,500 for a small etching or relief press.
  Worth it only if linocut with a spoon has already become a regular practice.
  Otherwise use a studio's press.
- **A kiln**, $1,000–3,500 plus dedicated electrical work and ventilation, and
  a place to put it that isn't your living room. Be very slow here: **studio
  membership at $100–200/month** covers firing, glazes, wheels and other
  potters, and most people who buy a kiln find they bought a firing schedule
  they don't have time to keep. Buy the kiln when the studio's schedule is the
  thing annoying you.
- **Artist-grade materials across the board.** Worth it and cheap in absolute
  terms — this is a consumables line, not a capital line, and it belongs there.
- **Film study**: a proper 4K projector and screen at $2,000–5,000. Honestly
  marginal above a good large panel, except for aspect ratio and scale, which
  do matter for some films and not for the study of most.

## Made & applied

The biggest section, because this is where equipment is least optional.

**A temperature-controlled soldering station.** $50–120. The $12 fixed-temp
pencil is the standard false economy: too cold for ground planes, too hot for
small parts, its tip oxidizing into uselessness within a month, producing cold
joints you will misdiagnose as circuit bugs for hours. Buy temperature control,
a spare tip, a brass sponge. Solder fumes are flux, not lead — still, ventilate
and fan the smoke away from your face.

**A multimeter.** $40–80 — the instrument that turns "it doesn't work" into a
location. If you will ever measure mains, buy a genuine CAT III rating with
fused current inputs; a $10 meter is fine below that and dangerous above it.

**An oscilloscope.** A cheap USB scope at $60–150 is entirely fine to start and
shows what a multimeter cannot: what the signal *does* over time. Move to a
benchtop 2-channel 100 MHz unit ($350–500) only when the USB one's noise or
sample rate genuinely obstructs you, which for most people is never.

**Breadboards and a components assortment**, $50–80 — resistor and capacitor
kits, LEDs, transistors, a few op-amps and 555s. **A microcontroller starter
kit**, $30–70 for an Arduino-class board or $50–90 for a Raspberry Pi-class
board. Together the cheapest bridge in this document between computing and
physical reality, and working through Platt's *Make: Electronics* with them on
the desk is the fastest route into the domain that exists.

**Hand tools for woodworking — few and good.** A small set of good hand tools
beats a large set of bad power tools: hand tools are quiet enough for an
apartment, safe enough to learn on unsupervised, and give continuous feedback
instead of removing material faster than you can think. Start with a bench
plane (No. 4 or No. 5), four bevel-edge chisels, a backsaw, a marking gauge, a
square and a mallet — $300–600 new at decent quality, or $100–250 buying
vintage and restoring it, which is itself an education.

**A sharpening setup, bought with the chisels — and buy the good one.**
$80–200 for diamond plates or waterstones plus a honing guide. This is a
buy-good-immediately category: cheap stones dish, cut slowly, and leave you
unable to tell whether the problem is the edge or your technique. Sharpening is
the actual first skill — a dull edge tears rather than cuts, needs force,
slips, and convinces beginners they lack talent. Learn it before attempting a
joint.

**A workbench and vise.** Build the bench; buy the vise, $100–250. Work that
moves while you cut it cannot be cut accurately, and nearly every beginner is
fighting workholding without knowing it.

**Safety equipment, stated once and not negotiable:** ANSI-rated eye protection
($10–25), hearing protection at NRR 25+ for anything with a motor ($20–40), a
fitted respirator plus real dust extraction ($40–100 — fine wood dust is a
long-term respiratory hazard, not a nuisance), and an ABC fire extinguisher
within reach of the bench ($30–60). Buy these before the tools that need them.

**A 3D printer.** $200–400, and the honest note: worth it if you already have
parts you want and can model them, and an expensive time-consuming hobby *about
the printer* if you don't. Use a print service until a specific part is the
reason.

**A sewing machine.** $80–200 used mechanical, $150–300 new. Buy mechanical,
not cheap computerized; a solid old all-metal machine will outlive you.

**Kitchen equipment for cooking as a craft.** The **scale is the single
highest-value purchase in this file relative to cost — $15–25** — because it
converts cooking from imitation into a repeatable measured process you can
debug. Then a real 8-inch **chef's knife** ($40–120) *and* a way to sharpen it
($30–50 whetstone), a **cast iron pan** ($25–40, effectively permanent), and an
**instant-read thermometer** ($20–100), which is what finally makes bread and
meat come out the same way twice. The knife is a buy-good-immediately item: a
cheap knife will not hold an edge, so it teaches you to saw and to press
instead of to cut, and those habits transfer to every knife you touch
afterwards.

**A camera.** $300–600 for a used mirrorless or DSLR body and one prime lens.
The argument for a prime over a zoom is pedagogical: a fixed focal length
forces you to move, and moving is composition. Your phone is good enough that
you should buy this only if photography is the point rather than the record.

**When budget isn't the constraint.** This cluster has the most real upgrades
in the file and also the most expensive mistakes.

- **A benchtop oscilloscope**, $400–1,200 for a 2- or 4-channel 100–200 MHz
  unit with decoding. Worth it once electronics is a standing practice, and
  the honest reason is not specification but friction: a scope sitting on the
  bench with knobs gets used, a USB scope that needs a laptop and a driver
  does not. Add a **bench power supply** ($100–250) and a **hot-air rework
  station** ($100–300) if surface-mount work starts.
- **A full hand-tool kit and a proper bench**, $2,000–5,000 all in — a bench
  built to your height with a face and tail vise, a plane for each job, a full
  chisel set, a set of saws, and a cabinet to keep them in. Worth it, and the
  cost-per-year arithmetic is absurd in its favour: these are fifty-year tools
  that improve with use, and vintage examples are often better than new.
- **A lathe.** Wood, $800–3,000; metal, $2,000–8,000 plus tooling that costs
  as much again. The wood lathe is the single most immediately rewarding
  machine in a home shop — a finished object in an hour. The metal lathe is a
  different commitment entirely, is the machine that lets you make machines,
  and should be preceded by a community college machining course rather than
  followed by one.
- **A bandsaw, a jointer and a planer**, $600–2,500 each. Worth it only when
  dimensioning stock by hand has become the thing standing between you and the
  work you want to do. That is a real point, and it arrives later than people
  expect.
- **Laser cutters and CNC routers**, $2,000–8,000. Usually *not* worth
  owning — this is what makerspaces are for, they need ventilation and
  maintenance and space, and the software learning curve is the actual
  content.
- **A resin or large-format 3D printer**, $500–2,000. Marginal. The cheap
  filament printer is now genuinely good, and the constraint on your printing
  is your CAD skill, which costs nothing to improve.
- **A serious kitchen**: induction, a good oven, a stand mixer ($300–500), a
  sous-vide circulator ($100–200). Real but modest gains. Above a good knife,
  a heavy pan and a scale, most kitchen spending is decoration — the
  exceptions are the heat source and, if you bake, a **proper baking steel and
  a scale you trust**. A **$1,000 knife** is jewellery.
- **Camera upgrades**, $1,500–4,000 for a full-frame body and fast primes.
  Honestly: this rarely makes anyone's photographs better, and a workshop or a
  critique group at $300–1,000 will. Buy the lens only when a specific
  photograph you tried to take failed for a reason the lens fixes.

## Consumables and maintenance

The recurring cost nobody budgets for, and the one that most reliably predicts
whether a tool is real. Expect **$300–800/yr at the floor budget**, more once
several domains are active.

- **Sharpening**: stone flatteners, lapping plate, honing compound, strops,
  replacement diamond plates — $50–150/yr for anyone using edge tools.
- **Electronics**: solder, flux, tips, desoldering braid, protoboard, and the
  endless small components you're always missing — $50–200/yr.
- **3D printing**: filament or resin, nozzles, build plates, belts —
  $100–300/yr if the printer is actually running.
- **Ceramics**: clay, glazes, firing fees — $200–600/yr, and firing is the
  hidden one.
- **Music**: strings ($50–150/yr), reeds ($100–400/yr for a woodwind and this
  surprises people), tuning twice a year ($300–500), bow rehair, humidity
  control.
- **Visual art**: paper is the real cost, then pigment, ink, blocks, canvas —
  $100–500/yr. Film photography adds film and processing at $15–25 a roll,
  which is a per-frame discipline as much as a cost.
- **Living**: slides, coverslips, stains, immersion oil, seeds, soil, potting
  mix, soil tests — $50–200/yr.
- **Everywhere**: batteries and rechargeables, replacement parts, blades and
  bits, calibration and servicing, and the cost of shipping things back.

**Consumables are the sign a tool is being used.** This is the most useful
diagnostic in the file, and it is nearly free to apply: at the annual review,
look at what you spent on consumables per tool. **A tool with no consumable
spend is a tool you are not using.** No solder means no soldering. No clay
means no pots. No paper means no drawing. Sell it, lend it, or schedule a use
for it — but do not let it sit there quietly certifying a competence you don't
have.

Maintenance is the other half. Tools that last forty years last forty years
*because* someone oils, tunes, sharpens, calibrates and stores them. Budget an
afternoon a quarter for it, and treat the maintenance session as practice
rather than overhead — it is, in most crafts, where a large fraction of the
real understanding lives.

## The budget

Three tiers, then a lifetime view. The tiers are annual equipment allocations;
books, tuition and direct learning materials sit on a separate line and are not
counted here.

### The floor — about $3,000/yr

This is a workable allocation, not an aspiration. A suggested split:

| Category | Share | Approx. |
|----------|-------|---------|
| Access and people — tutors, courses, memberships, workshops | ~45% | $1,300 |
| Domain equipment for the years currently active in `03-spine.md` | ~30% | $900 |
| Consumables and maintenance | ~13% | $400 |
| Opportunity reserve | ~13% | $400 |

Three things about this table. **Access is the largest line, deliberately** —
if your split has objects on top, you have reverted to buying things because
things are easier to choose. **Equipment is tied to the spine**, so you buy the
microscope in the year biology is actually scheduled, not the year you read
about microscopes; and equipment for a domain that hasn't reached T2 stays
small. **The reserve exists because opportunity is lumpy** — a field school
that appears in March, a used instrument at the right price, a lab liquidation
two towns over. Do not spend the reserve on schedule; let it roll over, and
accept that some years it goes unused and some years it goes on one thing.

Software — Anki, LaTeX, R, SageMath, Stellarium, PsychoPy, IMSLP — is free and
installs in week one. It is the cheapest half of this document and the half
most likely to still be in use in decade five.

### Comfortable — $5,000–8,000/yr

What actually gets added, in order of return:

- **More live instruction.** A weekly tutor rather than a block of six, in two
  domains rather than one. This is where most of the additional money should
  go and it is the least exciting answer.
- **One significant trip or program a year** — a field school, a language
  immersion month, a summer institute, an archive trip. $2,000–5,000, and it
  will be the thing you remember from that year.
- **Better versions of daily-use tools.** Note the qualifier: *daily-use*. The
  chair, the desk, the light, the knife, the bench, the eyepieces, the
  binoculars. Things touched weekly justify their upgrade; things touched
  annually do not, however much more interesting they are.
- **One durable big-ticket item every two or three years**, chosen from the
  "when budget isn't the constraint" lines above, and only for a domain that
  has already reached T2 with the literacy artifact to prove it.

### Unconstrained — above $10,000/yr

Be honest: **the returns flatten fast, and they flatten sooner than anyone
spending the money wants to believe.** What still scales:

- **Instruction** — more of it, and better. There is no ceiling here worth
  worrying about; genuinely expert teachers cost $150–300/hour and are worth
  it for a specific problem.
- **Access** — every membership, every lab, every studio, simultaneously.
- **Travel** — the one line where more money reliably buys more learning,
  because it buys duration, and duration is the variable that matters.
- **Time** — and this is the real luxury purchase. Paying for cleaning, food
  preparation, repairs and admin buys hours, and hours are the actual currency
  of this repo. Ten thousand dollars converted into two hundred hours is a
  better trade than ten thousand dollars converted into objects, every time.
- **A very small number of genuinely expensive instruments** — an acoustic
  piano, a large telescope with a tracking mount, a metal lathe, a research
  microscope. Perhaps four or five such purchases across seventy years.

Beyond that, more money mostly buys the illusion of progress. **The limiting
factor is hours.** You have roughly the same number of them as someone
spending $3,000, and no purchase converts money into practice — it only
converts money into the *option* of practice, which expires unused unless it
gets scheduled. If you find yourself with a budget and nothing you can name a
scheduled use for, the correct move is to spend nothing that quarter and put
the money aside. That is not frugality; it is the same discipline as the rest
of this file, applied at a level where nothing else enforces it.

### The lifetime view — seven decades

Years 1 to 70, ages 20 to 90. That changes the arithmetic on the expensive
things, and mostly in their favour.

**Judge big-ticket items on cost per year of use, not sticker price.** A
$2,000 telescope used for forty years is $50/yr — less than a streaming
subscription, for an instrument that will show you every planet, a few
thousand deep-sky objects, and several total lunar eclipses. A $4,000 piano
across fifty years is $80/yr and holds most of its value. A $3,000 hand-tool
kit and bench will outlive you and can be handed to someone who will use it. A
kiln, a lathe, a good microscope, a serious set of field optics — all durable
goods on a seventy-year horizon, all absurdly cheap when amortised, and all
worthless if the practice doesn't happen. The sticker price was never the
risk. The unused years are.

Three consequences worth planning around:

- **Buy the durable version once, at the right moment** — after the domain has
  proven itself, and while you are young enough to build the skill on it.
  Instruments and craft tools especially: technique acquired at 25 is still
  there at 75, and the tool will be too.
- **Maintain deliberately.** A forty-year tool requires forty years of
  sharpening, tuning, oiling and calibration. Budget it (see above) and treat
  it as part of the practice.
- **Expect the kit to change shape late.** Decades six and seven favour
  lighter binoculars over heavier ones, better light over more magnification,
  a smaller lathe over a bigger one, a chair that actually fits. Plan for the
  handover too: tools of this quality should go to someone who will use them,
  and deciding that in advance is part of owning them well.

### Before you buy anything: inventory what you have

You may already own a good deal of this. Spend an hour before the first
purchase — literally an hour, with a list — walking the house and writing down
what is already here: a camera, binoculars, a decent pan, a hand lens, tools
inherited from a relative, a keyboard in a cupboard, a microscope from school.
Then note what condition each is in, because a dull chisel and a rusty plane
are working tools thirty minutes away, not things to replace.

**The commonest error at a comfortable budget is buying a second thing that
does what the first thing already did.** A second pair of binoculars in a
different specification. Another multimeter. A better sketchbook while the
current one is a third full. This is how equipment budgets vanish without
capability changing, and it is invisible from the inside because each purchase
is individually defensible. The inventory is the only defence: keep the list,
update it at the annual review, and require any new purchase to name what it
does that nothing on the list already does.

### The tier gate still applies

Every purchase above a few hundred dollars should be attached to a domain the
spine has actually reached, and the largest ones to a domain **promoted to T2**
— the literacy artifact exists and an annual review has funded it. A benchtop
oscilloscope, a research microscope, a printmaking press, a kiln, a lathe, an
acoustic piano, a tracking mount: every one is justified for someone doing the
work and a closet ornament for someone who isn't. Money removes the financial
consequence of getting that wrong; it does not remove the consequence. The
tier system exists to tell you which you are.

## Borrow before you buy

- **Makerspaces and hackerspaces** — $30–150/month for laser cutters, CNC,
  welding and electronics benches, and more valuably the people who will show
  you how to use them.
- **Tool libraries and library "libraries of things"** — free to $50/yr. Check
  before buying anything with a motor.
- **University surplus sales and lab liquidations** — where professional
  microscopes, optics, meters and glassware go for a tenth of new.
- **Community college shop and lab courses** — a few hundred dollars buys a
  semester of supervised access to a machine shop, chemistry lab or ceramics
  studio, plus an instructor who corrects the error you cannot see.
- **Club loaner programs** — astronomy clubs in particular lend telescopes, so
  you can try a Dobsonian before spending $500 on one.
- **Used markets** — estate sales, auctions, the vintage hand-tool trade.
  Instruments, tools and optics are the three categories where used is not a
  compromise but the better purchase.

If something on this page can be borrowed for a month before it is bought,
borrow it for a month. Half the time you will find you wanted the practice and
not the object, and that is the cheapest lesson in the file — and at a budget
where you could simply have bought it, it is also the most necessary one.
