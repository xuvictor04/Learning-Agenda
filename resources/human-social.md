# Human & Social

Ten domains, each laid out at all three depth tiers from `../02-map.md`. T3 is
the default entry point — a survey, the field in its own voice, and a written
artifact. T2 and T1 are here so that promotion at an annual review means
picking up a real curriculum rather than inventing one. Hour figures are for
T3 only; T2 and T1 are measured in years, and the tier definitions in the map
already say how many.

A standing warning for this whole cluster: these are fields where a confident
bestseller can be twenty years out of date with the specialists. Where that's
true below, the corrective is named.

## World history (deep-time to modern)

**The question it asks.** How do you explain change at scales larger than any
single life — why societies diverge, why some transitions (agriculture,
industry) happen at all — when the evidence is fragmentary and produced by
the winners? History's real problem is not "what happened" but what counts as
a cause, and how you argue from sources that were never made for you.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Periodization is an argument, not a fact. Every "age" is somebody's claim.
- Braudel's three speeds: the *longue durée* (geography, climate), the
  *conjoncture* (institutions, prices), and the event. Most popular history
  only sees the third.
- The Neolithic transition was slow, plural, and costly — shorter lives and
  worse nutrition for centuries before it paid.
- The Malthusian ceiling: before ~1800, productivity gains converted into
  population, not living standards. Industrialization broke the trend line.
- The Great Divergence is late. Chinese and Indian core regions were
  comparable to European ones well into the 18th century.
- Disease and biota as historical agents — the Columbian Exchange killed and
  moved more people than any army.
- The archive is a selection effect. Literacy, storage, and power determine
  who leaves traces.
- Historiography: schools (Marxist, Annales, microhistory, postcolonial,
  global) are answers to "what is a cause," not just topics.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Treating history as a settled sequence to be memorized rather than a live
  argument over evidence.
- Taking grand-synthesis bestsellers as consensus. *Sapiens* (Harari) is a
  superb narrative and routinely overclaims where specialists disagree;
  *Guns, Germs, and Steel* (Diamond) is environmentally determinist in a way
  most historians reject. Read both, then read Pomeranz and Blaut.
- Assuming Europe was always ahead, then explaining a head start that didn't
  exist for most of the period.

**T3 — Literacy (~250 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **J.R. McNeill & William H. McNeill, *The Human Web*.** Short,
  genuinely global, written by working world historians. It's the door because
  it organizes everything around connection and exchange rather than a march
  of civilizations, which is the frame you want before any survey.
- *Survey:* Don't pretend one book does this. Run a route: (1) deep time —
  the free **Big History Project** or David Christian's *Maps of Time*; (2) a
  global spine — **J.M. Roberts & Odd Arne Westad, *The Penguin History of the
  World***, ~1,200 pages, the best single reference narrative; (3) two or
  three regional/period anchors read properly rather than skimmed — e.g.
  Peter Frankopan's *The Silk Roads* to decenter Europe, Eric Hobsbawm's
  *Age of Revolution / Capital / Empire / Extremes* quartet for 1789–1991,
  and one non-Western regional history (John Keay's *India*, John Iliffe's
  *Africans*, or Timothy Brook's *The Troubled Empire* for Ming China).
- *Canon:* Herodotus, *The Histories* (Book 1 and Book 7 — enough to see the
  invention of the genre); Thucydides, *The Peloponnesian War* (Book 1, the
  Melian Dialogue in Book 5, the Sicilian expedition in Books 6–7 — the
  hardest, best political history ever written); Ibn Khaldun, *The
  Muqaddimah* (the abridged Rosenthal/Dawood — read the chapters on
  *asabiyyah* and the dynastic cycle); Sima Qian, *Records of the Grand
  Historian* (selected biographies). Then the method book, which is
  non-optional: **Marc Bloch, *The Historian's Craft*** or **E.H. Carr,
  *What Is History?*** Bloch is warmer; Carr is sharper on causation.
- *Course:* **Big History Project** (free) for the deep-time frame; **Open
  Yale's *European Civilization, 1648–1945*** (John Merriman) and ***The
  Early Middle Ages, 284–1000*** (Paul Freedman) are complete free lecture
  courses and both are excellent. There is no good free *world* history
  course at this level — the route above replaces it.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words on the Great Divergence. State Pomeranz's case,
  state the strongest institutionalist counter (Landes, or Broadberry's
  quantitative work), and say precisely what evidence would settle it. If you
  can't name the evidence, you haven't understood the argument.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Tignor et al., *Worlds Together, Worlds Apart* (the standard world
  survey text, use it as scaffolding not reading); Jeremy Black & Donald
  MacRaild, *Studying History* for method; *The Cambridge World History* (7
  vols) as reference you consult, never read through.
- *Practice:* Learn a research language your period requires — Latin, classical
  Chinese, German, Arabic, Spanish. Get into an actual archive, learn its
  finding aids, and spend days there; paleography if you're premodern. Write
  historiographical essays: take one question, map who has argued what and on
  what evidence. Master Chicago-style footnote apparatus until it's automatic
  — it is how historians audit each other.
- *Primary literature:* *American Historical Review*, *Past & Present*,
  *Journal of Global History*, *Annales*, *Journal of World History*.
  Landmarks: Braudel, *The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the
  Age of Philip II*; Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence*; E.P. Thompson,
  *The Making of the English Working Class*; Carlo Ginzburg, *The Cheese and
  the Worms* (microhistory in one small book).
- *You've arrived when:* handed a folder of primary documents you've never
  seen, you can produce a defensible interpretive essay with full apparatus,
  and you can name the historiographical camps any given question sits inside.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Anna Green & Kathleen Troup, *The Houses of
  History*; Hayden White, *Metahistory*; Reinhart Koselleck, *Futures Past*;
  the relevant regional Cambridge history (*of China*, *of Africa*, *of
  Islam*) as your reference floor.
- *Frontier:* Global and connected history; history of capitalism (Sven
  Beckert, *Empire of Cotton*); environmental history and the Anthropocene;
  quantitative and digital methods on large text corpora. Jürgen
  Osterhammel's *The Transformation of the World* is the current model of
  what a global 19th century looks like.
- *Contribution looks like:* a monograph built on archival material nobody has
  synthesized, or a reinterpretation of a known corpus that forces others to
  re-argue. Articles matter; in history the book is still the unit.
- *Community:* American Historical Association, World History Association,
  Social Science History Association; the university presses (Cambridge,
  Princeton, Chicago, Harvard) are the venue that counts.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* **Economic history** — Robert Allen, *Global Economic
  History: A Very Short Introduction*, then the Cambridge economic histories;
  owns "why did living standards move when they did." **Intellectual
  history** — Quentin Skinner, *Visions of Politics* vol. 1 for the Cambridge
  School's contextualist method; owns "what did this text mean to do, in its
  own vocabulary." **Environmental history** — Alfred Crosby, *Ecological
  Imperialism*, and J.R. McNeill, *Something New Under the Sun*; owns nature
  as an agent rather than a stage. **Military history** — Michael Howard,
  *War in European History*, and John Keegan, *The Face of Battle*; owns what
  organized violence does to societies and they to it. **Social history from
  below** — E.P. Thompson and the *Past & Present* back catalogue; owns the
  lives of people who left no archive of their own. **History of science and
  technology** — Shapin & Schaffer, *Leviathan and the Air-Pump*; owns how
  knowledge acquired its authority. **Connected and global history** — Sanjay
  Subrahmanyam's "Connected Histories" (1997); owns the spaces between the
  national containers. **One region or period, properly** — pick it and read
  its Cambridge history as your floor.
- *The long canon:* Braudel, *Civilization and Capitalism, 15th–18th Century*
  (3 vols) — the great synthesis, read a volume a year; Gibbon, *Decline and
  Fall* — argument and prose both; Marc Bloch, *Feudal Society*; Marshall
  Hodgson, *The Venture of Islam* (3 vols) — still the most ambitious history
  of a civilization by an outsider who took it on its own terms; Eric Wolf,
  *Europe and the People Without History*; C.A. Bayly, *The Birth of the
  Modern World, 1780–1914*; Jonathan Spence, *The Search for Modern China*;
  Sheldon Pollock, *The Language of the Gods in the World of Men*; Michel-Rolph
  Trouillot, *Silencing the Past* — on how the Haitian Revolution became
  unthinkable; Dipesh Chakrabarty, *Provincializing Europe*; Columbia's
  *Sources of Chinese / Indian / Japanese Tradition* readers as your
  primary-source shelf; Robert Caro's *Years of Lyndon Johnson* for what
  biography can do. For the imperial argument, read Niall Ferguson's *Empire*
  against Caroline Elkins' *Legacy of Violence* — both are contested by
  specialists, and the disagreement is the education.
- *Re-foundation watch:* prehistory and premodern demography are being
  rewritten from outside the archive — ancient DNA, isotopes, ice cores, tree
  rings, pollen cores. The Justinianic plague's severity, the Black Death's
  reach, and every Bronze Age migration story have all moved since 2015 and
  will move again. Mass digitization plus text mining is opening corpora no one
  could read by hand; archives open and close politically (Soviet ones opened
  in the 1990s, Chinese ones have been closing since roughly 2013). Durable:
  Braudel's speeds, the lateness of the Great Divergence, the
  archive-as-selection-effect. Fashion: whichever single-cause grand narrative
  is currently selling. You have gone stale when your account of the Neolithic,
  of plague, or of who moved where predates the genomics, or when your reflex
  citation is a trade bestseller rather than a monograph.
- *A lifetime practice:* adopt one place — a town, a parish, a river valley,
  a city block — and work its records for the rest of your life. Land
  registers, censuses, newspapers, wills, photographs. Nobody else will do it,
  the sources are local and free, and after thirty years you will know
  something genuinely new. Transcription projects and a county archive will
  take you as a volunteer. Alongside it, read one journal cover to cover for
  decades; *Past & Present* is the best candidate.
- *Rabbit holes:* the Late Bronze Age collapse around 1200 BCE and whether it
  was one event; the Sogdian merchant network and its private letters; the
  Taiping Rebellion, the deadliest civil war in history and barely known
  outside China; 1816, the year without a summer, and Tambora; the Indian
  Ocean world before the Portuguese arrived in it.

## Anthropology & archaeology

**The question it asks.** What is universal about being human and what is
merely ours? And methodologically: how do you understand a way of life from
the inside without either projecting your own categories or dissolving into
the group? Archaeology asks the same question of people who left no one to
interview — how do you reconstruct behavior from broken objects and dirt?

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Culture as a learned, shared, symbolic system — not a trait list.
- Cultural relativism as a *method* (suspend judgment to understand) which is
  not the same as moral relativism. Conflating them is the classic error.
- Participant observation and thick description: the difference between a
  wink and a twitch is not in the eyelid (Geertz).
- Emic vs. etic — the insider's categories vs. the analyst's.
- The gift and reciprocity (Mauss): exchange creates obligation and
  relationship, and markets are the unusual case, not the default.
- Kinship as social technology; descent and alliance as ways of organizing
  property, labor, and politics.
- Theory lineages: functionalism → structuralism → interpretive →
  practice theory (Bourdieu's *habitus*: structure lived as disposition).
- Archaeological method: stratigraphy, seriation, radiocarbon with Bayesian
  modeling, taphonomy — absence of evidence usually is a preservation fact.
- Sedentism, monumentality, and agriculture came apart, not as a package
  (Göbekli Tepe, Poverty Point). The "Neolithic revolution" was many events.
- Ancient DNA has rewritten prehistoric migration in fifteen years.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Treating living societies as time capsules of the past. No group is a
  living fossil; everyone has the same amount of history.
- Trusting the field's famous popularizations uncritically. Mead's *Coming of
  Age in Samoa* is historically pivotal and empirically contested (see Derek
  Freeman's critique, and the rebuttals to Freeman). *The Dawn of Everything*
  (Graeber & Wengrow) is exhilarating and criticized by specialists for
  selective use of evidence — read it with the published reviews open.
- Thinking archaeology is about objects. It's about depositional context;
  an artifact without its context is nearly worthless.

**T3 — Literacy (~200 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **Clifford Geertz, *The Interpretation of Cultures*** — read
  "Thick Description" and "Deep Play: Notes on the Balinese Cockfight." It's
  the door because it shows you what interpretation actually consists of,
  and everything you read afterward is either doing that or arguing with it.
- *Survey:* For cultural, **Thomas Hylland Eriksen, *Small Places, Large
  Issues*** (short, global, not US-centric). For archaeology, **Colin Renfrew
  & Paul Bahn, *Archaeology: Theories, Methods and Practice*** — big,
  illustrated, and the genuine standard; read the method chapters, browse the
  rest.
- *Canon:* Malinowski, *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (the Introduction
  on method, plus the kula chapters — the Introduction alone is the founding
  document of fieldwork); Mauss, *The Gift* (short, read whole);
  Evans-Pritchard, *The Nuer* (the cattle and time-reckoning chapters);
  Lévi-Strauss, *Tristes Tropiques* (as literature and as a mind at work).
- *Course:* No open flagship is worth your time here — the lecture format
  fails this field. Read one full ethnography cover to cover instead; that is
  the training. Robert Sapolsky's Stanford *Human Behavioral Biology* lecture
  series (free, YouTube) is superb and covers the biological-anthropology
  flank. MIT OCW's 21A anthropology subjects publish full reading lists worth
  stealing.
- *Artifact:* an ethnographic sketch, 2,000 words, of a setting you can
  actually access — a gym, a church, a trading floor, a game server, a
  hospital waiting room. Minimum ten hours of observation with written field
  notes taken the same day. Produce thick description, then close with a
  paragraph on what your own position let you see and what it hid.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* H. Russell Bernard, *Research Methods in Anthropology* (the
  methods bible, quantitative and qualitative); Emerson, Fretz & Shaw,
  *Writing Ethnographic Fieldnotes*; Robert Layton, *An Introduction to
  Theory in Anthropology*; Renfrew & Bahn again, read properly.
- *Practice:* Sustained fieldwork — months, not weeks, in one site, with
  daily notes, semi-structured interviews, and enough language to stop using
  an interpreter. Build kinship and network diagrams. Get ethics approval and
  take consent seriously. For archaeology: a field school is non-negotiable —
  excavate, draw sections, fill context sheets, process finds, and write the
  stratigraphic report.
- *Primary literature:* *American Anthropologist*, *Current Anthropology*
  (with its peer-commentary format — the best way to see a field argue),
  *American Ethnologist*, *JRAI*, *Antiquity*, *Journal of Archaeological
  Science*. Landmarks: Clifford & Marcus (eds), *Writing Culture*; Bourdieu,
  *Outline of a Theory of Practice*; Sidney Mintz, *Sweetness and Power*;
  David Reich, *Who We Are and How We Got Here*.
- *You've arrived when:* you can design and defend a fieldwork project — site
  selection, sampling, ethics protocol, exit plan — and turn its data into a
  publishable article; or excavate a trench and write a report a regional
  specialist would accept.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Bourdieu, *The Logic of Practice*; Sherry
  Ortner, *Anthropology and Social Theory*; Michael Schiffer, *Formation
  Processes of the Archaeological Record*; Hodder & Hutson, *Reading the
  Past*; the *Annual Review of Anthropology* as your orientation each year.
- *Frontier:* archaeogenetics (the Reich lab; Max Planck Institute for
  Evolutionary Anthropology); the ontological turn (Philippe Descola, *Beyond
  Nature and Culture*; Viveiros de Castro); multispecies ethnography (Anna
  Tsing, *The Mushroom at the End of the World*); repatriation and
  decolonizing method; LiDAR and computational survey.
- *Contribution looks like:* an original ethnography from your own long
  fieldwork, or a site synthesis that changes a regional sequence. Both are
  book-shaped and take years.
- *Community:* American Anthropological Association, Society for American
  Archaeology, Royal Anthropological Institute, EASA; the AAA and SAA annual
  meetings.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* **Linguistic anthropology** — Alessandro Duranti, *Linguistic
  Anthropology*; owns how talk builds social worlds moment by moment.
  **Biological anthropology** — Boyd & Silk, *How Humans Evolved*; owns which
  of our behaviour is deep and which is local. **Economic anthropology** —
  Marshall Sahlins, *Stone Age Economics*, then Hann & Hart, *Economic
  Anthropology*; owns what an economy is when the market is not the default.
  **Medical anthropology** — Arthur Kleinman, *The Illness Narratives*, and
  Paul Farmer, *Infections and Inequalities*; owns how suffering is produced,
  named, and treated. **Legal and political anthropology** — Sally Engle Merry
  on rights translation, James Scott on evasion of the state; owns order
  without a court. **Archaeological science** — isotopes, residues, proteomics,
  zooarchaeology; owns "what did this person actually eat and where were they
  born." **Heritage, museums, and repatriation** — owns who the past belongs
  to, and it is now a legal question as much as a scholarly one. **Digital and
  computational archaeology** — LiDAR, photogrammetry, agent-based models.
- *The long canon:* Mary Douglas, *Purity and Danger*; Victor Turner, *The
  Ritual Process*; Evans-Pritchard, *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the
  Azande* — still the best demonstration that a system can be rational
  internally and false; Lévi-Strauss, *The Elementary Structures of Kinship*
  (hard, skim with a guide); Talal Asad (ed.), *Anthropology and the Colonial
  Encounter*; Jomo Kenyatta, *Facing Mount Kenya* — Malinowski's student
  writing his own people, and later Kenya's president; Zora Neale Hurston,
  *Mules and Men*; Marilyn Strathern, *The Gender of the Gift*; Nancy
  Scheper-Hughes, *Death Without Weeping*; Philippe Bourgois, *In Search of
  Respect*; Li Liu & Xingcan Chen, *The Archaeology of China* — the standard
  in English and a corrective to a very Near-East-centric field; Steven
  Mithen, *After the Ice*, for the whole postglacial world in one book. The
  Chagnon–Tierney affair over the Yanomamö is worth reading in full from all
  sides, including the AAA task force and its later rescission — it is the
  discipline's hardest case on evidence, ethics, and reputation.
- *Re-foundation watch:* the archaeological sciences are moving faster than any
  other field in this file. Ancient DNA, sedimentary DNA, palaeoproteomics,
  and Bayesian chronological modelling have already overturned Clovis-first,
  reopened Indo-European origins, and put dates on things that had none.
  Radiocarbon calibration curves get revised; check which one your source used.
  Meanwhile the legal and ethical frame around sampling human remains and
  holding collections is tightening fast — repatriation law is the live
  constraint on what research is even possible. Durable: thick description,
  the emic/etic distinction, context over object, the gift. Fashion:
  whichever theoretical turn is current. Stale when you cite a peopling date,
  a migration, or a domestication event without knowing whether genomics has
  touched it in the last decade.
- *A lifetime practice:* go back. Longitudinal fieldwork — the same site,
  the same families, every few years for forty years — is the rarest and most
  valuable thing anthropology produces, and almost nobody manages it. Failing
  that, join one local excavation every summer for decades and become the
  person who remembers the regional sequence. Keep field notes on your own
  society continuously; it is free, and you are the only researcher with
  access.
- *Rabbit holes:* the Sahlins–Obeyesekere argument over whether Hawaiians took
  Captain Cook for a god; Göbekli Tepe and Karahan Tepe, monumental building
  before farming; the settlement of the Pacific and what the sailing
  reconstructions proved; the Indus script and why sixty years of decipherment
  attempts have failed; the potlatch and its criminalization.

## Sociology

**The question it asks.** How does collective life produce stable patterns
that no individual intends and few would choose? The discipline's permanent
problem is structure versus agency — how much of what you do is you, and how
much is a position you occupy — and its permanent methodological problem is
inferring causation from a world it cannot randomize.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- The sociological imagination: converting private troubles into public
  issues, and back (Mills).
- Social facts are real and external — rates of suicide, marriage, and crime
  are stable properties of groups, not sums of biographies (Durkheim).
- Rationalization and the iron cage; bureaucracy as the characteristic modern
  form; *verstehen* and ideal types (Weber).
- Class, alienation, and the reproduction of advantage; Bourdieu's extension
  into cultural and social capital, field, and habitus.
- The social construction of reality (Berger & Luckmann) and labeling —
  deviance is created by the rule, not just by the act (Becker).
- Networks: weak ties carry novel information; economic action is embedded in
  social relations (Granovetter).
- Organizations converge in form for legitimacy, not efficiency —
  institutional isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell).
- Stratification: race and gender as structures, not attributes; Du Bois'
  double consciousness and *The Philadelphia Negro* as the first great
  empirical urban sociology.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- "Common sense with jargon." Many of the field's core results are
  counterintuitive and hard-won (weak ties, neighborhood effects, the
  weakness of contact-based prejudice reduction).
- Confusing description with endorsement. Explaining why a pattern persists
  is not defending it.
- Thinking sociology is all theory. The modal ASR paper is a regression on
  panel microdata.

**T3 — Literacy (~200 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **C. Wright Mills, *The Sociological Imagination*** —
  chapter 1, "The Promise," and chapter 2 on grand theory. It's a hundred
  pages, it's a mission statement, and it tells you what the discipline is
  *for* before you learn what it does.
- *Survey:* **Anthony Giddens & Philip Sutton, *Sociology*** — the standard
  global intro, deliberately not US-centric. The free **OpenStax
  *Introduction to Sociology*** is a solid, no-cost substitute for the map.
- *Canon:* Durkheim, *Suicide* (Book 2, especially egoistic and anomic
  suicide) and the conclusion of *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*;
  Weber, *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (whole — it's
  short) plus the essays "Politics as a Vocation" and "Science as a
  Vocation"; Marx, the 1844 manuscripts on alienation plus *The Communist
  Manifesto*; Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk* (chapter 1); Erving Goffman,
  *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* — the most immediately usable
  book on this list.
- *Course:* the free-course landscape here is genuinely thin; nothing rises to
  the level of CORE Econ or Shiller. **Open Yale's *Foundations of Modern
  Social Theory*** (Iván Szelényi) is the exception and covers the classical
  theorists well. Otherwise read Goffman and one modern ethnography instead of
  watching lectures.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words applying one classical frame (Durkheim, Weber,
  Marx, Bourdieu, or Goffman) to a contemporary pattern you can find real data
  on — remote work, declining religious attendance, a collapsing local
  institution. Then a section on what the frame cannot explain. The second
  section is the one that proves literacy.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Earl Babbie, *The Practice of Social Research* (methods); Craig
  Calhoun et al., *Classical Sociological Theory* and *Contemporary
  Sociological Theory* (readers — read the sources, not summaries of them);
  Angrist & Pischke, *Mostly Harmless Econometrics* for the causal toolkit
  sociology now runs on.
- *Practice:* Get your hands on public microdata and stay there — GSS, IPUMS
  (ACS/CPS), PSID, Add Health, World Values Survey — in R or Stata. Learn
  regression properly, then fixed effects, difference-in-differences,
  matching, and why each fails. Separately, do one qualitative project end to
  end: 20+ semi-structured interviews, transcribed, coded, with a memo trail.
  Design a survey instrument and then watch it produce artifacts.
- *Primary literature:* *American Sociological Review*, *American Journal of
  Sociology*, *Annual Review of Sociology*, *Social Forces*, *Sociological
  Science* (open access). Landmarks: Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties"
  (AJS, 1973); DiMaggio & Powell, "The Iron Cage Revisited" (ASR, 1983);
  William Julius Wilson, *The Truly Disadvantaged*; Annette Lareau, *Unequal
  Childhoods*; Matthew Desmond, *Evicted*.
- *You've arrived when:* you can take a question to a dataset, choose and
  defend an identification strategy, clean the data yourself, and write a
  results section that survives a methods-literate referee — and you can
  explain to a non-sociologist why the coefficient is not the causal effect.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Bourdieu, *Distinction*; Giddens, *The
  Constitution of Society*; Morgan & Winship, *Counterfactuals and Causal
  Inference* (the field's methodological center of gravity); Judea Pearl,
  *Causality*; Wasserman & Faust, *Social Network Analysis*; Peter Hedström,
  *Dissecting the Social* for the analytical-sociology program.
- *Frontier:* computational social science (Matthew Salganik, *Bit by Bit* —
  free online, and the best entry); digital trace data and its selection
  problems; mass collaboration and prediction (the Fragile Families
  Challenge, whose central finding was that prediction is *hard*); sociology
  of algorithms; mobility research reshaped by administrative data
  (Opportunity Insights).
- *Contribution looks like:* an ASR/AJS paper establishing a mechanism or
  killing a stylized fact, or a book-length ethnography that becomes the
  reference for a phenomenon.
- *Community:* American Sociological Association (the sections are where the
  actual subfields live), European Sociological Association; SocArXiv for
  preprints.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* **Economic sociology** — Viviana Zelizer, *The Social Meaning
  of Money*, and the Smelser & Swedberg *Handbook*; owns how markets are
  built, not assumed. **Demography** — Preston, Heuveline & Guillot,
  *Demography: Measuring and Modeling Population Processes*; owns the
  arithmetic of birth, death, and migration, and it is the most predictive
  social science there is. **Urban sociology** — Robert Sampson, *Great
  American City*; owns whether place itself causes anything. **Social
  movements** — Doug McAdam, *Political Process and the Development of Black
  Insurgency*, and Charles Tilly on contention; owns why people act together
  at some moments and not others. **Sociology of religion** — José Casanova,
  *Public Religions in the Modern World*; owns secularization, which turned
  out not to be a law. **Science and technology studies** — Latour & Woolgar,
  *Laboratory Life*, against Merton's earlier norms; owns how facts get made.
  **Comparative-historical sociology** — Michael Mann, *The Sources of Social
  Power*; owns the very long run. **Punishment and social control** — David
  Garland, *The Culture of Control*.
- *The long canon:* Weber, *Economy and Society* — a reference monument, not a
  read-through; Norbert Elias, *The Civilizing Process*; Robert Merton,
  *Social Theory and Social Structure* (manifest and latent functions,
  middle-range theory); Goffman, *Asylums* and *Stigma*; Bourdieu & Passeron,
  *Reproduction in Education, Society and Culture*; Immanuel Wallerstein, *The
  Modern World-System* (4 vols); Ulrich Beck, *Risk Society*; Du Bois, *The
  Philadelphia Negro* in full; Mitchell Duneier, *Sidewalk*, and Elijah
  Anderson, *Code of the Street*; Nancy Fraser & Axel Honneth, *Redistribution
  or Recognition?*; Fei Xiaotong, *From the Soil* — the founding text of
  Chinese sociology and the best short account of a non-Western social
  structure on its own terms; Raewyn Connell, *Southern Theory*, on why the
  canon is shaped the way it is; Syed Farid Alatas on Ibn Khaldun as a
  sociologist. Where books are contested, read the pair: Putnam's *Bowling
  Alone* with Claude Fischer's *Still Connected*, which finds far less
  decline; Herrnstein & Murray's *The Bell Curve* with Fischer et al.'s
  *Inequality by Design* and the ASA's statement.
- *Re-foundation watch:* two method revolutions are still running. First,
  survey data is in trouble — response rates have collapsed, probability
  samples are expensive, and mode changes break time series (the GSS's 2021
  redesign is the concrete example; check comparability before you plot a
  trend across it). Second, linked administrative data is replacing surveys
  for anything about income, mobility, or health — Nordic registers,
  Opportunity Insights, tax-and-employer records. Expect the causal
  identification bar to keep rising and preregistration to become normal.
  Durable: weak ties, institutional isomorphism, neighborhood effects,
  social facts as rates. Fashion: whatever the current single-variable
  explanation of inequality is. Stale when you quote a famous social-psych
  finding without checking whether it replicated, or treat a pre-2020 survey
  series as continuous.
- *A lifetime practice:* sit on something. A school board, a housing
  association, a union local, a co-op, a parish council — for years, taking
  notes. You get the internal view of an organization that no survey reaches,
  and organizational life is the discipline's real subject. Alongside it, read
  the *Annual Review of Sociology* front to back every year; it is the cheapest
  way to stay current across a field far too big to follow directly.
- *Rabbit holes:* the Stanford Prison Experiment and Milgram revisionism (Gina
  Perry, *Behind the Shock Machine*; Le Texier on the SPE archives) — a
  masterclass in how a finding becomes furniture; the Project on Human
  Development in Chicago Neighborhoods and what collective efficacy actually
  measured; the demography of religious groups that don't fertility-transition;
  Durkheim's suicide data re-examined with modern statistics; the sociology of
  queueing, tipping, and other small orders nobody legislated.

## Political science

**The question it asks.** How is power acquired, exercised, constrained, and
made to look legitimate — and why do some institutional arrangements produce
order, growth, and rights while others produce predation? The field is really
two enterprises sharing a building: normative theory asking what a state
*ought* to do, and empirical political science asking what actually explains
political behavior and outcomes.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- The state as the monopoly on legitimate violence (Weber); state *capacity*
  and state *scope* are different axes and often confused.
- Fukuyama's triad: a strong state, the rule of law, and accountability are
  three separable achievements. Most political dysfunction is one of the
  three missing.
- Collective action: shared interests do not produce shared action (Olson);
  Ostrom's fieldwork showed communities solving commons problems without
  either markets or states, under identifiable conditions.
- Institutions as the rules of the game; credible commitment as the scarce
  good; veto players explain policy stability.
- Selectorate logic: leaders survive by paying the coalition that keeps them
  in power, which explains why small-coalition regimes prefer private goods
  and predation.
- Electoral rules shape party systems (Duverger); the median voter theorem
  and its narrow conditions; Arrow's impossibility theorem — there is no
  perfect aggregation rule.
- Principal-agent chains all the way down: voters → legislators →
  bureaucrats.
- IR paradigms: realism (anarchy, security dilemma), liberalism
  (institutions, interdependence), constructivism (identity and norms); the
  democratic peace as the field's most durable empirical regularity.
- Political behavior: partisanship functions as social identity, not policy
  preference; most voters are low-information and retrospective.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Mistaking punditry for the discipline. Modern political science is
  statistical, formal, and often deliberately unexciting.
- Assuming their own country's arrangements are how politics works.
- Reading normative claims out of empirical findings, or vice versa.

**T3 — Literacy (~250 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **Francis Fukuyama, *The Origins of Political Order*** — the
  door because after it you will never again treat "state," "rule of law,"
  and "democracy" as one thing, and you'll have a genuinely comparative
  sweep (China, India, the Islamic world, Europe) rather than a Western one.
  *Political Order and Political Decay* continues it to the present.
- *Survey:* Robert Dahl, *On Democracy* (short, precise, and the standard
  definition of polyarchy); paired with Acemoglu & Robinson, *Why Nations
  Fail* — read it for the inclusive/extractive frame and read the critiques
  (it is widely faulted for causal overreach and selective cases).
- *Canon:* Machiavelli, *The Prince* (whole — it's 100 pages) and the
  *Discourses on Livy* Book 1 (more important, far less read); Hobbes,
  *Leviathan* (Introduction and chapters 13–18 — that's the whole argument);
  Locke, *Second Treatise*; Rousseau, *The Social Contract* Books 1–2;
  **Federalist 10 and 51** (non-negotiable, twenty pages, the best short
  statement of institutional design ever written); Tocqueville, *Democracy in
  America* (Vol. 1 Part 2; Vol. 2 Part 2); Mill, *On Liberty*.
- *Course:* **Michael Sandel's *Justice*** (Harvard, free at
  justiceharvard.org) is outstanding but it is political *philosophy*;
  **Open Yale's *Introduction to Political Philosophy*** (Steven B. Smith)
  is the more systematic version of the same. For the empirical side there is
  no comparable free flagship — use MIT OCW's political science subjects for
  their syllabi and reading lists rather than their videos.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words explaining one country's specific political
  outcome — a transition, a collapse, a durable authoritarian equilibrium —
  using two competing frameworks, and stating what observable evidence would
  distinguish them. Naming the discriminating evidence is the test.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Kenneth Shepsle, *Analyzing Politics* (the standard positive-theory
  introduction — do the exercises); Robert Jervis, *Perception and
  Misperception in International Politics* for IR; King, Keohane & Verba,
  *Designing Social Inquiry*, read together with its critics in Brady &
  Collier, *Rethinking Social Inquiry*. Reading KKV without the reply gives
  you half an education.
- *Practice:* Live inside the datasets: V-Dem, Polity, Correlates of War,
  the Comparative Manifesto Project, ANES and CSES, Afrobarometer and
  Latinobarómetro. Run survey experiments. Read constitutions and legislative
  records closely — the text is data too. Build one formal model by hand: a
  spatial voting model, or a simple bargaining game solved to equilibrium.
- *Primary literature:* *American Political Science Review*, *American
  Journal of Political Science*, *International Organization*, *World
  Politics*, *Comparative Political Studies*. Landmarks: Mancur Olson, *The
  Logic of Collective Action*; Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons*; Theda
  Skocpol, *States and Social Revolutions*; Bueno de Mesquita et al., *The
  Logic of Political Survival*.
- *You've arrived when:* you can referee an APSR submission — spot the
  endogeneity, name the measurement validity problem, and say what design
  would have answered the question.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Austen-Smith & Banks, *Positive Political
  Theory*; Gary Cox, *Making Votes Count*; Imbens & Rubin, *Causal Inference
  for Statistics, Social, and Biomedical Sciences*; for theory, Rawls, *A
  Theory of Justice* and Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* read against
  each other.
- *Frontier:* the credibility revolution arriving in comparative politics;
  text-as-data (Grimmer, Roberts & Stewart, *Text as Data*); conflict
  microdata; democratic backsliding as a research program (Levitsky &
  Ziblatt; the annual V-Dem reports); field experiments in development
  politics (EGAP, Metaketa).
- *Contribution looks like:* a mechanism plus credible identification in a
  top-three journal, or a book that changes what a subfield thinks its
  question is.
- *Community:* APSA, MPSA, EPSA, International Studies Association; EGAP for
  experimental design standards.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* **Authoritarian politics** — Milan Svolik, *The Politics of
  Authoritarian Rule*; owns the fact that most dictators are removed by their
  own elite, not by the people. **Political economy of development** — Atul
  Kohli, *State-Directed Development*; owns why some states can build and
  others cannot. **Bureaucracy and public administration** — James Q. Wilson,
  *Bureaucracy*; owns why agencies behave as they do once the statute is
  passed. **Public opinion and political behavior** — Achen & Bartels,
  *Democracy for Realists*, and Converse's 1964 "Nature of Belief Systems";
  owns how little most voters resemble the model. **Security studies** —
  Thomas Schelling, *Arms and Influence*, still the best book on coercion.
  **International political economy** — Jeffry Frieden, *Global Capitalism*,
  and Dani Rodrik's globalization trilemma; owns the politics behind trade and
  capital flows. **Republican political theory** — Philip Pettit, *On the
  People's Terms*; owns freedom as non-domination rather than non-interference.
  **Subnational and local politics** — the level where most governing happens
  and least research goes.
- *The long canon:* Aristotle, *Politics* Books 3–6 — the original comparative
  politics, and still sharp on regime decay; Karl Polanyi, *The Great
  Transformation*; Barrington Moore, *Social Origins of Dictatorship and
  Democracy*; Charles Tilly, *Coercion, Capital, and European States*; Juan
  Linz & Alfred Stepan, *Problems of Democratic Transition and
  Consolidation*; Robert Dahl, *Who Governs?*; Adam Przeworski et al.,
  *Democracy and Development*; James C. Scott, *Weapons of the Weak* and *The
  Art of Not Being Governed*; Guillermo O'Donnell on bureaucratic
  authoritarianism and delegative democracy — the best theorist Latin
  America's own experience produced; Partha Chatterjee, *The Politics of the
  Governed*, on how most of the world is actually governed; Kenneth Waltz,
  *Theory of International Politics*, read against Alexander Wendt's *Social
  Theory of International Politics*. Huntington is a required argument on both
  ends: *Political Order in Changing Societies* is a genuine classic, while
  *The Clash of Civilizations* is far weaker and has been picked apart by area
  specialists — read Edward Said's and Amartya Sen's replies. On democracy's
  worth, read Bryan Caplan's *The Myth of the Rational Voter* and Jason
  Brennan's *Against Democracy* against Hélène Landemore's *Democratic
  Reason*; the disagreement is honest and unresolved.
- *Re-foundation watch:* the credibility revolution is still arriving in
  comparative politics and IR, and preregistration plus data sharing became
  norms only after a fraud scandal (the LaCour retraction) forced the issue.
  Text-as-data and now language models are opening legislative records, court
  opinions, and party manifestos to analysis at a scale nobody had; conflict
  event data (ACLED, UCDP) keeps improving and keeps revising past estimates.
  Watch the measurement debates over expert-coded indices like V-Dem, because
  the entire backsliding literature rests on them. Durable: Duverger, veto
  players, selectorate logic, the democratic peace, collective action. Fashion:
  each decade's confident story about where regimes are heading — the third
  wave, the end of history, the backsliding wave. Stale when your model of
  party competition predates the last realignment, or you cannot name a serious
  scholar who disagrees with your read of your own country.
- *A lifetime practice:* adopt a country that is not yours and follow it for
  life — its elections, budgets, constitutional court, and press, in its own
  language if you can. Record a written forecast before each election and grade
  yourself afterwards; thirty years of scored predictions will teach you more
  about political science than thirty years of reading it. Do local party or
  council work at least once, so you know what the incentives feel like from
  inside.
- *Rabbit holes:* the single transferable vote and its known pathologies;
  Ostrom's Nepali irrigation fieldwork and why farmer-built canals outperformed
  engineered ones; the drafting of the Indian constitution (Granville Austin,
  *The Indian Constitution*); Michels' iron law of oligarchy tested on real
  organizations; Portugal's 1974–76 transition, the messiest and most
  instructive of the third wave.

## Law & legal systems

**The question it asks.** What makes a rule *law* rather than a command backed
by force — and how do legal systems generate predictable, legitimate outcomes
from text that is always open to more than one reading? Comparatively: why do
common law and civil law systems, with opposite methods, so often arrive at
similar answers?

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Positivism vs. natural law (Hart vs. Fuller): whether law's validity depends
  on its content. Hart's rule of recognition and the "internal point of view"
  are the load-bearing concepts.
- Common law reasoning: precedent, *stare decisis*, *ratio decidendi* vs.
  *obiter dicta*, and distinguishing — which is how judges change law while
  claiming to follow it.
- The civil law tradition: codes, deduction from general principles, the judge
  as career civil servant, no formally binding precedent. Most of the world
  works this way.
- Statutory interpretation: textualism, purposivism, and what legislative
  history is worth.
- Procedure is substance in disguise — standing, jurisdiction, burdens of
  proof, and limitation periods decide more cases than doctrine.
- Constitutionalism and judicial review; Bickel's countermajoritarian
  difficulty — why unelected judges overriding legislatures needs a story.
- Private law's core: contract (bargain, consideration, breach), tort
  (duty, negligence, the Hand formula), property (a bundle of rights, not a
  thing), crime (*actus reus* plus *mens rea*).
- Legal realism: what officials do in fact, not what doctrine says they do.
  Law and economics is realism with a price tag (Coase, efficient breach).
- Law on the books vs. law in action — the gap is the subject of most
  empirical legal studies.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Imagining law as a lookup table. Almost all legal work is arguing over
  which rule applies and which facts count.
- Universalizing their own system. If you've only met common law, you've met
  the minority tradition.
- Reading opinions as neutral deduction. They are also policy choices written
  in the grammar of deduction.

**T3 — Literacy (~250 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **H.L.A. Hart, *The Concept of Law*** — chapters 1–6 in
  particular. It's the door because afterward you can never again confuse law
  with orders backed by threats, or with morality, and both confusions are
  everywhere.
- *Survey:* **Merryman & Pérez-Perdomo, *The Civil Law Tradition*** — short,
  and it decenters the common law immediately. Add Jay Feinman, *Law 101* if
  you want the American map, and Raymond Wacks, *Law: A Very Short
  Introduction*, for the concept map.
- *Canon:* Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., "The Path of the Law" (1897, twenty
  pages, still the best statement of realism); Lon Fuller, *The Morality of
  Law* (chapter 2, the King Rex parable — a whole legal theory as a story);
  Ronald Dworkin, *Law's Empire* (chapter 1 and the Hercules material). Then
  read six to eight landmark opinions in full and brief them yourself:
  *Marbury v. Madison*, *Brown v. Board of Education*, *Donoghue v.
  Stevenson* (UK — modern negligence), *Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.*,
  *Riggs v. Palmer*, and at least one from your own jurisdiction.
- *Course:* **Penn's *Introduction to American Law*** (Coursera, free to
  audit) is a decent orientation; **Harvard's *CopyrightX*** is genuinely
  excellent if the subject fits. But the highest-value 200 pages are Fischl &
  Paul, ***Getting to Maybe*** — nominally an exam-taking book, actually the
  clearest available account of how legal argument is constructed.
- *Artifact:* brief three cases yourself in a standard format (facts, issue,
  holding, reasoning, dissent), then write 1,500 words tracing how one
  doctrine shifted across them — and argue whether the shift was
  interpretation or policy.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* At this tier you need a real casebook in a real field — Farnsworth
  on contracts, Prosser & Keeton on torts, or your jurisdiction's standard.
  Chirelstein's *Concepts and Case Analysis in the Law of Contracts* is the
  short, humane version. For theory: Brian Bix, *Jurisprudence: Theory and
  Context*. For research: your jurisdiction's citation manual, cover to cover.
- *Practice:* Read cases in full, constantly — briefing a hundred-plus
  opinions *is* the training, and there is no substitute. Do IRAC-structured
  issue spotting on unfamiliar fact patterns. Draft a contract, then draft the
  clause that breaks it. Write a memo and a motion. Follow one live case
  through its docket (CourtListener and RECAP are free). A clinic or moot
  court if you can get one.
- *Primary literature:* *Harvard Law Review*, *Yale Law Journal*, *Journal of
  Legal Studies*, *American Journal of Comparative Law*; SSRN's legal
  networks are where working papers live. Landmarks: Coase, "The Problem of
  Social Cost" (1960); Calabresi & Melamed, "Property Rules, Liability Rules,
  and Inalienability" (1972); Bickel, *The Least Dangerous Branch*; Hart &
  Sacks, *The Legal Process*.
- *You've arrived when:* given an unfamiliar fact pattern plus the relevant
  statutes and cases, you can write a memo that spots the issues, argues both
  sides honestly, and predicts the outcome with reasons a practitioner would
  accept.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Joseph Raz, *The Authority of Law*; John
  Finnis, *Natural Law and Natural Rights*; Richard Posner, *Economic
  Analysis of Law*; Steven Shavell, *Foundations of Economic Analysis of
  Law*; Zweigert & Kötz, *An Introduction to Comparative Law*. Note plainly:
  in most jurisdictions the practicing and academic paths run through a
  professional degree (JD, LLB, then LLM/SJD or PhD). This is one of the few
  domains in the ledger where autodidacticism hits a licensing wall.
- *Frontier:* empirical legal studies (quantitative work on judges, courts,
  and sentencing); algorithmic governance and AI regulation; comparative
  constitutional design (the Comparative Constitutions Project); climate
  litigation; international criminal law.
- *Contribution looks like:* doctrinal scholarship that reframes a line of
  cases, empirical work on how legal institutions actually behave, or
  changing doctrine directly through litigation, drafting, or the bench.
- *Community:* American Law Institute, American Society of Comparative Law,
  Conference on Empirical Legal Studies, Society of Legal Scholars (UK), your
  own bar association.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* **Comparative law** — Mathias Siems, *Comparative Law*, on
  top of Zweigert & Kötz; owns whether different systems converge on the same
  answers by different routes. **Public international law** — Malcolm Shaw,
  *International Law*; owns what makes a rule binding with no sovereign above
  it. **Islamic law** — Wael Hallaq, *An Introduction to Islamic Law*, then
  *Sharī'a: Theory, Practice, Transformations*; owns what law looks like when
  jurists rather than states are its authors, which is most of legal history.
  **Chinese law** — Jianfu Chen, *Chinese Law: Context and Transformation*;
  owns the largest legal system that fits neither tradition you were taught.
  **Legal history** — J.H. Baker, *An Introduction to English Legal History*,
  and Lawrence Friedman, *A History of American Law*; owns where the doctrines
  came from and how contingent they are. **Constitutional design** — Ginsburg
  & Huq, and the Comparative Constitutions Project data; owns what actually
  makes a constitution last. **Criminal justice and punishment** — owns why
  incarceration rates differ by an order of magnitude between rich democracies.
  **Customary and indigenous law** — owns law without writing or a state.
- *The long canon:* Justinian's *Institutes* and selections from the *Digest*
  — the root of the tradition most of the world uses; Henry Maine, *Ancient
  Law*; Hans Kelsen, *Pure Theory of Law*; A.V. Dicey on the rule of law, and
  Carl Schmitt's *Political Theology* as the strongest attack on it, read with
  its critics; Benjamin Cardozo, *The Nature of the Judicial Process* — a
  sitting judge explaining what he actually does; Karl Llewellyn, *The Bramble
  Bush*; E.P. Thompson, *Whigs and Hunters*, whose closing pages are a Marxist
  historian conceding the rule of law is "an unqualified human good" — the
  most interesting concession in the literature; Marc Galanter, "Why the
  'Haves' Come Out Ahead" (1974); Catharine MacKinnon, *Toward a Feminist
  Theory of the State*, and Kimberlé Crenshaw's intersectionality articles;
  H. Patrick Glenn, *Legal Traditions of the World*, the only genuinely global
  survey. On mass incarceration read the argument as a set: Michelle
  Alexander's *The New Jim Crow*, then John Pfaff's *Locked In*, which shows
  the drug-war account cannot explain the growth and points at prosecutors,
  then James Forman Jr.'s *Locking Up Our Own*.
- *Re-foundation watch:* case law has become machine-readable at scale
  (CourtListener, the Caselaw Access Project), which is turning doctrinal
  claims into testable ones for the first time. Language models are entering
  practice, and the resulting rules on evidence, competence, and
  privilege will be written in the next decade. Watch the EU's AI Act and data
  regimes as the world's default regulatory template, climate litigation after
  Urgenda and KlimaSeniorinnen, and the long swing of interpretive method in
  whatever apex court governs you. Durable: the positivism/natural-law
  problem, procedure as substance, the law-in-action gap. Fashion: the
  jurisprudential school of the moment. Stale when you cannot name the last
  five years' leading cases in an area you have opinions about, or when your
  picture of your own constitution is a decade old.
- *A lifetime practice:* pick one legal question and follow it for forty years
  — water rights, land title, free expression, corporate personhood, the
  status of a minority language. Read every significant judgment as it lands
  and keep a running annotated file. Almost nobody tracks a doctrine across
  decades, and the person who does can see the shape of a change while it is
  happening. Serve where you are allowed to: a jury, a tribunal panel, a
  planning or licensing board, a charity's legal committee.
- *Rabbit holes:* the reception of Roman law in medieval Europe and how a dead
  code conquered a continent; the drafting of the German BGB against the
  French Code civil; Nuremberg's improvised legality and the charge of victor's
  justice; the *Lochner* era and the ongoing fight over whether it was wrong;
  India's public interest litigation experiment, where a court invited the
  poor to write it letters.

## Economics

**The question it asks.** How do millions of decentralized choices under
scarcity aggregate into prices, growth, unemployment, and inequality — and
when does that aggregation fail badly enough to justify intervention?
Underneath sits the discipline's methodological obsession: separating cause
from correlation in a world you cannot run twice.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Opportunity cost and thinking at the margin. Almost every economic argument
  is one of these two, dressed up.
- Supply, demand, equilibrium — and elasticity, which tells you *who actually
  bears* a tax or a price control regardless of who writes the check.
- Comparative advantage: gains from trade don't require anyone to be better
  at anything.
- Market failure as a taxonomy: externalities, public goods, information
  asymmetry (Akerlof's lemons), market power. Each implies a different remedy.
- The welfare theorems — markets are efficient *given* strong assumptions;
  learn the assumptions, because policy lives in their violation.
- Macro scaffolding: the national accounts identity, money and inflation, the
  Phillips curve's instability, monetary vs. fiscal transmission, and the
  Lucas critique (relationships estimated under one policy regime break when
  the regime changes).
- Growth: Solow's model, conditional convergence, and TFP as "a measure of
  our ignorance." The institutions/geography/culture debate lives here.
- Strategic interaction: Nash equilibrium, commitment, principal-agent.
- Behavioral corrections: bounded rationality, prospect theory, present bias.
- The credibility revolution: RCTs, difference-in-differences, instrumental
  variables, regression discontinuity — design, not just estimation.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Thinking economics is about money or predicting markets. It's about
  constrained choice; money is one instance.
- "Economists never agree." There is broad consensus on many microeconomic
  propositions and genuine, deep disagreement in macro. The IGM Forum's
  expert panel polls are a free, quick reality check on which is which.
- Reading a positive result as a policy recommendation.

**T3 — Literacy (~250 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **CORE Econ, *The Economy*** (free at core-econ.org). Start
  with a course, not a book — it is the modern introduction, and it leads with
  inequality, institutions, power, and real data instead of Chapter 1
  supply-and-demand. It is the single best free resource named in this file.
- *Survey:* **Robert Heilbroner, *The Worldly Philosophers*** — the great
  economists as a story, readable in a weekend, and it gives you the
  intellectual lineage CORE assumes. Then Charles Wheelan, *Naked Economics*,
  if you want the concepts once more in plain language.
- *Canon:* Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* Book I chapters 1–3 (division
  of labor, extent of the market) and Book IV chapter 2 — the "invisible
  hand" is one sentence and is almost always misquoted; Ricardo, *Principles*
  chapter 7 (comparative advantage); **Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in
  Society" (1945)** — fifteen pages, free online, essential, and the best
  argument for prices ever written; Keynes, *The General Theory* chapters 12
  and 24 only (the rest is genuinely hard and you should read *about* it
  first); Milton Friedman, *Capitalism and Freedom* chapters 1–2.
- *Course:* CORE, above. Then **MIT OCW 14.01 *Principles of
  Microeconomics*** and **14.02 *Principles of Macroeconomics*** — and do the
  problem sets, which are posted with solutions. Marginal Revolution
  University's free video library is a good supplement.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words on a real policy with real data — a minimum wage
  change, a carbon tax, a zoning reform. Include a supply/demand analysis, an
  explicit incidence claim (who bears it), the strongest counterargument
  stated fairly, and at least one chart you made yourself from public data
  (FRED, World Bank WDI, or your national statistics office).

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Micro — Hal Varian, *Intermediate Microeconomics*. Macro — Olivier
  Blanchard, *Macroeconomics*, then David Romer, *Advanced Macroeconomics* as
  the bridge. Econometrics — Jeffrey Wooldridge, *Introductory Econometrics*,
  then Angrist & Pischke, *Mostly Harmless Econometrics*. Math — Simon &
  Blume, *Mathematics for Economists*, which is where most self-taught
  economists stall; do not skip it.
- *Practice:* Economics is not a reading subject. Work every problem set.
  Then replicate published papers from journal data archives — the AEA
  journals require deposits, and reproducing a table from someone's real code
  teaches more than three textbooks. Build a small model in Python, R, or
  Julia. Write a referee report on an NBER working paper and compare it to
  the published version.
- *Primary literature:* *American Economic Review*, *Quarterly Journal of
  Economics*, *Econometrica*, *Journal of Political Economy*, *Review of
  Economic Studies*. Start with the *Journal of Economic Perspectives* — it
  is deliberately readable and free. Landmarks: Akerlof, "The Market for
  Lemons" (1970); Card & Krueger on minimum wages (AER, 1994); Acemoglu,
  Johnson & Robinson, "The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development"
  (AER, 2001); Solow (1956).
- *You've arrived when:* you can read an AER empirical paper, state its
  identifying assumption in one sentence, name a plausible violation of it,
  and reproduce one of its tables from the replication package.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Mas-Colell, Whinston & Green, *Microeconomic
  Theory* (MWG — the standard and genuinely brutal); Ljungqvist & Sargent,
  *Recursive Macroeconomic Theory*; Stokey & Lucas, *Recursive Methods in
  Economic Dynamics*; Acemoglu, *Introduction to Modern Economic Growth*;
  econometrics — Hayashi, *Econometrics*, Wooldridge's *Econometric Analysis
  of Cross Section and Panel Data*, and Bruce Hansen's *Econometrics* (drafts
  free online).
- *Frontier:* the credibility revolution consolidated (Card, Angrist and
  Imbens, Nobel 2021); heterogeneous-agent macro (HANK); development field
  experiments (J-PAL; Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer); inequality measurement
  (Piketty, Saez, Zucman; WID.world); market design (Roth, Milgrom);
  machine learning for causal inference (Athey, Chernozhukov); climate
  economics.
- *Contribution looks like:* a credibly identified empirical result on a
  first-order question, a model that changes what people condition on, or —
  often the most durable — a new dataset or measurement others build on for
  decades.
- *Community:* American Economic Association (the ASSA January meetings),
  Econometric Society, NBER, CEPR; NBER and CEPR working papers are the real
  publication timeline.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* **Development economics** — Banerjee & Duflo, *Poor
  Economics*, read against Angus Deaton's critique of what randomization can
  and cannot tell you; owns why poverty persists at the household level.
  **Public economics** — Jonathan Gruber, *Public Finance and Public Policy*;
  owns who really bears a tax and what governments should provide. **Labour
  economics** — Cahuc, Carcillo & Zylberberg, *Labor Economics*; owns wages,
  matching, and the most policy-relevant empirical literature in the field.
  **Industrial organization** — Jean Tirole, *The Theory of Industrial
  Organization*; owns market power and what to do about it. **Economic
  history and cliometrics** — Joel Mokyr, *A Culture of Growth*, and Robert
  Allen; owns the growth question at the century scale. **History of economic
  thought** — Mark Blaug, *Economic Theory in Retrospect*; owns why the field
  asks what it asks. **Environmental and climate economics** — the
  Nordhaus–Stern discount-rate argument and Weitzman on fat tails; owns
  valuing the far future. **Heterodox traditions** — Minsky for
  post-Keynesian, Mises and Hayek for Austrian, Folbre and Marilyn Waring's
  *If Women Counted* for feminist economics; each owns something the mainstream
  measures badly.
- *The long canon:* Alfred Marshall, *Principles of Economics*; Schumpeter,
  *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* Part II, then *History of Economic
  Analysis* as reference; Albert Hirschman, *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* and
  *The Passions and the Interests* — the most reliably surprising economist
  to read; Friedman & Schwartz, *A Monetary History of the United States*;
  Hyman Minsky, *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy*; Amartya Sen, *Collective
  Choice and Social Welfare* and *Development as Freedom*; W. Arthur Lewis on
  dual-sector development; Cardoso & Faletto, *Dependency and Development in
  Latin America*, with Prebisch behind it — the tradition that shaped policy
  across the global South and is barely taught in Anglophone programmes;
  Ha-Joon Chang, *Kicking Away the Ladder*, heterodox and contested but a real
  argument; Robert Gordon, *The Rise and Fall of American Growth*; Deirdre
  McCloskey's *Bourgeois* trilogy as the culture-first counter to
  institutionalism; Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* read with
  the measurement critiques (Rognlie on housing, Auten & Splinter on US top
  shares) — the debate is about data construction, and that is worth learning
  in itself.
- *Re-foundation watch:* macro is the likeliest part of this field to be
  unrecognizable in twenty years. It has been rebuilt twice already — once
  after 2008 around finance and heterogeneous agents, again after 2020 around
  fiscal-monetary interaction and the return of inflation. Meanwhile the
  credibility revolution is having its own reckoning: external validity,
  publication bias, and specification searching are now measured (Brodeur and
  co-authors), the AEA mandates data deposits, and administrative and
  firm-level microdata are replacing surveys. Durable: incidence, comparative
  advantage, the market-failure taxonomy, the Lucas critique. Fashion: the
  current consensus on the Phillips curve, on optimal debt, and on trade's
  labour-market effects — the last was rewritten by the China shock papers
  (Autor, Dorn & Hanson). Stale when you repeat a result that has failed
  replication; Reinhart & Rogoff's 90 percent debt threshold and the Herndon,
  Ash & Pollin correction is the standing example.
- *A lifetime practice:* maintain one long series of your own. Prices at your
  local shops, rents on one street, your household's complete accounts,
  wages in one trade — collected the same way, the same month, for decades.
  Nothing else teaches you what a statistic is made of, and after twenty years
  you own data nobody else has. Pair it with reading the *Journal of Economic
  Perspectives* cover to cover every quarter; it is free, deliberately
  readable, and over fifty years it is an education by itself.
- *Rabbit holes:* the socialist calculation debate — Mises and Hayek against
  Lange and Lerner, and whether computation changes the answer; the Cambridge
  capital controversy, which one side won and everyone kept teaching anyway;
  the great hyperinflations, especially Hungary 1946; the ultimatum game run
  across fifteen small-scale societies (Henrich et al.) and what it did to
  "economic man"; Japan's lost decades as the rich world's most studied
  natural experiment.

## Finance & markets

**The question it asks.** How do you price a claim on uncertain future cash
flows today, and who ends up bearing which risk? The field's live empirical
puzzle: markets are efficient enough that beating them consistently is very
hard, and not efficient enough to explain bubbles, crashes, and anomalies
that persist for decades.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Time value of money and discounting. Everything else is built on this one
  operation.
- No-arbitrage as the master principle: if you can replicate a payoff, you
  can price it. This, not the formula, is what Black-Scholes is about.
- Diversification and the split between systematic and idiosyncratic risk —
  only the first is compensated. CAPM formalizes it and then fails
  empirically; factor models (Fama-French and successors) are the patch.
- The efficient market hypothesis in three forms, and the joint hypothesis
  problem: any test of efficiency is also a test of your pricing model. The
  practical consequence is passive indexing.
- Options as building blocks: payoff diagrams, put-call parity, and the fact
  that what actually trades is volatility.
- Banking as maturity transformation, and why that makes runs a rational
  equilibrium (Diamond-Dybvig). Leverage and liquidity are the two ways
  institutions die.
- Modigliani-Miller as a benchmark: capital structure is irrelevant in a
  frictionless world, so every real capital-structure decision is about
  taxes, distress costs, or agency problems.
- Market microstructure: bid-ask spreads compensate for adverse selection;
  liquidity is a service someone is paid to provide.
- Behavioral finance and limits to arbitrage — knowing a price is wrong is
  not enough if you can be forced out before it corrects.
- Systemic risk, moral hazard, and Bagehot's rule: lend freely, at a penalty
  rate, against good collateral.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Reading "efficient" as "correct." It means hard to beat, which is a much
  weaker and much more defensible claim.
- Confusing investing with trading, and mistaking a backtest for evidence —
  survivorship bias and overfitting make almost any strategy look good in
  sample.
- Equating risk with volatility. Ruin risk, leverage, and
  correlations-going-to-one in a crisis are what actually destroy capital.

**T3 — Literacy (~200 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **Burton Malkiel, *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*** — the
  door because it forces the efficiency question first, and your answer to
  that question reorganizes everything else you'll read.
- *Survey:* **Robert Shiller's Open Yale ECON 252, *Financial Markets*** —
  free, complete video lectures, a Nobel laureate walking through the entire
  system from insurance to behavioral finance. Add John Kay, *Other People's
  Money*, for a clear-eyed account of what finance is actually for.
- *Canon:* Benjamin Graham, *The Intelligent Investor* — chapters 8 (Mr.
  Market) and 20 (margin of safety); the rest is dated and you may skip it.
  Walter Bagehot, *Lombard Street* (1873) — still cited by central bankers,
  which tells you something. Kindleberger & Aliber, *Manias, Panics, and
  Crashes*. Fama, "Efficient Capital Markets" (1970). Michael Lewis's *Liar's
  Poker* and *The Big Short* are reportage, not canon — read them for texture.
- *Course:* Open Yale ECON 252 above; and **MIT OCW 15.401 *Finance Theory
  I*** (Andrew Lo), which has problem sets and is where you actually learn to
  compute. Aswath Damodaran (NYU) posts his entire valuation course free —
  the best finance teaching available at any price.
- *Artifact:* build a DCF for one real public company from its own 10-K (not
  a summary site). State every assumption explicitly. Then invert it: compute
  what growth rate the current market price implies, and write 1,500 words on
  why the market might be right and you wrong. Optionally run a paper
  portfolio against a broad index for six months and write the postmortem
  honestly.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Bodie, Kane & Marcus, *Investments*; Brealey, Myers & Allen,
  *Principles of Corporate Finance*; John Hull, *Options, Futures, and Other
  Derivatives* (the derivatives standard — work the problems); Damodaran,
  *Investment Valuation*.
- *Practice:* Read 10-Ks and their footnotes — the footnotes are where the
  information is. Build three-statement models that actually tie. Backtest a
  factor strategy using Ken French's free data library, then deliberately try
  to break your own backtest with out-of-sample and transaction costs. Price
  the same option two ways — binomial tree and Black-Scholes — and reconcile
  them. Sit through a full earnings call including the analyst Q&A.
- *Primary literature:* *Journal of Finance*, *Journal of Financial
  Economics*, *Review of Financial Studies*, *Financial Analysts Journal*.
  Landmarks: Markowitz, "Portfolio Selection" (1952); Black & Scholes (1973);
  Fama & French, "The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns" (1992);
  Diamond & Dybvig (1983).
- *You've arrived when:* you can value a company or a derivative from primary
  documents, defend every input, and articulate what the market is implicitly
  assuming instead — and you know which of your assumptions the answer is
  most sensitive to.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* John Cochrane, *Asset Pricing*; Darrell Duffie,
  *Dynamic Asset Pricing Theory*; Steven Shreve, *Stochastic Calculus for
  Finance* I and II; Campbell, Lo & MacKinlay, *The Econometrics of Financial
  Markets*; Jean Tirole, *The Theory of Corporate Finance*.
- *Frontier:* the factor zoo and asset pricing's own replication crisis
  (Harvey and co-authors on multiple testing; Hou, Xue & Zhang); machine
  learning for return prediction (Gu, Kelly & Xiu); intermediary asset
  pricing (He & Krishnamurthy); market structure after high-frequency
  trading; climate risk pricing; private credit and the migration of
  intermediation outside banks.
- *Contribution looks like:* a priced factor or anomaly that survives out of
  sample and in other people's data; a theoretical mechanism that explains an
  anomaly rather than naming it; or measurably better institutional practice.
- *Community:* American Finance Association (AFA meetings), Western Finance
  Association, NBER Asset Pricing; SSRN's financial economics network for
  working papers; CFA Institute on the practitioner track.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* **Fixed income and rates** — Bruce Tuckman & Angel Serrat,
  *Fixed Income Securities*; owns how the term structure prices time, and it
  is a much larger market than equities. **Money and banking** — Perry
  Mehrling's *The New Lombard Street* and his free Coursera course on the
  economics of money and banking; owns who backstops the payment system, which
  is the question every crisis turns out to be about. **Corporate governance**
  — owns who controls the firm and for whom; the Bebchuk–Hirst work on index
  funds' voting power is where it now bites. **Financial regulation and
  stability** — Admati & Hellwig, *The Bankers' New Clothes*; owns capital
  requirements and why the industry's objections to them are mostly wrong.
  **Financial history** — Homer & Sylla, *A History of Interest Rates*, a
  four-thousand-year dataset in one volume. **Household finance** — John
  Bogle, *Common Sense on Mutual Funds*, and William Bernstein; owns what an
  ordinary person should actually do, which is the only part of this field
  most people need. **Quantitative investing** — Marcos López de Prado,
  *Advances in Financial Machine Learning*, opinionated and unusually honest
  about backtest overfitting. **Insurance and catastrophe risk** — owns
  pricing tails you cannot diversify away.
- *The long canon:* Peter Bernstein, *Against the Gods* and *Capital Ideas* —
  risk and modern finance as intellectual history; Edward Chancellor, *Devil
  Take the Hindmost*; Fred Schwed, *Where Are the Customers' Yachts?*, still
  the funniest true book about the industry; Warren Buffett's shareholder
  letters, free on the Berkshire site and a primary-source collection in
  themselves (Cunningham's *The Essays of Warren Buffett* organizes them);
  Robert Shiller, *Irrational Exuberance*; Andrew Lo, *Adaptive Markets*;
  Emanuel Derman, *My Life as a Quant*; Perry Mehrling, *Fischer Black and the
  Revolutionary Idea of Finance*; Reinhart & Rogoff, *This Time Is Different*
  — the sovereign-debt dataset is a genuine achievement even though their
  separate debt-threshold paper did not survive; Timur Kuran, *The Long
  Divergence*, on how Islamic commercial and inheritance law shaped the Middle
  East's financial development; Ronald Dore, *Stock Market Capitalism: Welfare
  Capitalism*, on Japan and Germany as live alternatives to the Anglo-American
  model. Taleb's *Fooled by Randomness* and *The Black Swan* are worth reading
  and overclaim; pair them with actual extreme value theory (Embrechts,
  Klüppelberg & Mikosch). Mackay's *Extraordinary Popular Delusions* is
  charming and its tulip chapter is largely wrong — Anne Goldgar's
  *Tulipmania* is the corrective.
- *Re-foundation watch:* market plumbing changes faster than theory.
  Decimalization, then high-frequency trading, then shortened settlement, and
  next some form of tokenized settlement and central bank digital currency —
  each rewrote who holds inventory and where liquidity comes from.
  Intermediation keeps migrating out of banks into private credit and funds,
  which means the next crisis will happen somewhere the last regulations do
  not reach. Asset pricing is working through its own replication crisis:
  most published factors do not survive out of sample. Durable: no-arbitrage,
  discounting, diversification, maturity transformation, Bagehot's rule.
  Fashion: whichever factor or asset class is currently being sold as
  uncorrelated. Stale when you cannot describe how a trade in your own market
  is actually executed and settled today, or say where leverage currently sits.
- *A lifetime practice:* keep a decision journal. Every investment or
  forecast: the thesis, the price, the time horizon, and what would prove you
  wrong — written before the outcome. Audit it every January for forty years.
  This single habit is worth more than any book here, and it is the only
  reliable defence against hindsight. Alongside it, read one company's annual
  report every year for decades and watch a business actually change.
- *Rabbit holes:* Long-Term Capital Management in 1998, a Nobel-decorated
  model meeting liquidity; the 1907 panic and how it produced a central bank;
  the accidental birth of the Eurodollar market; the 2010 flash crash minute by
  minute; Japan's 1989 peak and the arithmetic of a thirty-year drawdown.

## Geography & geopolitics

**The question it asks.** How does location — terrain, climate, distance,
adjacency, resources — shape what people do, and how much of that is
constraint versus choice? Geopolitics narrows the question to states: given
where you are, what strategies are cheap and which are expensive? The
discipline's permanent tension is explaining spatial patterns without sliding
into determinism.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Space is not a backdrop. Distance has a price, and it shows up in gravity
  models of trade and migration with remarkable regularity.
- Site vs. situation: a place's own attributes vs. its relative position. Most
  cities are explained by the second.
- Agglomeration and increasing returns — economic activity clusters because
  it clusters (Krugman's new economic geography).
- Scale matters: results change with the unit of analysis. The modifiable
  areal unit problem and the ecological fallacy are the two errors every map
  can commit.
- Place vs. space: space is produced socially, not just occupied (Lefebvre,
  Harvey, Massey).
- The physical drivers: latitude, monsoon, watersheds, soils, orography,
  ocean currents — learn enough to read a physical map as an argument.
- Chokepoints and the sea-power/land-power distinction: Mackinder's Heartland,
  Mahan's sea power, Spykman's Rimland. All three are frameworks, not
  predictions.
- Borders are made, not found; states must make territory legible to govern it
  (Scott's *Seeing Like a State*).
- Resource geography: the "resource curse" is institutional, not geological —
  the same oil produces Norway and Equatorial Guinea.
- GIS and remote sensing are the discipline's technical core. Doing geography
  means making maps.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Confusing geography with capital-city memorization. It's spatial
  explanation, and it's quantitative.
- Environmental determinism, the field's oldest failure mode. *Guns, Germs,
  and Steel* is the famous case: most geographers and historians consider it
  determinist and under-evidenced. Read James Blaut's *Eight Eurocentric
  Historians* and Pomeranz as correctives. *Prisoners of Geography*
  (Marshall) is useful mental furniture and overclaims badly — treat it as
  journalism, not scholarship.
- Expecting geopolitics to predict. It tells you which constraints bind, not
  what leaders will choose.

**T3 — Literacy (~200 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State*** — the door because
  it shows you the state's need to flatten complicated space into something
  countable, and that single idea explains cadastral maps, surnames, city
  grids, forestry, and collectivization all at once.
- *Survey:* Harm de Blij, *Why Geography Matters* (or *The Power of Place*)
  for human geography; Robert Christopherson's *Geosystems* for the physical
  half, which most readers skip and shouldn't — you cannot reason about
  climate, agriculture, or water without it.
- *Canon:* Halford Mackinder, "The Geographical Pivot of History" (1904) —
  twenty pages, free, and the origin of the whole geopolitical vocabulary;
  Alfred Thayer Mahan, *The Influence of Sea Power upon History*
  (introduction and chapter 1 only); Nicholas Spykman, *America's Strategy in
  World Politics*; Carl Sauer, "The Morphology of Landscape" (1925); David
  Harvey, *The Condition of Postmodernity* (Part III, on time-space
  compression).
- *Course:* there is no dominant free flagship. The highest-value substitute
  is to learn the tool: QGIS is free and its own documentation and tutorials
  are good, and UC Davis's GIS specialization on Coursera is solid if you
  want structure. Our World in Data and the CIA *World Factbook* are free
  reference infrastructure worth knowing exists.
- *Artifact:* pick one strait, basin, or borderland — Malacca, Hormuz, the
  Nile, the North European Plain, the Taiwan Strait — and write 2,000 words
  on what geography constrains there, what it demonstrably does *not*
  constrain, and how technology has changed the constraint over a century.
  Include a map you made yourself in QGIS, with a defensible projection.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Knox & Marston, *Human Geography: Places and Regions in Global
  Context*; Longley, Goodchild, Maguire & Rhind, *Geographic Information
  Science and Systems* (the GIS standard); Fujita, Krugman & Venables, *The
  Spatial Economy*; Colin Flint, *Introduction to Geopolitics*.
- *Practice:* QGIS or ArcGIS most days — projections and what they distort,
  joins, spatial statistics, choropleths that don't lie about their
  classification breaks. Work real data: OpenStreetMap, Natural Earth,
  Landsat and Sentinel imagery, WorldPop, GADM. Do a supervised land-cover
  classification. Learn PostGIS. Learn spatial autocorrelation (Moran's I)
  and spatial regression, because ordinary regression on spatial data is
  usually wrong. Then do fieldwork: walk a transect, do a land-use survey.
- *Primary literature:* *Annals of the American Association of Geographers*,
  *Progress in Human Geography*, *Political Geography*, *Journal of Economic
  Geography*, *Geopolitics*. Landmarks: Krugman, "Increasing Returns and
  Economic Geography" (JPE, 1991); Doreen Massey, *For Space*; Gerard Toal
  (Gearóid Ó Tuathail), *Critical Geopolitics*.
- *You've arrived when:* you can take raw spatial data, justify your
  projection and unit of analysis, run an appropriate spatial model, and
  produce a map a specialist can't fault for MAUP, classification abuse, or a
  misleading color ramp.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* David Harvey, *The Limits to Capital*; Doreen
  Massey, *Spatial Divisions of Labour*; Peter Taylor & Colin Flint,
  *Political Geography*; Luc Anselin's spatial econometrics papers; Michael
  Goodchild on GIScience foundations.
- *Frontier:* critical geopolitics (geopolitics as discourse rather than
  terrain); geospatial machine learning and satellite-derived economic
  measurement (nightlights, crop yields, poverty mapping — the
  Burke/Lobell/Ermon line of work); climate migration and adaptation; supply
  chain and chokepoint mapping after 2020; Arctic and seabed jurisdiction.
- *Contribution looks like:* measuring something spatially that nobody could
  measure before; a theoretical account of a spatial process; or regional
  expertise deep enough that people call you before acting.
- *Community:* American Association of Geographers (the AAG annual meeting),
  Royal Geographical Society, International Geographical Union; for the
  geopolitics side, IISS, CSIS, and the war college journals.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* **Urban geography and planning** — Peter Hall, *Cities of
  Tomorrow*; owns why cities take the shapes they do and who decided.
  **Political ecology** — Blaikie & Brookfield, *Land Degradation and
  Society*, and Michael Watts, *Silent Violence*; owns who bears environmental
  harm and why it is never evenly distributed. **Water and climate as
  constraints** — Peter Gleick's *The World's Water* volumes; owns the two
  variables that bind hardest and soonest. **Remote sensing** — Lillesand,
  Kiefer & Chipman, *Remote Sensing and Image Interpretation*; owns measuring
  the surface of the earth without touching it. **Cartography and its politics** — J.B. Harley,
  "Deconstructing the Map" (1989), with Mark Monmonier's *How to Lie with
  Maps*; owns the fact that every map is an argument. **Migration and
  population geography** — owns who moves, who cannot, and what borders
  actually do. **Military geography and strategy** — the war college and IISS
  literature; owns terrain as a budget constraint on force. **One region,
  cold** — physical, political, and economic geography of a single area until
  people ask you about it.
- *The long canon:* Alexander von Humboldt — start with Andrea Wulf's *The
  Invention of Nature*, then his own *Personal Narrative*; William Cronon,
  *Nature's Metropolis*, arguably the finest work of economic geography in
  English; Jane Jacobs, *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*; Yi-Fu
  Tuan, *Space and Place* and *Topophilia*, the humanist tradition and the
  most quietly original geographer of the century; Jean Gottmann,
  *Megalopolis*; David Harvey, *Social Justice and the City*; Saskia Sassen,
  *The Global City*; Mike Davis, *City of Quartz* and *Late Victorian
  Holocausts* — the latter a fierce and contested account of famine as policy
  plus climate; Pomeranz & Topik, *The World That Trade Created*, for
  geography as commerce; a serious world atlas (the Times comprehensive, or
  your national atlas) treated as a book you read rather than consult. Read
  Ellen Churchill Semple and Ellsworth Huntington once, deliberately, as the
  cautionary case of where determinism leads. Robert Kaplan's *The Revenge of
  Geography* is readable journalism that overclaims in the same direction —
  useful only if you already have the critiques.
- *Re-foundation watch:* the data revolution here is not finished. Free
  near-daily satellite imagery plus machine learning now measures poverty,
  crop yields, emissions, deforestation, and shipping in places that publish no
  statistics — expect a decade in which arguments that were rhetorical become
  empirical. Climate attribution science is maturing to the point of settling
  causal claims about individual events; downscaled projections and sea-level
  ranges will be revised more than once. Watch the Arctic and seabed
  jurisdictional regimes, satellite navigation jamming as a routine tool, and
  supply chain remapping. Durable: distance costs, site versus situation,
  agglomeration, MAUP, the institutional reading of the resource curse.
  Fashion: whichever chokepoint is currently in the headlines. Stale when your
  mental map of energy flows, shipping routes, or a region's borders is a
  decade old.
- *A lifetime practice:* adopt one basin, strait, or borderland for life — its
  hydrology, treaties, traffic, disputes, and settlements — and keep a file.
  Thirty years of watching one river negotiate its way through three states
  teaches more than any comparative survey. In parallel, map your own locality
  repeatedly across decades: walk it, survey land use, contribute to
  OpenStreetMap, and redraw one map a year from primary data.
- *Rabbit holes:* the Radcliffe Line — a boundary drawn in five weeks by a man
  who had never been to India; the Aral Sea's disappearance as a planned
  outcome; the Nile Basin negotiations and the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance
  Dam; the Darién Gap, the one place the Pan-American Highway does not go; the
  Hanseatic League's geography of trust and warehouses.

## Education (how humans learn, how systems teach)

**The question it asks.** What actually causes durable learning in a human
mind — and why do the institutions built to produce it so reliably produce
something else, namely sorting and credentialing? These are two half-fields
with different methods: the cognitive science of learning, which replicates
well, and the political economy of schooling, which mostly doesn't.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Retrieval practice (the testing effect), spacing, and interleaving. These
  are the strongest, most replicated findings in the entire field, and they
  are the operating system of this whole repo.
- Desirable difficulties (Bjork): conditions that slow learning during
  practice often improve retention. Ease during study is a bad signal.
- Cognitive load theory (Sweller): working memory is tiny; worked examples
  beat problem-solving for novices, and the advantage reverses with expertise
  (the expertise reversal effect).
- Transfer is rare and hard. Most "critical thinking" turns out to be domain
  knowledge wearing a disguise (Willingham).
- Learning styles are not real, and the myth's survival is itself the field's
  best case study in research failing to reach practice.
- Bloom's 2-sigma problem: one-to-one tutoring plus mastery learning produced
  enormous gains, and nobody has scaled it. It is the benchmark every
  edtech claim should be measured against.
- Zone of proximal development and scaffolding (Vygotsky); and the genuinely
  unsettled fight between discovery/constructivist approaches and explicit
  direct instruction.
- Motivation: self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan) — autonomy, competence,
  relatedness. Mindset effects are real but far smaller than popularized.
- Systems: human capital vs. signaling as competing accounts of what schooling
  buys; teacher effects are the largest measured in-school effect; assessment
  becomes invalid the moment it becomes a target (Goodhart).

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Trusting the feeling of learning. Rereading and highlighting feel
  productive and do almost nothing; retrieval feels like failing and works.
- Assuming nothing in education is known. The cognitive findings are solid;
  it's the system-level interventions that shrink or vanish at scale.
- Blaming policy failure entirely on funding or teacher quality — the
  measurement, incentive, and selection problems are usually deeper.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **Brown, Roediger & McDaniel, *Make It Stick*** — the door
  because it converts a large empirical literature into things you can do
  this week, and it will immediately improve your work in every other domain
  in this file. Read it before anything else in the repo, honestly.
- *Survey:* **Daniel Willingham, *Why Don't Students Like School?*** — short,
  scrupulous about what the evidence does and doesn't support. Then Daisy
  Christodoulou, *Seven Myths About Education*, for the systematic demolition
  of what schools believe without evidence.
- *Canon:* John Dewey, *Democracy and Education* (chapters 1–4 and 11) and
  *Experience and Education* — the second is short and is Dewey correcting
  the progressives who claimed him; Paulo Freire, *Pedagogy of the Oppressed*
  (chapters 1–2, the banking model of education); Vygotsky, *Mind in Society*
  (chapter 6 on the zone of proximal development); Benjamin Bloom, "The 2
  Sigma Problem" (1984, free, fifteen pages).
- *Course:* **Barbara Oakley & Terrence Sejnowski, *Learning How to Learn***
  (Coursera, free) — about fifteen hours and genuinely worth them; the most
  enrolled course in the world for defensible reasons. If you intend to
  teach, add Oakley's *Uncommon Sense Teaching*.
- *Artifact:* run the experiment on yourself. Take a body of material you
  need anyway, split it, learn half by rereading and half by spaced retrieval,
  test cold at four and eight weeks, and write 1,500 words reporting your
  actual numbers plus an honest section on what your design cannot rule out.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Richard Mayer, *Multimedia Learning*; Sweller, Ayres & Kalyuga,
  *Cognitive Load Theory*; John Hattie, *Visible Learning* — use it as an
  index of effect sizes and read the methodological critiques of
  meta-meta-analysis right alongside it, or you will over-trust it. For the
  policy half, Hanushek & Woessmann, *The Knowledge Capital of Nations*.
- *Practice:* Teach, and instrument it. A class, a tutoring load, a workshop
  series — with pre/post assessments you wrote, item analysis, and a taxonomy
  of the errors your learners actually make. Write assessments and check their
  reliability. Run a small within-subject or randomized study on your own
  learners, with consent. Analyze public data: NAEP, PISA, TIMSS, and any
  state longitudinal system you can access.
- *Primary literature:* *Journal of Educational Psychology*, *Educational
  Researcher*, *Review of Educational Research*, *Learning and Instruction*,
  *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis*. Landmarks: Roediger &
  Karpicke, "Test-Enhanced Learning" (2006); Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, "Why
  Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work" (2006); Bloom (1984);
  and Dunlosky et al., "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning
  Techniques" (2013) — free, and the single best review in the field.
- *You've arrived when:* you can design a curriculum with stated objectives,
  aligned assessments, and an explicit spacing/retrieval schedule, then
  demonstrate measured gains against a baseline you defined *in advance*.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* R. Keith Sawyer (ed.), *The Cambridge Handbook
  of the Learning Sciences*; the APA *Handbook of Educational Psychology*;
  Ericsson et al., *The Cambridge Handbook of Expertise and Expert
  Performance*; Shadish, Cook & Campbell, *Experimental and
  Quasi-Experimental Designs*; item response theory (de Ayala) if you touch
  measurement at all.
- *Frontier:* learning analytics and intelligent tutoring systems; large
  language models as tutors and whether they finally close Bloom's 2-sigma
  gap; the scale-up problem (why efficacy trials shrink when they grow);
  learning engineering as a discipline; the science-of-reading policy shift;
  education in low-income settings (J-PAL and RISE evidence).
- *Contribution looks like:* an instructional effect that replicates in
  someone else's classroom, a validated assessment instrument, or a scaled
  intervention with credible causal evidence — the last being the rarest.
- *Community:* AERA (its SIGs are where the real subfields meet),
  International Society of the Learning Sciences, Cognitive Science Society,
  EARLI in Europe.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* **Psychometrics and measurement** — item response theory,
  reliability, validity, differential item functioning; owns the question of
  what a score actually means, and almost every education argument is secretly
  a measurement argument. **Economics of education** — Hanushek & Woessmann,
  plus Chetty and co-authors on teacher value-added; owns what schooling buys
  and for whom. **Curriculum and instructional design** — Wiggins & McTighe,
  *Understanding by Design*, and the Direct Instruction literature; owns
  turning objectives into sequences. **Comparative and international
  education** — Lant Pritchett, *The Rebirth of Education* ("schooling ain't
  learning"), plus PISA and the RISE programme; owns the fact that most of the
  world's classrooms look nothing like the ones the research is done in.
  **Higher education** — Clark Kerr, *The Uses of the University*; owns what
  universities are for once research, teaching, and credentialing pull apart.
  **Expertise and deliberate practice** — Ericsson, read with the Macnamara,
  Hambrick & Oswald meta-analysis showing practice explains far less variance
  than the popular version claims. **Philosophy and history of education** —
  David Labaree, *Someone Has to Fail*; owns why systems built for learning
  produce sorting instead. **Adult and vocational learning** — the least
  researched and, at seventy years, the most relevant to you.
- *The long canon:* Rousseau, *Emile*, as the source of most progressive
  intuitions, including the wrong ones; Maria Montessori, *The Montessori
  Method*; Jerome Bruner, *The Process of Education* and *Toward a Theory of
  Instruction*; Piaget, read alongside the modern verdict that the broad
  insight held and the stage theory did not; Ivan Illich, *Deschooling
  Society*, the strongest case against the whole apparatus; Bowles & Gintis,
  *Schooling in Capitalist America*, for the reproduction argument; Stigler &
  Hiebert, *The Teaching Gap*, on Japanese lesson study and what a profession
  that improves itself looks like; Lisa Delpit, *Other People's Children*;
  Doug Lemov, *Teach Like a Champion*, valuable as craft and explicitly not
  causal evidence; India's annual ASER reports as a primary-source series on
  learning in a low-income system; NAEP long-term trend data for the same
  reason. E.D. Hirsch's *Cultural Literacy* and Bryan Caplan's *The Case
  Against Education* are both serious and both contested — read Hirsch with
  his critics on whose culture is being listed, and Caplan with the human
  capital economists who think his signalling share is far too high.
- *Re-foundation watch:* language models as tutors are the largest live test of
  Bloom's two-sigma benchmark, and you should get an answer within fifteen
  years; expect it to be real but smaller than promised, and watch for whether
  gains survive outside the developers' own trials. Assessment is being
  redesigned because unsupervised written work can no longer be verified.
  Education psychology has already run its replication reckoning — learning
  styles gone, growth mindset and grit shrunk hard — and the surviving core is
  small, which is why it is trustworthy. Durable: retrieval, spacing,
  interleaving, cognitive load, the weakness of transfer. Fashion: every
  branded intervention with a book deal. Stale when you quote an effect size
  from a popular book without checking the meta-analysis, or cite Hattie
  without the methodological critiques.
- *A lifetime practice:* teach someone, continuously, for the rest of your
  life — a class, a tutee, an apprentice, a study group — and keep an error
  log of the mistakes learners actually make in each subject. Over decades
  that log becomes a real research asset and the single best diagnostic of
  your own understanding. Separately, run this repo's own retrieval schedule
  on yourself and keep the data; seventy years of personal learning records is
  a study nobody has ever run.
- *Rabbit holes:* Bloom's original two-sigma studies and the dissertations
  behind them; the Perry Preschool and Abecedarian cohorts followed past age
  forty; the reading wars, from whole language to phonics and back; Sugata
  Mitra's "hole in the wall" and its careful debunking; the monitorial schools
  of Bell and Lancaster, an industrial answer to tutoring at scale in 1800.

## Media & communication

**The question it asks.** How do the form and the economics of a medium shape
what a society is able to know, believe, and coordinate on? The field
insists on separating three things people usually blur: the message, the
medium that carries it, and the market that pays for it.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- The medium is the message (McLuhan): the form of a communication
  technology reshapes perception and social organization independently of any
  content it carries.
- Agenda setting, framing, and priming: media rarely change what you think;
  they reliably change what you think *about* and which considerations feel
  relevant (McCombs & Shaw; Entman).
- The two-step flow (Katz & Lazarsfeld) — influence runs through opinion
  leaders, which killed the hypodermic-needle model.
- Cultivation (Gerbner) and the spiral of silence (Noelle-Neumann): long
  exposure shifts perceived reality; perceived minority status silences.
- The propaganda model (Herman & Chomsky): ownership, advertising, sourcing,
  flak, and ideology as structural filters. Contested as a total theory; the
  ownership-and-advertiser mechanism is well documented.
- The public sphere (Habermas) as an ideal type — useful precisely because
  real media never match it.
- Attention is the scarce good, advertising is the business model that
  shapes editorial, and the collapse of that model is why local news
  disappeared.
- Encoding/decoding (Stuart Hall): audiences decode texts in dominant,
  negotiated, or oppositional ways. Reception is not reception of intent.
- Media effects are typically small, conditional, and heterogeneous. This is
  the field's most robust and least popular finding.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Assuming large direct persuasion effects. The best-identified evidence —
  Kalla & Broockman on campaign contact, for instance — finds persuasion
  effects near zero in general elections. Media matter more through agenda,
  framing, and mobilization than conversion.
- Treating "the media" as one actor rather than a competitive industry with
  incentives, deadlines, and cost structures.
- Confusing media criticism with media research. McLuhan and Postman are
  essayists; you need the empirical literature too, and vice versa.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **Neil Postman, *Amusing Ourselves to Death*** — the door
  because it takes McLuhan's aphorism and turns it into a concrete,
  arguable claim about what television did to public discourse. Short, and
  its argument transfers to every medium since.
- *Survey:* **McQuail's *Mass Communication Theory*** (now with Deuze) — dry
  but complete, and it gives you the whole theoretical map with names
  attached. Curran & Seaton's *Power Without Responsibility* is the livelier
  alternative if you want media history and political economy together.
- *Canon:* Walter Lippmann, *Public Opinion* (1922) — read Part I, especially
  "The World Outside and the Pictures in Our Heads," which anticipated most
  of the field; McLuhan, *Understanding Media* (chapters 1–4; past that it
  becomes aphorism); Herman & Chomsky, *Manufacturing Consent* (chapter 1 for
  the model, then skim the cases); Stuart Hall, "Encoding/Decoding" (1973
  essay); Habermas, *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere*
  (introduction and chapter 6).
- *Course:* no open flagship here is worth the hours — the offerings are
  either media-literacy tips or unrigorous surveys. Read Lippmann and Postman
  and then do the artifact instead; the doing is the course.
- *Artifact:* a real content analysis. Pick one event, sample coverage from
  six outlets across at least two countries, build an explicit coding scheme,
  code the sample, re-code a subset two weeks later to measure your own
  agreement with yourself, and write 1,500 words on what varied, what didn't,
  and what your coding scheme couldn't see.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* McQuail above, read properly; Klaus Krippendorff, *Content
  Analysis: An Introduction to Its Methodology* (the standard); Shanto
  Iyengar, *Media Politics: A Citizen's Guide*, for the political
  communication core.
- *Practice:* Content analysis at scale — build a corpus, hand-code a sample,
  train a classifier, and validate it against human coders rather than
  against itself. Run framing experiments on a survey platform. Use the free
  open corpora: Media Cloud, GDELT, the Internet Archive's TV News Archive.
  Do reception interviews, because effects research without audiences becomes
  guesswork. And produce media yourself — write, edit, shoot, publish — since
  the constraints of the form are invisible until you've hit them.
- *Primary literature:* *Journal of Communication*, *Communication Research*,
  *Political Communication*, *New Media & Society*, *Public Opinion
  Quarterly*. Landmarks: McCombs & Shaw, "The Agenda-Setting Function of Mass
  Media" (POQ, 1972); Entman, "Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured
  Paradigm" (1993); Katz & Lazarsfeld, *Personal Influence*; Bakshy, Messing
  & Adamic on ideological exposure on Facebook (*Science*, 2015).
- *You've arrived when:* you can design and execute a study that measures a
  media effect against a credible counterfactual — and then report accurately
  how small it is, which is the part most people fail.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Manuel Castells, *The Rise of the Network
  Society* and *Communication Power*; Bourdieu, *On Television*; *The Oxford
  Handbook of Political Communication*; Grimmer, Roberts & Stewart, *Text as
  Data* — the computational standard shared with political science.
- *Frontier:* computational communication science; platform governance and
  content moderation research, currently in crisis because platforms have
  closed the APIs the field depended on — data access *is* the frontier
  problem; generative AI and synthetic media; misinformation research and its
  own replication disputes (see Chris Bail, *Breaking the Social Media
  Prism*, for how weak the echo-chamber story turned out to be); algorithmic
  auditing.
- *Contribution looks like:* causal evidence on a media effect, a new way to
  measure an information environment at scale, or theory that survives the
  move from broadcast to platform — most inherited theory does not.
- *Community:* International Communication Association (ICA), National
  Communication Association, AEJMC, Association of Internet Researchers
  (AoIR); ICA's annual conference is the center of gravity.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* **Comparative media systems** — Hallin & Mancini, *Comparing
  Media Systems*, then *Comparing Media Systems Beyond the Western World*;
  owns the fact that press-state relations differ structurally by country and
  most theory was built on two of them. **Media economics** — owns who pays,
  and therefore what gets made; the collapse of classified advertising
  explains more about journalism than any editorial argument. **Journalism
  studies and practice** — Kovach & Rosenstiel, *The Elements of Journalism*;
  owns what makes a claim publishable and who verifies it. **Media history** —
  Elizabeth Eisenstein, *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change*, with
  Adrian Johns' *The Nature of the Book* as the standing corrective; owns
  whether technologies cause social change or are recruited by it. **Platform
  governance** — Tarleton Gillespie, *Custodians of the Internet*; owns who
  decides what speech exists. **Diffusion and networks** — Duncan Watts and
  Sinan Aral, *The Hype Machine*; owns how information actually travels, which
  is rarely how people assume. **Advertising and public relations** — Edward
  Bernays' *Propaganda* as a primary source, Stuart Ewen's *PR!* as the
  history. **Audience and reception research** — Janice Radway, *Reading the
  Romance*, and Henry Jenkins on participatory culture.
- *The long canon:* Harold Innis, *Empire and Communications* and *The Bias of
  Communication* — McLuhan's teacher, harder and deeper; Raymond Williams,
  *Television: Technology and Cultural Form*, the direct rebuttal to
  technological determinism; Walter Lippmann, *The Phantom Public*, bleaker
  than *Public Opinion* and worth it; Jacques Ellul, *Propaganda*; James
  Carey, *Communication as Culture*; Benedict Anderson, *Imagined
  Communities*, on print capitalism inventing the nation; Todd Gitlin, *The
  Whole World Is Watching*; Tim Wu, *The Master Switch*, on the cycle every
  information industry runs; Yochai Benkler, *The Wealth of Networks*, and
  Benkler, Faris & Roberts, *Network Propaganda*, whose asymmetry finding is
  the most important empirical result of the last decade here; Zeynep Tufekci,
  *Twitter and Tear Gas* (free online), on protest movements outside the West;
  Daya Thussu, *International Communication*, for the global flows literature;
  the Reuters Institute *Digital News Report*, free and annual, read as a
  long series rather than a snapshot. Shoshana Zuboff's *The Age of
  Surveillance Capitalism* is influential and criticized by scholars for
  overclaiming its mechanism — read Benkler's and Morozov's responses with it.
- *Re-foundation watch:* the binding constraint on this field is data access.
  Platforms closed the APIs the discipline was built on, and the replacement
  is regulatory — the EU Digital Services Act's researcher-access provisions
  are the thing to watch, because whether they work determines what the field
  can know. The 2023 Facebook–Instagram election studies in *Science* and
  *Nature* are the template for platform-researcher collaboration and the
  argument about its independence is instructive. Misinformation research has
  had its own correction: exposure turns out to be small and heavily
  concentrated in a few heavy users, and the echo-chamber story is much weaker
  than the popular version (Bail, *Breaking the Social Media Prism*; the
  Guess, Nyhan and Reifler line of work). Durable: agenda setting, framing,
  two-step flow, small conditional effects. Fashion: each new medium's moral
  panic. Stale when you argue from filter bubbles as established, or when your
  model of how people encounter news is more than five years old.
- *A lifetime practice:* keep a media diary. The same week each year, log
  everything you read, watch, and hear, with sources and durations, and
  archive a sample of the actual content. Do it for forty years and you own a
  longitudinal record of one information environment that nobody else has.
  Alternatively, read one newspaper cover to cover every day for life,
  preferably in a language and country not your own — it is the cheapest
  possible immersion in another public sphere.
- *Rabbit holes:* the *War of the Worlds* panic that mostly did not happen
  (Pooley & Socolow's debunking, and why the myth persists); the Payne Fund
  studies, the first mass-media effects research and still surprisingly good;
  Radio Rwanda and the incitement literature, including Yanagizawa-Drott's
  estimate of radio's causal contribution to the genocide; Brazilian
  telenovelas and fertility decline (La Ferrara and co-authors); the Associated
  Press wire and how objectivity started as a business decision.
