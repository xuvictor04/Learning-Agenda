# Modes — The Full Range of Sources

The domain files are book-heavy on purpose. Books are dense, cheap, portable,
indexed, and they hold an argument long enough to develop it — no other
medium does all five. They are the correct default.

They are also one channel, with structural weaknesses. A book cannot show
motion, so fluid dynamics, a lathe cutting, and a conductor's beat all arrive
degraded. It cannot answer back, so your misunderstanding survives contact
with it. It cannot put a thing in your hands, and some knowledge — the weight
of a trilobite, the real size of *Las Meninas*, how a spring feels at yield —
only transfers physically. And it is stale: a year to write, a year to
publish.

Every domain in `../02-map.md` has modes that beat books for particular
purposes. You cannot read your way to a language, a craft, a diagnosis, or a
telescope.

**The design rule: for any domain, use at least three different modes.** Not
because variety is stimulating, but because each mode fails differently.
Books make you feel you understand mechanisms you couldn't operate; video,
results you couldn't derive; doing gives skill without theory. Where two
modes disagree, or where one makes you notice what another skipped, is where
understanding forms. Three modes is triangulation, not garnish.

## 1. Lecture courses (recorded)

**Good for.** Structure, and a teacher's ordering instinct. A good lecturer
has taught the subject twenty times and knows which idea must come third or
nothing later lands, and that sequencing is expensive knowledge given away.

**Fails at.** Feedback, entirely. Nothing checks whether you understood, and
video is optimised to feel comprehensible. Completion rates for open online
courses run in single digits.

**How to use it.** Schedule it like an enrolled class — fixed hours, fixed
weeks — and do the problem sets, which are the only part that is really the
course. If you won't do the problem sets, don't start.

**Where the good ones are.** MIT OpenCourseWare (`ocw.mit.edu`) — problem
sets and exams with solutions, which is what separates it. Open Yale Courses
(`oyc.yale.edu`) — filmed complete semesters, strongest in humanities and
social science. Stanford's *Theoretical Minimum* (Susskind) for physics, and
Stanford's posted machine learning and NLP series. NPTEL (`nptel.ac.in`) for
engineering — thousands of IIT courses, the deepest free catalogue anywhere.
On the platforms, take university flagships over certificate mills: Harvard's
CS50, Sandel's *Justice*, core-econ.org's *The Economy*, Oakley's *Learning
How to Learn*.

## 2. Live courses and classes

**Good for.** The two things recorded lectures cannot give: a human who tells
you that you are wrong, and a deadline. That is the whole argument, and it is
enough.

**Fails at.** Pace and coverage — one speed, chosen for the median student.

**How to use it.** Take live classes for what you keep failing to start
alone, and for anything where technique goes wrong invisibly: languages,
drawing, welding, statistics, an instrument, field ID. Ask about auditing: at
community colleges and most extension schools it costs a fraction of tuition
or nothing, and sitting in on a university course is frequently permitted if
you ask the instructor directly.

**Where the good ones are.** Community colleges, the most underrated
institutions in adult education. University extension and continuing
education — Harvard Extension School, UC Berkeley Extension, the Oxford
Department for Continuing Education, Birkbeck's evening degrees. The Workers'
Educational Association in the UK, Osher Lifelong Learning Institutes at over
a hundred US universities, Nordic folk high schools. Field and summer schools
are the concentrated form: Marine Biological Laboratory courses at Woods
Hole, Organization for Tropical Studies field courses, archaeological field
schools such as Crow Canyon's, and immersion language schools — Middlebury,
the Goethe-Institut, Instituto Cervantes, Alliance Française.

## 3. Primary documents and papers

**Good for.** Hearing the field's own voice rather than a populariser's
summary — the hedges, the error bars, the size of the actual claim. Most
famous results are shorter and stranger in the original.

**Fails at.** Orientation; a paper assumes the field. And the signature error
is mistaking one paper for a field's consensus — a single study is a data
point, not a finding, whatever the press release said.

**How to use it.** Read out of order: abstract, figures and tables, methods,
discussion — introduction last, because it is advertising. Ask what would
have had to come out differently for the conclusion to fail. Keshav's "How to
Read a Paper" (2007) formalises this and is two pages long.

**Where the good ones are.** arXiv and bioRxiv for preprints; PubMed Central
for open-access biomedicine; JSTOR, which a public library card often opens
free; HathiTrust and the Internet Archive for out-of-print scholarship;
Project Gutenberg for the out-of-copyright canon; the Perseus Digital Library
for classics in the original; Yale's Avalon Project for diplomatic and legal
documents. For the state's own voice: the US and UK National Archives,
national statistical yearbooks, the IPCC Assessment Reports (read the
chapters, not the summary for policymakers), and court opinions via
`supremecourt.gov` or CourtListener — free, self-explaining, and the best
available training in reading argument.

## 4. Data and datasets

**Good for.** Learning a field by handling the evidence it argues from. An
afternoon fitting a line to real, messy numbers teaches what ten book
chapters don't: how noisy the world is, how much rides on the choice of
denominator, what a finding looks like before it is written up.

**Fails at.** Interpretation. Data won't tell you which question is worth
asking, and a confident wrong answer is very fast to produce.

**How to use it.** Pick one question you can state in a sentence, get the
smallest dataset that could answer it, and write up what you found including
what went wrong — that write-up is an artifact. In quantitative fields do it
*before* the survey book; the book reads differently afterwards.

**Where the good ones are.** World Bank Open Data and Our World in Data for
development, health, and energy. FRED (St. Louis Fed) for macroeconomics —
hundreds of thousands of series, instantly plottable. The US Census, IPUMS,
and Eurostat for demography. NASA Earthdata and NOAA's National Centers for
Environmental Information for climate and oceans. SDSS SkyServer for
astronomy. GenBank and UniProt for sequence data, GBIF for biodiversity
records. `data.gov` and its national equivalents for anything municipal.
Kaggle for cleaned datasets with worked notebooks — a good training ground
and a misleading picture of real data.

## 5. Video and visual explainers

**Good for.** Motion, scale, and geometry — the three things prose handles
worst. A rotating manifold, a phase change, a bridge failing: minutes of
video, pages of unsuccessful sentences.

**Fails at.** Everything else, while feeling excellent. Watching is the most
passive mode here and gives the strongest illusion of learning; consumption
without an artifact counts for nothing. This is where people spend the most
hours for the least gain.

**How to use it.** Targeted, as a lookup for a specific thing you can't
picture — never as a feed. Afterwards, reproduce the central figure by hand
from memory, or you didn't watch it.

**Where the good ones are.** 3Blue1Brown for mathematical intuition, without
peer. Veritasium for physics and the sociology of science. Practical
Engineering for civil infrastructure, with real physical models. Applied
Science for lab instrumentation built from scratch. Technology Connections
for how ordinary machines work — the best existing argument that engineering
history is interesting. Primitive Technology for materials and process, no
narration at all. Numberphile for mathematicians talking. Smarthistory for
art history, written by art historians, free. Every Frame a Painting for film
form. Kurzgesagt is beautifully made and reliably oversimplifies — an
invitation to a subject, never a source.

## 6. Podcasts and long-form audio

**Good for.** Hours that were otherwise unusable — commuting, exercise,
chores — and for hearing practitioners think aloud, hedge, and change their
minds, which almost no written source shows.

**Fails at.** Anything with notation, equations, diagrams, or maps. Audio
also produces the most convincing pseudo-study here: a hundred hours feels
like a course and leaves almost nothing, because there is no retrieval and no
artifact.

**How to use it.** Treat podcasts as recruitment — they are excellent at
showing you which fields you want to study properly. When one matters, stop
and write three sentences, or admit it was entertainment.

**Where the good ones are.** *In Our Time* (BBC Radio 4) is the standout:
over a thousand episodes, three academics per topic, across history, science,
philosophy, and literature at a level nothing else broadcasts. Dan Carlin's
*Hardcore History* and Mike Duncan's *Revolutions* for narrative depth.
Stephen West's *Philosophize This* and Peter Adamson's *History of Philosophy
Without Any Gaps*. The *Nature Podcast* and *Science*'s podcast for weekly
research. EconTalk for economics as argument.
The *London Review of Books* podcast, and the New Books Network — academic
authors interviewed across essentially every field, the fastest way to hear
what a new scholarly book claims.

## 7. Museums, sites, and physical places

**Good for.** Reorganising understanding through the actual object. A real
specimen, the true scale of a painting, a beam engine turning — these fix
proportion, and proportion is what secondary sources lose. The most underused
mode on this list by a wide margin, and usually cheap or free.

**Fails at.** Systematic coverage. Museums are curated for visitors passing
through once, and their labels are thin.

**How to use it.** Go with a specific written question, and go repeatedly to
one place rather than once to many — ten visits to one natural history
museum, each with a question, beats a tour of ten. Take the free docent tour
— docents are often retired specialists — and ask about collections access
behind the scenes, which many institutions grant to amateurs.

**Where the good ones are.** Natural history museums (the Smithsonian, the
AMNH, the NHM in London). The Deutsches Museum in Munich and the Science
Museum in London, the two best in the world for how things work. Industrial
heritage: Ironbridge Gorge, Lowell National Historical Park, working mills
and mines. Botanical gardens (Kew, the Missouri Botanical Garden).
Planetariums and public observatory nights — Griffith Observatory, the Adler,
Mount Wilson. Factory tours, working farms, and archaeological sites visited
with the excavation report in hand.

## 8. People — teachers, mentors, practitioners, communities

**Good for.** Bandwidth. A practitioner answers the question you didn't know
how to ask, corrects in ten seconds an error a book would leave standing for
years, and tells you which parts of the literature the field quietly ignores.

**Fails at.** Scale and reliability. One person's experience is one sample,
and confident practitioners are wrong in patterned ways.

**How to use it.** Buy time deliberately — an hour a week of a tutor is the
highest-value purchase in most people's learning budget, and for languages it
is non-negotiable. italki and comparable marketplaces make conversation with
native speakers cheap and immediate; Wyzant-style services cover academic
subjects. Then join something local. Amateur societies are astonishingly
welcoming, chronically short of members under sixty, and will hand a newcomer
real equipment and real responsibility within a month: astronomy clubs,
mycological societies, historical and archaeological societies, birding
groups (Audubon chapters, the RSPB), amateur radio clubs, makerspaces, chess
clubs, native plant societies, model engineering societies. University
seminars and colloquia are very often open to anyone who walks in; the
schedule is on the department web page. The Royal Institution, Gresham
College, and Perimeter Institute post their public lectures free.

## 9. Doing the thing

**Good for.** Feedback — what principle 1 says makes practice count. The
best feedback of any mode here, because reality answers immediately and does
not flatter you.

**Fails at.** Breadth and theory. Doing teaches you your instance of the
problem; it won't tell you where that instance sits in the field.

**How to use it.** Get to the smallest real version of the work as early as
you can tolerate, and let the reading follow what it exposed. Expect to be
bad in public for a while; that is the price of the feedback.

**Where the good ones are.** Citizen science first, because the barrier is
zero: eBird and iNaturalist (both feeding real research datasets), Zooniverse,
Foldit for protein folding, the AAVSO for variable star photometry, Audubon's
Christmas Bird Count. Then volunteering with institutions — museum collections
and herbarium digitisation, archaeological dig seasons, trail crews (the
Student Conservation Association, the Appalachian Mountain Club), Cooperative
Extension's Master Naturalist and Master Gardener programmes, community
biology labs such as Genspace and BioCurious. Then certifications that are
also curricula: EMT-Basic, wilderness first responder, amateur radio
licensing, CERT emergency response. Serving as a poll worker or accredited
election observer teaches more civics in two days than a semester.

## 10. Conversation and teaching

**Good for.** Exposing the gaps reading conceals. You can read a chapter and
feel fluent; you cannot field a beginner's third follow-up unless you are.
This is principle 6 — teaching is the final exam — used as a source, not just
a test.

**Fails at.** Little — except that teaching too early transmits your errors
to someone else.

**How to use it.** Run or join a reading group with a fixed schedule and one
person responsible per session. Offer a short class at a public library,
makerspace, or community centre — they usually say yes, and the deadline does
the work. Answer questions where you can be corrected: Stack Exchange,
field-specific forums, Wikipedia editing, a blog with comments open. Being
publicly wrong is cheap; staying privately wrong is not.

## 11. Reference works and encyclopaedias

**Good for.** Orientation before you commit, and checking after. The right
article tells you in twenty minutes what a field's divisions are and what is
contested.

**Fails at.** Depth, argument, and retention. Reference prose is written to
be consulted, not read.

**How to use it.** Use one to decide what to study, and again when a term
recurs and you can't place it. Then leave, and mine the bibliographies —
that is the real value.

**Where the good ones are.** The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy is the
gold standard for the genre: free, peer-reviewed, maintained, and often the
best thing written on its topic. Oxford's Very Short Introductions run to
hundreds of volumes by specialists and are the fastest legitimate survey in
most fields. The Princeton Companions (to Mathematics, to Applied
Mathematics) are commissioned essays rather than entries, and unusually good.
Cambridge Companions for individual authors and philosophers, Oxford
Handbooks for the state of an academic subfield, Grove Music Online for
music, the DLMF for special functions. Wikipedia's real role is honest and
limited: excellent for orientation, excellent for citation-mining, uneven in
depth, never a source to cite.

## 12. AI as a mode

**Good for.** On-demand explanation at whatever level you ask for; Socratic
questioning, when you instruct it to question rather than answer; unlimited
practice problems; translating jargon and notation; and — the genuinely
distinctive one — finding the name of a concept you can only describe. That
last removes a class of dead end that used to cost weeks.

**Fails at.** Truth, and it fails invisibly. It produces the feeling of
understanding without the substance, in fluent, confident, well-organised
prose. An unverified answer from a model is a rumour. It is also happy to
write your artifact, which would make the artifact worthless as evidence —
its whole point is being unfakeable proof you did the work.

**How to use it.** Use it to accelerate your own thinking and to quiz
yourself — never to produce the artifact. Make it ask you questions. Have it
generate twenty problems and grade yourself against them. Have it name the
concept, then go read the reference. Verify anything you intend to repeat
against a source with an author and a date.

## 13. Fiction, film, and art about a field

**Good for.** Motivation, and for what a field feels like from inside — the
texture of the work, the politics of the institution. A real on-ramp, and
underrated; a great many scientists were recruited by a novel.

**Fails at.** Evidence, completely. Fiction is under no obligation to be
right and is usually obliged to be wrong, because drama needs what reality
lacks.

**Where the good ones are.** Primo Levi's *The Periodic Table* is the best
book ever written about what it is like to be a chemist. *The Wire* on how
urban institutions actually fail. Hilary Mantel's *Wolf Hall* for the texture
of Tudor politics. Stanisław Lem's *Solaris* for the limits of scientific
understanding. Use them to start, then go read the history.

## Building a mode mix for one domain

Take the T3 astronomy entry in `physical.md` — roughly 150 hours. Spent
entirely on books, that is three or four titles and a summary you half
believe. Spread across modes, the same hours look like this.

**Survey book** (~30 hrs). OpenStax *Astronomy 2e*, free, skimmed whole in a
month for the vocabulary.

**Recorded course** (~35 hrs). Open Yale ASTR 160, on a schedule, with the
arithmetic actually performed.

**Binoculars and a star chart, outdoors** (~20 hrs). Twenty nights. You learn
that the sky moves, that the Moon ruins everything, that magnitude is a
sensation and not a number — none of which transfers from a page.

**A local astronomy club** (~15 hrs). Six meetings and two star parties.
Someone shows you collimation, someone lends you a telescope worth more than
your car, someone stops you buying the wrong eyepiece.

**A real dataset** (~30 hrs). SDSS SkyServer: pull a few thousand galaxy
spectra, plot a colour–magnitude diagram, find the red sequence yourself.
This is the hour the textbook figure stops being a figure.

**A planetarium or observatory visit** (~5 hrs). One public observing night
at a real instrument, plus a show on a question you brought.

**The artifact** (~15 hrs). The 2,000 words the domain file asks for: how we
know the age, composition, and geometry of the universe, with the
measurements and error bars named.

Same 150 hours. But the club corrects errors the book left standing, the
dataset contradicts what the video implied, the eyepiece makes the diagram
mean something, and the artifact forces all six into one account that has to
hold together. Three books would have been easier and mostly gone in two
years. This survives, because it was built from sources that fail in
different directions.
