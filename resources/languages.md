# Languages

A language is not a T3 domain. There is no 200-hour version of speaking
Japanese; the literacy artifact for "French" would be a lie. Reaching real
use costs 1–3 years of near-daily contact — T2 by nature — so budget it
against the T2 cap, not the T3 ledger.

That also means this file is organized by **method**, not by tongue. The
method is the transferable asset. Learn it properly on your first language
and every later one is cheaper.

## The question it asks

Acquiring an adult second language is not the acquisition of facts about a
language. It is building an unconscious, real-time processing system: a
sound inventory your ear can segment, a lexicon retrievable in milliseconds,
and a grammar you apply without consulting it. You already did this once, as
a child, with no instruction and no metalinguistic vocabulary.

The adult case differs in three ways that matter for planning. Your first
language now interferes — its phoneme categories reshape what you can hear,
its syntax leaks into what you produce. You have far less raw exposure time
than a child's tens of thousands of hours. But you have literacy, abstract
reasoning, and the ability to run a deliberate system, which children do
not. The design question is how to spend a few hundred conscious hours to
substitute for a childhood.

## How adult acquisition actually works

**Comprehensible input is the engine.** Stephen Krashen's *Principles and
Practice in Second Language Acquisition* (1982, free on sdkrashen.com) is
the foundational statement: we acquire language by understanding messages
slightly beyond our current level — his "i+1" — not by studying rules.
Krashen's model is contested in its strong form, but its practical core is
uncontroversial: hours of understood language is the single best predictor
of progress. Everything else in this file exists to make more input
comprehensible sooner.

**Spaced repetition buys the lexicon.** Input alone delivers frequent words
efficiently and rare words slowly. An SRS closes that gap deliberately — the
same 15–20 minute habit principle 2 of this agenda already assumes.

**Pronunciation comes first, not last.** Gabriel Wyner's *Fluent Forever*
(2014) makes the strongest practical case: train the sound system before
building vocabulary on top of it, using minimal-pair drills until you can
*hear* contrasts your native language collapses. Sounds you cannot
distinguish, you cannot store, and a fossilized accent laid down over two
years is expensive to undo. Wyner is also the best source on card design —
images and target-language context over translation pairs.

**Output has a job, but it isn't the engine.** Merrill Swain's comprehensible
output hypothesis (1985) is the honest counterweight to Krashen: producing
language forces you to notice what you cannot yet say, which directs
attention on later input. Speak early — but treat speaking as a diagnostic
and a fluency drill, not as the source of new competence.

**The interface debate, and why you should care.** Krashen held that
consciously *learned* rules never become *acquired* competence — the
"non-interface" position. Weak-interface researchers (Rod Ellis, Nick Ellis,
Bill VanPatten) argue that explicit knowledge helps indirectly, by making
you notice a feature in input you'd otherwise filter out. Nobody credible
argues the strong version: that drilling a rule installs it. The planning
consequence: read a grammar explanation once, briefly, to prime noticing —
then go get input. Grammar exercises underperform because they train a
slow, conscious monitor that real-time speech has no time to consult.

**Vocabulary thresholds (Paul Nation's numbers).** Nation's coverage
research gives the most useful map of the road ahead. Roughly:

- **~2,000 word families** — about 80% coverage of running text. Enough for
  survival conversation and graded readers. Native material is still noise.
- **~3,000–5,000** — around 95% coverage; roughly one unknown word per 20.
  Native content becomes workable *with* a dictionary or subtitles. This is
  where most learners quit, and where the payoff starts.
- **~8,000–9,000 word families** — the ~98% coverage Nation identifies as the
  threshold for comfortable *unassisted* reading of novels and newspapers.
  Listening needs somewhat less, ~6,000–7,000.

Call it 2k to survive, 5k to function, 10k to be free. Educated native
adults sit well above that; you do not need to reach them.

## What outsiders get wrong

- **App streaks are not acquisition.** A 900-day gamified streak can coexist
  with an inability to hold a five-minute conversation, because tapping
  recognition exercises never trains retrieval, listening, or production.
  Measure hours of input and minutes of speech, never streaks.
- **"Immersion" without comprehension is noise.** Moving to a country or
  leaving the radio on does nothing by itself; unparsed audio is not input.
  Immersion works only where the material is at or just above your level,
  which is why graded readers and CI-style channels beat native news at the
  start.
- **Adults are not doomed.** The "critical period" claim is about ultimate
  native-like accent and intuition, not about learnability. Adults acquire
  vocabulary, morphology, and reading *faster* than children per hour of
  exposure; children win on eventual pronunciation and on sheer exposure
  budget. You will likely keep an accent. You can absolutely reach C1.

## Milestones: CEFR, and what the hours actually cost

The Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR) is the real, standard
ladder. Use it instead of "conversational" or "fluent," which mean nothing.

| Level | What it actually lets you do | Approx. cumulative hours* |
|-------|------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **A1** | Fixed phrases, introductions, simple needs | ~90–100 |
| **A2** | Routine exchanges on familiar topics; survive as a tourist | ~180–200 |
| **B1** | Handle most travel situations; describe experience and give reasons; follow clear standard speech | ~350–400 |
| **B2** | Real conversations with natives without strain on both sides; read a novel with effort; work in the language | ~500–600 |
| **C1** | Flexible, effective use for social, academic, professional purposes; implicit meaning; long complex texts | ~700–800 |
| **C2** | Effortless comprehension, precise expression of fine shades of meaning | ~1,000–1,200 |

\*Approximate guided-learning-hour estimates in wide use (Cambridge
English), for a native English speaker learning a closely related language.
Treat them as orders of magnitude, not promises. **B2 is the target that
changes your life**; C2 is a specialist's expense.

**The US Foreign Service Institute's category system** explains why those
hours multiply for some languages. FSI has taught diplomats for decades and
publishes how long its intensive courses take native English speakers to
reach roughly professional working proficiency (ILR 3, near CEFR B2/C1):

- **Category I (~600–750 class hours, 24–30 weeks):** Spanish, French,
  Italian, Portuguese, Romanian, Dutch, Danish, Norwegian, Swedish,
  Afrikaans.
- **Category II (~900 hours, 36 weeks):** German, Indonesian, Malay,
  Swahili, Haitian Creole.
- **Category III (~1,100 hours, 44 weeks):** the large middle — Russian,
  Polish, Czech, Greek, Hebrew, Hindi, Urdu, Persian, Turkish, Finnish,
  Hungarian, Thai, Vietnamese, Tagalog and many more.
- **Category IV (~2,200 hours, 88 weeks)** — sometimes numbered V in older
  charts: Arabic, Mandarin and Cantonese Chinese, Japanese, Korean.

Two caveats. These are *class* hours in a full-time program with homework on
top, so total time is higher. And the difficulty is **relative to the
learner's native language**, not absolute — the ladder is measuring distance
from English, and a Korean speaker's ladder for Japanese looks nothing like
this one. If your first language is not English, rebuild the estimate around
your own distance: shared script, shared vocabulary stock, shared word
order, and shared phoneme inventory are what actually set the price.

## The daily loop

An hour a day, every day, beats five hours on Saturday — this is
consolidation-bound, and principle 8 applies.

1. **SRS, 15–20 min.** Cards from your own encounters: sentences you met in
   real input, with the target word in context, an image where possible, and
   audio. Never a bare word-to-translation pair. Add 10–20 new cards a day
   early on; hold the queue where reviews stay under 20 minutes.
2. **Input, 30–45 min, non-negotiable.** Below B1: graded readers, learner
   podcasts, comprehensible-input video channels. B1–B2: native shows with
   target-language subtitles via Language Reactor, then novels you've already
   read in your first language. B2+: whatever you'd consume anyway, in the
   target language, permanently.
3. **Output, 2–3 sessions a week.** A paid tutor from month one or two
   (italki or a local equivalent) — one hour, in the language, with someone
   who will correct you. Between sessions: talk to yourself. Narrate your
   commute out loud. It feels absurd and it builds retrieval speed.
4. **Pronunciation, front-loaded.** Minimal-pair drills and shadowing daily
   for the first two months, then weekly maintenance and whenever a tutor
   flags something.
5. **Grammar, on demand only.** When a structure keeps appearing and you
   can't parse it, look it up, read the page, close the book, return to
   input.

## Tools that stay durable

- **Anki** — the SRS backbone; free, open, will outlive every app on this
  list. Your deck is an asset you own.
- **Graded readers** — the single most underused resource in language
  learning. Extensive reading at 98% known-word coverage is where fluency
  quietly gets built.
- **A human tutor** (italki, community classes, a conversation exchange) —
  the one irreplaceable item. Conversation is the feedback loop, and
  deliberate practice without feedback is just exposure.
- **Language Reactor / LingQ** — turn native video and text into parseable
  input with dual subtitles and one-click lookups feeding your SRS.
- **A grammar reference for the specific language** — to consult, not to
  study. Buy the one with an index, not the one with exercises.
- **Frequency lists** — the first 1,000–2,000 words by frequency are the
  highest-leverage vocabulary you will ever learn; get them early and
  deliberately rather than waiting for input to deliver them.

## Sequencing over the decades

**One language to real fluency first.** Depth beats collection, decisively.
Two languages at A2 is a party trick; one at B2 reads half a civilization's
canon in the original, changes what you can hear in a room, and gives you
the felt experience of a second conceptual system — which is the actual
prize, and the reason this belongs in a map of human knowledge at all.

**Later languages get cheaper.** Not because vocabulary transfers (usually
it doesn't) but because *method* does: you already know how to build cards,
what comprehensible input feels like, how to survive the A2–B1 plateau, and
that the plateau ends. The second language typically costs 60–70% of the
first at equal distance. Add one per decade if the pull persists; never run
two below B1 at once. For how far that actually goes over seven decades, and
for the maintenance bill that decides it, see **The seventy-year language
plan** at the end of this file.

## The classical languages

If your curiosity runs toward philosophy, literature, religion, or history,
a *reading* knowledge of Latin or Ancient Greek is a different and cheaper
proposition than a modern language — no listening, no speaking, no
production, one skill only. Two or three years of steady work gets you into
the primary sources.

- **Latin:** Hans Ørberg's *Lingua Latina per se Illustrata* — *Familia
  Romana* then *Roma Aeterna*. It teaches Latin entirely in Latin, by
  comprehensible input, with no English on the page; it is the closest thing
  the field has to magic. *Wheelock's Latin* is the grammar-translation
  alternative if you want explicit paradigms alongside it.
- **Ancient Greek:** *Athenaze* (Balme & Lawall), a reading-based course in
  the same spirit; Hansen & Quinn's *Greek: An Intensive Course* if you want
  the fast, brutal, morphology-first route. Expect Greek to cost roughly
  twice what Latin does.
- **Then read.** The Loeb Classical Library gives you facing-page text and
  translation for almost the entire canon; Perseus and the Dickinson College
  Commentaries are free and excellent. What it unlocks: Plato, Aristotle,
  Homer, Thucydides, the Greek New Testament, Vergil, Lucretius, Tacitus,
  Augustine, and the whole medieval and early-modern scholarly conversation
  that ran in Latin until roughly Newton — read directly, without a
  translator standing between you and the argument.

## The seventy-year language plan

Everything above is the method for one language. This section is what
changes when the horizon is seven decades rather than one or two — which
turns out to be mostly a question about maintenance, not acquisition.

**How many languages is honestly realistic.** Do the arithmetic with the
numbers already in this file. A Category I language to C1 costs roughly
700–1,000 hours all in; at an hour a day that is two and a half to three
years. A Category III language — Russian, Turkish, Hindi, Finnish — runs
closer to 1,500–2,000 hours, so five or six years. Category IV — Arabic,
Mandarin, Japanese, Korean — starts around 2,500 hours, call it seven to ten
years at an hour a day, and be honest that most people who begin one at that
pace stop at B1.

Seventy years at an hour a day is an enormous number of hours, and it is the
wrong number to reason from, because most of it is already spoken for. Every
language you have reached takes hours off the top *forever*, and this agenda
budgets ten focused hours a week across the whole of it, not ten hours of
language.

Against that: **four to six languages to genuine use — B2 or better — is
realistic** across seventy years for someone who learned the method properly
on the first one and rarely lets a decade pass without one running. **Eight
to ten is possible**, but only for someone whose primary commitment is
languages, who lives in more than one of them, and who accepts that the
forty-domain ledger runs thinner for it. That is a different life, not a
more ambitious version of this one. Choose it deliberately or don't drift
into it. And the rule from the section above only hardens with more time:
**depth in two beats tourist-level in eight.** Eight at A2 is a dinner-party
fact. Two at C1 is two literatures, two countries where you are not a
visitor, and two ways of cutting the world up.

**A staged sequence across the decades.** One concrete shape, to be adapted:

- **Decade 1 — one language to C1.** Chosen on pull, not prestige. This is
  where you buy the method, and the method is the asset.
- **Decades 2–3 — the expensive one.** A language from a different family,
  deliberately: a Category III or IV if you want one at all. Take the
  distance early. Working memory, travel freedom, and the appetite for a
  five-year project are all cheaper at 35 than at 65, and a distant language
  taken late will be the last big one you start.
- **Decades 3–4 — the classical languages, when the canon work pays.**
  Reading-only Latin or Greek slots naturally beside the philosophy,
  literature and religion work in `meaning-expression.md`; the payoff is
  concrete, so take them when you actually have primary sources you want.
- **Decades 4–6 — cheap neighbours and circumstance.** Fourth and fifth
  chosen on pull or on life: where you moved, whom you married, the archive
  you need, the fieldwork you're doing, the community on your street.

The logic underneath: **the second language in a family is cheap, the first
in a new family is not.** Spanish after French, Dutch after German,
Ukrainian after Russian, Portuguese after either Romance — shared
vocabulary, shared morphology, shared word order, sometimes half the cost.
So spend distance early and collect neighbours late, which is exactly
backwards from how most people do it.

**Maintenance is the real problem.** At seventy years this section matters
more than all the acquisition advice combined. A language reached and
abandoned decays, and it decays fastest in production: you will still read
it long after you can no longer say anything in it.

A working protocol per maintained language, at B2 or above:

- **SRS, 5–10 minutes a day** on that language's deck. Never delete a deck.
  A mature deck is a decade of your own encounters and cannot be rebought.
- **One hour of input a week, minimum.** Make it something you'd want
  anyway, or it won't survive a busy month.
- **One book and one show a month.** The book is the load-bearing half —
  extensive reading is what holds a lexicon in place.
- **One hour of conversation a month.** Below that, output degrades first
  and fastest, and you get the humiliating experience of understanding
  everything and producing nothing.
- **An annual conversation check.** One recorded hour, same month every
  year, with a tutor briefed to rate you against CEFR and say so plainly.
  Compare to last year's recording. It is the only honest instrument you
  have, and it costs one hour in twelve months.

Now budget it. Two hours a week per maintained language, three or four if
you're holding C1 output — **five maintained languages is ten hours a week,
which is this agenda's entire weekly budget.** That arithmetic, not
enthusiasm, sets your ceiling. The practical resolution is to triage: two or
three **active** languages (spoken, maintained), some **reading-only** ones
(an hour a week or less, a book every month or two — far cheaper, because
reading survives on recognition), and the rest **dormant** by decision
rather than by neglect.

Dormancy is survivable, and this is the genuinely encouraging finding.
Relearning is far faster than first learning — the savings effect Ebbinghaus
identified — and Harold Bahrick's fifty-year study of school Spanish found
that attrition is steep for the first few years and then largely flattens,
leaving a substantial residue stable for decades, with more surviving the
higher the original level reached. The planning consequence is sharp: **get
a language to B2 before you let it lapse.** Above that line there is a
residue to come back to and a few months of work to reactivate it. Below it
there is nothing to preserve.

**What each language actually opens.** Primary sources, first: historians,
philosophers and scientists read in their own words, with no translator's
choices standing between you and the argument. A literature in the original
— the Russian novel, German philosophy and Lieder, the French essay,
classical Chinese and Japanese poetry, Arabic or Hebrew or Sanskrit
scripture — which is the direct tie to the canon work in
`meaning-expression.md`, and the reason a language belongs in a map of
knowledge at all. Then travel that isn't tourism, and fieldwork you can
actually do: anthropology, archaeology, ecology and history all have a
language prerequisite nobody advertises. And family or community — a
grandparent's language, or the one spoken on your street. That one pays in a
currency none of the others do, and it is the one people regret leaving too
late.

**Learning a language late.** Be honest and don't overclaim in either
direction. Crystallized abilities — vocabulary, reading, accumulated
knowledge — hold up well into late life, and adults go on acquiring them;
what declines is speed of retrieval, working-memory capacity under time
pressure, and any hope of improving pronunciation. So expect reading and
vocabulary to go roughly as they always did, conversation to feel slower
than the page suggests it should, and a longer runway to B2 than the same
work would have taken at thirty. Adapt accordingly: lean on reading and
graded input, pick a language close to one you already have, keep sessions
short and daily rather than long and occasional, and lengthen the timeline
instead of raising the intensity.

Starting a new language in your sixties or seventies is a legitimate
project, and reviving a dormant one is a better-value one. It is also among
the better-supported cognitively demanding activities available — sustained,
effortful, novel, and social, which is the combination that anything worth
doing here has. Note that the stronger claim, that bilingualism delays
dementia, is genuinely contested in the literature and you should not build
a plan on it. Learn the language because it opens a literature and a
country. Any cognitive dividend is a bonus you didn't pay for.
