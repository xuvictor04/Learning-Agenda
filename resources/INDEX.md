# Index — Everything Named in This Library

The eleven files in `resources/` run to roughly ten thousand lines and name
something close to two thousand distinct works. That is a library, and a
library without a catalogue is a pile. This file is the catalogue.

Use it three ways. **Lookup:** you remember a book was recommended for
genetics but not which one — go to `By domain`, find the domain, read the
tier. **Deduplication:** before adding anything to your own list, check
`By author` to see whether this library already has that person covered, and
under which domain. **Compression:** if you want the shortest defensible
version of the whole thing, read `The essential shelf` and stop.

Three conventions. Works are listed under the tier they appear at in the
source file — T3 (literacy), T2 (working depth), T1 (mastery), or the long
shelf that sits beyond T1. A work named in several domains is listed once
per domain, and its author entry carries all of them. Attributions are
reproduced exactly as the source files give them, including the ones that
name a translator, an editor, or a pair of surnames joined with an
ampersand. Nothing here has been added from outside the files; if a work is
missing, it is missing because the library never named it.

## The essential shelf

Fifty-eight works. If seventy years produced nothing but these, read
properly, you would still be extraordinarily well educated — every cluster
in the map represented, every one of them durable, and every one of them
written by a mind worth several years of your attention. They are ordered
in the sequence you should actually meet them: the ones that change how you
read come first, then the formal spine, then outward through the physical,
living, mental, social, expressive and made worlds. Nothing here is drawn
from outside the library.

1. **Brown, Roediger & McDaniel, *Make It Stick*** — education. First
   because it changes the return on every book below it.
2. **Mortimer Adler & Charles Van Doren, *How to Read a Book*** —
   fundamentals (reading). The syntopical chapter is the skill this whole
   library assumes and nothing else teaches.
3. **Joseph M. Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*** — rhetoric
   & writing. The only book that reliably improves prose on one pass, and
   every artifact you owe is prose.
4. **Plato, *Euthyphro / Apology / Crito*** (Grube) — philosophy. Sixty
   pages that show philosophy as an activity before any doctrine arrives.
5. **David Spiegelhalter, *The Art of Statistics*** — statistics. Teaches
   the reasoning through real cases, which is the reasoning you will need
   against every empirical claim in the rest of the list.
6. **Timothy Gowers, *Mathematics: A Very Short Introduction*** —
   mathematics. Explains the abstract method, which is the thing that locks
   outsiders out.
7. **Blitzstein & Hwang, *Introduction to Probability*** — statistics &
   probability. Free, complete, and the load-bearing half of quantitative
   literacy.
8. **Nagel & Newman, *Gödel's Proof*** — logic & foundations. A hundred
   pages that actually walk the argument instead of gesturing at it.
9. **Charles Petzold, *Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and
   Software*** — theoretical CS; computing in practice. Builds a computer
   from flashlights, so computation stops being a metaphor.
10. **Claude Shannon, *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*** (1948) —
    information theory. One of the most readable landmark papers ever
    written, and the origin of a concept now used everywhere.
11. **Richard Feynman, *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*** — physics. Free,
    inexhaustible, and the standing demonstration of what explanation is.
12. **Carl Sagan, *Cosmos*** — cosmology & astronomy. Still the best
    invitation ever made, and it models reasoning rather than results.
13. **Steven Weinberg, *The First Three Minutes*** — cosmology. The modern
    cosmological picture stated by someone who helped build it.
14. **Primo Levi, *The Periodic Table*** — chemistry. Chemistry as a way of
    seeing, by a working chemist who was one of the century's great writers.
15. **Linus Pauling, *The Nature of the Chemical Bond*** — chemistry. It
    built modern chemistry and is still readable.
16. **John McPhee, *Annals of the Former World*** — earth science. Nothing
    else installs deep time in your bones.
17. **J.E. Gordon, *Structures: Or Why Things Don't Fall Down*** — materials
    science; structural engineering. Teaches you to see force in ordinary
    objects, with almost no mathematics.
18. **Charles Darwin, *On the Origin of Species*** — evolutionary biology.
    The argument in the author's own careful, self-doubting voice.
19. **Richard Dawkins, *The Selfish Gene*** — evolutionary biology. Makes
    selection an algorithm you can run in your head and predict with.
20. **Siddhartha Mukherjee, *The Gene: An Intimate History*** — genetics.
    Braids mechanism with the eugenics history most accounts skip.
21. **Aldo Leopold, *A Sand County Almanac*** — ecology. The land ethic,
    and prose that has outlasted the science around it.
22. **David Quammen, *The Song of the Dodo*** — ecology. Island
    biogeography as narrative; own it and you can reason about extinction.
23. **Atul Gawande, *Complications*** — medicine & physiology. About the
    epistemics of practice — uncertainty, error, judgment — which is what
    medicine actually is.
24. **Geoffrey Rose, *Rose's Strategy of Preventive Medicine*** — public
    health. Under 150 pages, and it inverts most intuitions about where to
    intervene.
25. **Oliver Sacks, *The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat*** —
    neuroscience. Each chapter is a mind broken in one specific way, which
    is precisely how the field learned what the parts do.
26. **Kandel et al., *Principles of Neural Science*** — neuroscience;
    medicine. The reference you will return to for forty years.
27. **William James, *The Principles of Psychology*** — psychology. Written
    in 1890, still quoted, and better on attention and habit than most of
    what followed.
28. **Guy Deutscher, *The Unfolding of Language*** — linguistics. Shows
    grammar as the residue of erosion, and you never think of structure as
    designed again.
29. **Melanie Mitchell, *Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking
    Humans*** — AI. A working researcher who takes both the achievements and
    the skepticism seriously, and keeps the seventy-year history visible.
30. **J.R. McNeill & William H. McNeill, *The Human Web*** — world history.
    Organizes everything around connection rather than a march of
    civilizations, which is the frame you want before any survey.
31. **Thucydides, *The Peloponnesian War*** — world history; military
    strategy. The hardest, best political history ever written.
32. **Marc Bloch, *The Historian's Craft*** — world history. What it means
    to argue from sources that were never made for you.
33. **Clifford Geertz, *The Interpretation of Cultures*** — anthropology.
    Shows what interpretation actually consists of; everything after either
    does that or argues with it.
34. **Marcel Mauss, *The Gift*** — anthropology; economics. Short, whole,
    and it makes the market the unusual case rather than the default.
35. **C. Wright Mills, *The Sociological Imagination*** — sociology. A
    hundred pages telling you what a discipline is *for*.
36. **Max Weber, *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*** —
    sociology. Short, and the model for how a cultural cause gets argued.
37. **Francis Fukuyama, *The Origins of Political Order*** — political
    science. After it you never again treat state, rule of law and
    democracy as one thing.
38. ***Federalist* 10 and 51** — political science. Twenty pages, and the
    best short statement of institutional design ever written.
39. **H.L.A. Hart, *The Concept of Law*** — law. Afterward you cannot
    confuse law with orders backed by threats, or with morality.
40. **CORE Econ, *The Economy*** — economics. Free, and the modern
    introduction: inequality, institutions and real data before
    supply-and-demand.
41. **Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations*** — economics. Books I and IV, in
    the original, where the sentence everybody misquotes actually sits.
42. **F.A. Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society"** (1945) — economics.
    Fifteen free pages, and the best argument for prices ever written.
43. **Burton Malkiel, *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*** — finance;
    personal finance. Forces the efficiency question first, and your answer
    reorganizes everything else you read about money.
44. **James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State*** — geography; agriculture;
    political science. One idea that explains cadastral maps, surnames, city
    grids, forestry and collectivization at once.
45. **Neil Postman, *Amusing Ourselves to Death*** — media & communication.
    Turns McLuhan's aphorism into a concrete, arguable claim that transfers
    to every medium since.
46. **Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*** — philosophy. Reads as a checklist
    at 20 and as an account of your actual losses at 60.
47. **Stephen Prothero, *God Is Not One*** — world religions. Inoculates
    against the single commonest error before you read anything else.
48. **Homer, *The Odyssey*** (Emily Wilson) — literature. Nearly everything
    after is arguing with it, and Wilson's introduction is an education in
    what a translator decides.
49. **Erich Auerbach, *Mimesis*** — literature. A survey and an argument at
    once: how reality got represented, one passage at a time.
50. **Aaron Copland, *What to Listen For in Music*** — music. Converts
    passive listening into attention with a target.
51. **John Berger, *Ways of Seeing*** — visual art. Makes visible the
    ideology in how you already look, before you learn any names.
52. **E.H. Gombrich, *The Story of Art*** — visual art & architecture. One
    great voice moving through a tradition, and honest about being that.
53. **Aristotle, *Poetics*** (Heath) — theater & film. Forty pages; every
    story manual sold today is a diluted restatement of it.
54. **David MacKay, *Sustainable Energy — Without the Hot Air*** — energy
    engineering. Refuses to argue with adjectives; every claim is in kWh per
    person per day.
55. **Fred Brooks, *The Mythical Man-Month*** — computing in practice.
    Fifty years old and still describing your project.
56. **Andy Grove, *High Output Management*** — business & management. The
    best book ever written on managing, and it is about leverage, not
    charisma.
57. **Carl von Clausewitz, *On War*** (Howard & Paret) — military strategy.
    Book One Chapter One and Book Eight; friction and the trinity are
    portable far beyond war.
58. **David Pye, *The Nature and Art of Workmanship*** — craft; design. The
    workmanship of risk against the workmanship of certainty — a
    distinction that will organize forty years of your thinking.

## By domain

they appear under. Semicolons separate entries, because the entries
themselves contain commas. Courses, papers and datasets appear where the
source files treat them as reading. Where a file names a work only to warn
you off it, that is marked.
