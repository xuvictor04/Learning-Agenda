# Bridges — Where the Domains Meet

Every other file in `resources/` is organised by single domain — the right
way to build literacy and the wrong way to use it. This file is the other
half. It is about the joins.

## Why the intersections are where the work is

Almost all original work happens at intersections, for an unglamorous
reason: the interior of a field is crowded and the edges are not. A thousand
people work on the central problems of condensed matter physics and all of
them are better at it than you will ever be. But the number who hold physics
*and* archaeology *and* enough statistics to be dangerous is small, and the
number who have thought carefully about what that combination could ask is
smaller still.

The map in `02-map.md` has 44 domains. That is 946 unordered pairs, and
triples run to the tens of thousands. Most have never been held in one head
with any seriousness. A specialist has depth you can never buy; you have
combinatorics they can never fake. That is the comparative advantage of a
broad education, and it does not exist until the breadth does.

It is also why the second half of `03-spine.md` isn't empty. The ledger
completing at year 30 is not the end of acquisition; it is the moment the
bridges become visible. You cannot notice that two fields are asking one
question in two vocabularies until you have sat with both.

**The practical rule: a bridge is worth pursuing when you can name a specific
question that neither field alone can answer.** Not a theme, not a resonance,
not "these both involve networks." A question, in one sentence, that a
specialist in A would punt to B and a specialist in B would punt to A.
Everything else is interdisciplinary enthusiasm — a mood rather than a
project, and it produces conference panels and no results.

One warning before the list. Bridges are where the cranks live. Literacy in
two fields is exactly enough to produce confident nonsense in both: enough
vocabulary to write fluently, not enough depth to know what you assumed. The
failure is systematic — physicists reinventing bad economics, economists
reinventing bad ecology, computer scientists reinventing bad linguistics. The
countermeasure is not humility, which is a feeling and changes nothing. It is
procedural: **show the work to a specialist on each side, separately, before
you publish.** If both wince, you learned something cheaply.

## The bridges

### Biophysics and biological scaling
*Physics × Evolutionary & molecular biology*
- **The question it owns.** What do the laws of physics permit a living
  thing to be? Why does metabolic rate track mass to a fractional power, and
  why is there a largest possible insect and a smallest possible mammal?
- **Read.** Erwin Schrödinger, *What Is Life?* (1944); D'Arcy Wentworth
  Thompson, *On Growth and Form* (1917); Steven Vogel, *Life in Moving
  Fluids*; Geoffrey West, *Scale* (2017), read sceptically — the
  quarter-power claims are genuinely contested.
- **A project.** Pull a published dataset of mammalian body masses and basal
  metabolic rates, fit the exponent yourself, and say honestly whether your
  data can distinguish 3/4 from 2/3. Popular accounts assume it can.

### Tuning, harmony and number
*Mathematics × Music*
- **The question it owns.** Why do the ratios that sound consonant refuse to
  close into a consistent system, and what does every musical culture do
  about that failure? The problem is arithmetic and the solutions are
  aesthetic.
- **Read.** Hermann von Helmholtz, *On the Sensations of Tone* (1863); David
  Benson, *Music: A Mathematical Offering* (free PDF from the author); Ross
  W. Duffin, *How Equal Temperament Ruined Harmony (and Why You Should
  Care)*; Dmitri Tymoczko, *A Geometry of Music* (2011).
- **A project.** Build a monochord — a board, a string, a movable bridge —
  and find the Pythagorean comma with your own ears and a ruler. Then retune
  a recording into just intonation and hear where the modulation breaks.

### Economic history and the Great Divergence
*Economics × World history*
- **The question it owns.** Why did sustained growth begin where and when it
  did? Economics has models that can't be run on the past; history has
  archives that can't be aggregated. The bridge is where the two meet.
- **Read.** Robert Allen, *Global Economic History: A Very Short
  Introduction*; Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence* (2000); Joel Mokyr,
  *A Culture of Growth* (2017); Robert Gordon, *The Rise and Fall of American
  Growth* (2016).
- **A project.** Take a long series — the Bank of England's Millennium of
  Macroeconomic Data is free — and test one textbook claim against it. Then
  read the Reinhart–Rogoff episode, where a spreadsheet error reached policy
  because nobody re-ran the numbers.

### Evolutionary psychology
*Evolutionary & molecular biology × Psychology*
- **The question it owns.** Which features of the mind are adaptations, which
  are by-products, and how would you ever tell? The interesting work is in
  the epistemology, not the just-so stories.
- **Read.** Robert Trivers, "The Evolution of Reciprocal Altruism" (1971);
  Barkow, Cosmides & Tooby, *The Adapted Mind* (1992); Gould & Lewontin, "The
  Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm" (1979) — the standing
  corrective; Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan, "The Weirdest People in the
  World?" (2010).
- **A project.** Pick one widely repeated evolutionary-psychological claim
  and design the study that would falsify it. If no such study exists, say so
  in print — that is itself a finding about the claim.

### Evidence-based medicine
*Statistics & probability × Medicine & physiology*
- **The question it owns.** How do you know a treatment works? Clinicians see
  individual patients and cannot see selection effects; statisticians see
  effect sizes and cannot see patients.
- **Read.** Archie Cochrane, *Effectiveness and Efficiency* (1972); Austin
  Bradford Hill, "The Environment and Disease: Association or Causation?"
  (1965); Evans, Thornton, Chalmers & McPherson, *Testing Treatments* (free
  online); Ben Goldacre, *Bad Pharma* (2012).
- **A project.** Take one Cochrane review, retrieve its trials, re-run the
  meta-analysis, and draw the funnel plot. Publication bias stops being an
  abstraction the first time you see the hole in your own plot.

### Consciousness and the free-will problem
*Neuroscience × Philosophy*
- **The question it owns.** What would count as an explanation of
  experience? Neuroscience can find correlates indefinitely without settling
  whether a correlate explains anything — a philosophical question with
  empirical stakes.
- **Read.** Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974); Patricia
  Churchland, *Neurophilosophy* (1986); Daniel Dennett, *Consciousness
  Explained* (1991); David Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind* (1996).
- **A project.** Read Libet's original experiments and the thirty years of
  methodological objections to them, then write the strongest case against
  whatever you currently believe about free will. If you can't, you don't
  hold the position — it holds you.

### Computational linguistics
*Theoretical computer science & information theory × Linguistics*
- **The question it owns.** What does a system need in order to produce
  language, and does a model that produces fluent text thereby know
  anything? The question is now urgent rather than academic.
- **Read.** Claude Shannon, "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English"
  (1951); Noam Chomsky, "Three Models for the Description of Language"
  (1956); Jurafsky & Martin, *Speech and Language Processing* (free draft);
  Bender & Koller, "Climbing towards NLU" (ACL 2020).
- **A project.** Train an n-gram model and a small neural model on the same
  corpus and characterise where each fails. The failure taxonomy is the
  output that matters; the perplexity number is not.

### Ecological economics and the commons
*Ecology × Economics*
- **The question it owns.** How do you price something that has no market and
  cannot be replaced, and what governs a resource that everyone can take
  from? Both fields have an answer and the answers do not agree.
- **Read.** Garrett Hardin, "The Tragedy of the Commons" (1968), then Elinor
  Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990), which dismantles it empirically;
  Herman Daly & Joshua Farley, *Ecological Economics*; Partha Dasgupta, *The
  Economics of Biodiversity: The Dasgupta Review* (2021, free).
- **A project.** Find a commons you can reach — a fishery, an irrigation
  district, a shared car park, an open-source project — and audit it against
  Ostrom's eight design principles. Report which are missing and what happens
  where they are.

### Conservation science and the history of colour
*Chemistry × Visual art & architecture*
- **The question it owns.** What is a painting made of, and how did that
  constrain what could be painted, when, and where? Pigment availability is
  an art-historical fact and a supply-chain fact at once.
- **Read.** Philip Ball, *Bright Earth: Art and the Invention of Color*
  (2001); Cennino Cennini, *Il Libro dell'Arte* (as *The Craftsman's
  Handbook*); Gettens & Stout, *Painting Materials: A Short Encyclopaedia*;
  the National Gallery of Art's *Artists' Pigments* handbook series.
- **A project.** Grind an earth pigment, bind it in egg tempera, paint out a
  panel, and expose half of it to a window for six months. You will learn why
  certain colours vanish from the record.

### Archaeogenetics
*Genetics × World history / Anthropology & archaeology*
- **The question it owns.** Did a culture spread because people moved or
  because ideas moved? For a century this was unanswerable; ancient DNA
  answered it for several cases and complicated it for others.
- **Read.** Cavalli-Sforza, Menozzi & Piazza, *The History and Geography of
  Human Genes* (1994); Svante Pääbo, *Neanderthal Man* (2014); David Reich,
  *Who We Are and How We Got Here* (2018); Patrick Geary, *The Myth of
  Nations* (2002) as the corrective on what ancestry does *not* license.
- **A project.** The Reich Lab's Allen Ancient DNA Resource is public.
  Reproduce a principal-components plot from it, then write on what an
  admixture proportion can and cannot say about the existence of "a people."

### Geopolitics
*Geography & geopolitics × Political science*
- **The question it owns.** How much of a state's behaviour is downstream of
  its coastline, rivers, mountains and mineral endowment — and how much of
  "geography explains it" is retrospective storytelling?
- **Read.** Alfred Thayer Mahan, *The Influence of Sea Power upon History*
  (1890); Halford Mackinder, "The Geographical Pivot of History" (1904);
  Daniel Yergin, *The Prize* (1990); James C. Scott, *The Art of Not Being
  Governed* (2009); Jared Diamond, *Guns, Germs, and Steel*, with the
  anthropological objections to it.
- **A project.** Take one contested border, map its watersheds, passes and
  resource deposits yourself in QGIS, then write down precisely which
  features of the dispute geography fails to explain.

### Engineering × Design
*Engineering (any of the three) × Design*
- **The question it owns.** Why do objects that work well so often feel
  wrong, and vice versa? Engineering optimises a specified objective; design
  is largely the argument about what the objective should have been.
- **Read.** Henry Petroski, *To Engineer Is Human* and *The Evolution of
  Useful Things*; Donald Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*; David Pye,
  *The Nature and Art of Workmanship*; Christopher Alexander et al., *A
  Pattern Language*.
- **A project.** Take an object you dislike using, document every error it
  induces, then redesign and build it. Give the second version to somebody
  else and watch them fail differently.

### Law and technology
*Law & legal systems × Computing in practice*
- **The question it owns.** When code and statute both regulate behaviour,
  which one actually binds? Lessig's claim that architecture is a form of law
  is now the operating condition of daily life.
- **Read.** Lawrence Lessig, *Code: Version 2.0* (free); James Boyle, *The
  Public Domain* (free); Helen Nissenbaum, *Privacy in Context* (2010);
  Frank Pasquale, *The Black Box Society* (2015).
- **A project.** Read the actual text of one regulation — the GDPR, DMCA
  §1201, the EU AI Act — and write the engineering specification it implies.
  Then find the clause that cannot be implemented as written. There is always
  one, and naming it precisely is a contribution.

### Materials and structure
*Materials science × Visual art & architecture*
- **The question it owns.** Which buildings were possible when, and why? Span,
  height and form are set by material properties, and architectural history
  read without them is a history of taste with the physics deleted.
- **Read.** J. E. Gordon, *Structures: Or Why Things Don't Fall Down* and
  *The New Science of Strong Materials*; Mario Salvadori, *Why Buildings
  Stand Up*; Vitruvius, *Ten Books on Architecture*; Vaclav Smil, *Making the
  Modern World: Materials and Dematerialization*.
- **A project.** Pick a building you can walk to. Identify its structural
  system, estimate the load path, and calculate whether one visible member is
  sized as you would expect.

### Organisational ethnography
*Anthropology & archaeology × Business, management & entrepreneurship*
- **The question it owns.** What do people in an organisation actually do, as
  opposed to what the process diagram says? Management literature asks people
  to describe their work; ethnography watches it, and the two rarely match.
- **Read.** Erving Goffman, *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*
  (1956); Julian Orr, *Talking About Machines* (1996); Diane Vaughan, *The
  Challenger Launch Decision* (1996); Karen Ho, *Liquidated: An Ethnography
  of Wall Street* (2009).
- **A project.** Shadow one role for a week, with permission and a notebook.
  Write fieldnotes daily, then produce the gap analysis between documented
  process and observed practice. Every organisation has this gap; almost none
  have it written down.

### Foundations and the philosophy of infinity
*Mathematics / Logic & foundations × Philosophy*
- **The question it owns.** What makes an axiom true rather than merely
  useful, and in what sense do mathematical objects exist? Mathematicians
  answer this daily by their practice and rarely defend the answer.
- **Read.** Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations* (1976); Benacerraf &
  Putnam (eds.), *Philosophy of Mathematics: Selected Readings* — especially
  Benacerraf's "Mathematical Truth" (1973); Mary Tiles, *The Philosophy of
  Set Theory*; Penelope Maddy, "Believing the Axioms" (1988).
- **A project.** Argue for adopting a large cardinal axiom as a working set
  theorist would, then attack the same case as a nominalist would. Both
  halves in your own voice.

### Archaeoastronomy and historical eclipses
*Cosmology & astronomy × World history*
- **The question it owns.** Can the sky date the archive? Celestial mechanics
  is precise enough to pin a chronicle entry to a day, and the residuals
  between prediction and record turn out to measure the slowing of Earth's
  rotation.
- **Read.** F. Richard Stephenson, *Historical Eclipses and Earth's Rotation*
  (1997); Anthony Aveni, *Skywatchers*; Clive Ruggles, *Astronomy in
  Prehistoric Britain and Ireland*; E. G. Richards, *Mapping Time: The
  Calendar and Its History*.
- **A project.** Take one dated eclipse from a chronicle, retro-calculate
  it, and see whether it constrains the chronology. Or survey a local
  alignment with a compass and real error bars — the error bars are what
  separate this field from its lunatic fringe.

### The politics of health numbers
*Public health & care systems × Statistics × Political science*
- **The question it owns.** Which health disparities are real, which are
  artefacts of measurement, and what makes a number move a government? Each
  of the three fields is helpless on this without the other two.
- **Read.** John Snow, *On the Mode of Communication of Cholera* (1855); Doll
  & Hill on smoking and lung cancer (1954, 1956); Theodore Porter, *Trust in
  Numbers* (1995); Michael Marmot, *The Status Syndrome*; Oreskes & Conway,
  *Merchants of Doubt* (2010).
- **A project.** Reconstruct one local health disparity from open data, then
  identify the specific official who could act on it and what evidence their
  process accepts. The second half is the part nobody does.

### The energy budget of food
*Agriculture & food systems × Ecology × Engineering: energy & power systems*
- **The question it owns.** How many joules does a calorie cost? Modern
  agriculture is a system for converting fossil energy into food, and the
  conversion ratio governs both its productivity and its fragility.
- **Read.** Vaclav Smil, *Energy and Civilization: A History* (2017) and
  *Enriching the Earth* (2001), on Haber–Bosch; Howard T. Odum, *Environment,
  Power, and Society*; David Montgomery, *Dirt: The Erosion of
  Civilizations*; Miguel Altieri, *Agroecology*.
- **A project.** Audit the full energy cost of one meal or one garden bed —
  fuel, fertiliser, embodied energy, transport, labour — against calories
  delivered. Year 27–30's agriculture slot is the natural home for it.

### Fiction and the reading mind
*Literature × Psychology*
- **The question it owns.** What is a novel doing to a reader that no
  description of a novel can do? Literary criticism has three centuries of
  observation and no mechanism; psychology has mechanisms and thin materials.
- **Read.** William James, *The Principles of Psychology*, the "stream of
  thought" chapter; Erich Auerbach, *Mimesis* (1946); Elaine Scarry,
  *Dreaming by the Book* (1999); Lisa Zunshine, *Why We Read Fiction*
  (2006).
- **A project.** Take one technique — free indirect discourse is the obvious
  candidate — and write about what it achieves that a psychological
  description cannot. Read the Kidd and Castano theory-of-mind study and its
  failed replication first: this bridge's cautionary tale.

### Logistics, weapons and war
*Military history & strategy × Engineering: machines & manufacturing*
- **The question it owns.** Was the plan physically possible? Campaign
  narratives are written in intentions; fodder, tonnage, road capacity and
  metallurgy decide outcomes, and almost no military history does the
  arithmetic.
- **Read.** Martin van Creveld, *Supplying War: Logistics from Wallenstein to
  Patton* (1977); Geoffrey Parker, *The Military Revolution* (1988); Brodie &
  Brodie, *From Crossbow to H-Bomb*; David Edgerton, *The Shock of the Old*
  (2006).
- **A project.** Pick a campaign and work its supply arithmetic from primary
  figures — rations, fodder, wagon capacity, march rates — and state whether
  the plan was ever achievable. Van Creveld's method is reusable.

### Information in living systems
*Theoretical computer science & information theory × Genetics*
- **The question it owns.** Is "information" in biology a measurable quantity
  or a metaphor that has escaped its enclosure? Both answers have serious
  defenders and the disagreement is unusually clarifying.
- **Read.** Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (1948); Maynard
  Smith & Szathmáry, *The Major Transitions in Evolution* (1995); John Maynard
  Smith, "The Concept of Information in Biology" (*Philosophy of Science*,
  2000) with the published replies by Godfrey-Smith and Sterelny; Schneider
  & Stephens, "Sequence Logos" (1990).
- **A project.** Compute the information content of a transcription-factor
  binding site from an aligned set of real sequences, and compare it with the
  information needed to locate that site in the genome. Schneider's result —
  that the two roughly match — is reproducible in a weekend.

### Climate and the historical record
*Earth science & climate × World history*
- **The question it owns.** When did climate actually change the course of
  events, and when is that claim just determinism with proxy data attached?
  The methodological question is more interesting than any single case.
- **Read.** Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie, *Times of Feast, Times of Famine*
  (1967); Geoffrey Parker, *Global Crisis* (2013); John Brooke, *Climate
  Change and the Course of Global History* (2014); Kyle Harper, *The Fate of
  Rome* (2017).
- **A project.** Line one regional crisis's documentary chronology up against
  a published tree-ring or ice-core series at annual resolution. Then write
  the paragraph stating what the correlation does not establish.

### The science of learning
*Education × Psychology*
- **The question it owns.** Which study methods actually work, and why does
  almost every institution teach the ones that don't? This bridge is
  self-interested: it sets the efficiency of the other forty-three.
- **Read.** Hermann Ebbinghaus, *Memory* (1885); Dunlosky et al., "Improving
  Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques" (*Psychological
  Science in the Public Interest*, 2013); Bjork & Bjork, "Making Things Hard
  on Yourself, But in a Good Way" (2011); Ericsson & Pool, *Peak* (2016),
  read with the Macnamara meta-analysis on deliberate practice.
- **A project.** You already have the dataset. Export your SRS review history
  and weekly logs and test one claim above against your own record — interval
  schedule against retention, say. `resources/fundamentals.md` sets the
  practice; this checks whether it works.

## Finding your own bridges

Twenty-four is a starter set. The ones that matter to you will come out of
your own reading, and there is a method for noticing them.

**Watch for the same phenomenon in two literacy artifacts.** If you wrote
about feedback loops in the ecology artifact and again in the control-systems
artifact, that is a signal. Read the two passages side by side and ask
whether they are one claim or two that merely rhyme. Both answers are
useful.

**Keep a running "these two should talk" list.** One line per observation, no
justification required, written the moment it occurs. Most entries are
nothing; the value is in the ones that recur, because an idea you wrote down
three times across five years has a question underneath it. Review the list
at the annual review (`templates/annual-review.md`) under the map check, next
to the second-spike thinking — a repeated bridge observation is often what a
promotion should be aimed at.

**Listen for the punt.** The best signal of all is a specialist in A saying
"that's really a question for the B people," and the B people saying the
reverse. That question is orphaned, and an orphaned question in a live field
is exactly what a generalist can adopt. Collect punts; they are free and they
are specific.

**Then apply the rule and the countermeasure.** Name the question in one
sentence; if you can't, it isn't ready. Then get a specialist on each side to
tell you what you assumed, while correction is still cheap.

## The bridge as the third act's work

`phases/decade-3-synthesis.md` calls cross-field work "the work of the third
act"; `phases/decade-4-integration.md` names it the decade's signature
output; `phases/decade-5-reinvention.md` puts the book-length synthesis and
the freely chosen third spike at the centre of the free decade. This file is
what those instructions cash out to. When they ask for the work only you
could do, they are asking for a bridge — a specific one, named, with a
question at its centre.

That reframes the whole first half. The 44 literacy passes are not a
collection to be completed and admired; they are the price of the right to
stand between two fields and be taken seriously by both. Nobody builds a
bridge from one side. The ledger is what buys the far bank.

It also changes what "falling behind" means. A year spent on one bridge
instead of two ledger domains is not a lapse — after year 30 it is the
assignment. The checkboxes were always the instrument. The bridges are what
the instrument was for.
