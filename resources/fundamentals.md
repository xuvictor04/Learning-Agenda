# Universal Fundamentals

These are not domains. They are the capacities the whole map runs on — the
table in `../02-map.md`. A weak row here does not close one field; it taxes
*every* field, permanently, at compound interest. A slow reader pays that
tax on all forty ledger entries. Someone who can't write pays it on every
artifact, which is the only thing that earns a checkmark.

So these come **before** any ledger domain. Rate yourself honestly against
the capability statements below — they're written to be testable rather than
flattering — and schedule the weak rows first. None of these is a book you
finish. Each is a practice you keep.

## Writing clearly

**Why it's load-bearing.** Writing is the output channel for every literacy
artifact in this repo, so an unchecked box is usually a writing failure, not
a reading failure. It is also the primary *thinking* tool: unclear prose is
almost always unclear thought, and drafting is how you find out you didn't
understand the thing you just read.

**What "strong" actually looks like.**
- You can explain a technical idea to an intelligent non-specialist in 800
  words, and they can restate it correctly afterward.
- You cut a first draft by 25% and it gets better, not shorter-and-worse.
- You can name why a sentence is bad — buried subject, nominalization,
  passive hiding the actor, wrong order of old and new information — rather
  than just feeling that it is.
- You revise structure, not just wording: you'll move or delete a whole
  section when the argument demands it.
- You ship on a schedule instead of polishing indefinitely.

**Learn from**
- **William Zinsser, *On Writing Well*** — start here. The clearest argument
  for simplicity, humanity, and cutting, in nonfiction specifically.
- **Joseph M. Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*** — the one
  that actually teaches diagnosis: characters as subjects, actions as verbs,
  old information before new. It has exercises with answers; do them.
- **Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style*** — the cognitive-science account of
  *why* the advice works, plus the best available treatment of the curse of
  knowledge, which is your dominant failure mode when writing about a field
  you just learned.

**The practice that actually builds it.** Publish 500–1,500 words every week,
with your name on it, where strangers can see it. Draft, sleep on it, cut a
quarter, apply Williams to the ten worst sentences. **Feedback is the
bottleneck and you must go get it deliberately**: a standing writing group
or one reader who owes you honesty; a paid editor once or twice a year (the
fastest money you can spend on this); reading aloud, which catches rhythm
problems your eye skips; and the brutal one — ask a reader to summarize your
piece back to you, and treat every distortion as your fault.

**Failure mode.** Writing constantly but never being read, never being
edited, and never rewriting. Ten years of unreviewed journaling produces a
practiced writer of exactly one register, with all original bad habits
intact.

## Reading difficult material fast

**Why it's load-bearing.** The whole ledger is reading-bound; your throughput
here sets the ceiling on how much of the map you cover in a lifetime. More
importantly, the map's real work is *syntopical* — reading many books on one
question and constructing your own answer, which no single book gives you.

**What "strong" actually looks like.**
- You decide within 10 minutes of opening a book whether to skim it, read it
  closely, or put it down — and you're usually right.
- You can inspect a 400-page nonfiction book in an hour and state its thesis,
  structure, and main evidence.
- You can read six books on one question and produce a synthesis that
  identifies where they actually disagree, in terms none of them used.
- You take notes that you can use two years later.
- You finish hard books. Difficulty makes you slow down rather than stop.

**Learn from**
- **Mortimer Adler & Charles Van Doren, *How to Read a Book*** — start here,
  and treat it as a manual rather than an essay. Its four levels are the
  spine: *elementary* (decoding), *inspectional* (systematic skimming to
  decide what a book is and whether it deserves you), *analytical* (coming to
  terms with an author, stating their argument fairly, then judging it), and
  *syntopical* (many books, one question, your own framework). The syntopical
  chapter is precisely the skill this entire repo needs.
- **Sönke Ahrens, *How to Take Smart Notes*** — for the retrieval half:
  notes as a system you write *into*, not a pile you never reopen.

**Be honest about speed.** "Speed reading" as marketed is mostly false.
Rayner and colleagues' 2016 review in *Psychological Science in the Public
Interest* is the standard debunking: comprehension and speed trade off
directly, and the trained-expert demonstrations are skimming. What actually
scales is **selectivity** (most books deserve an hour, not ten),
**prior knowledge** (the tenth book in a field reads five times faster than
the first — this is the strongest argument for depth), and **knowing which
mode a text deserves**. A dense proof and a business book are not read at the
same speed by anyone.

**The practice that actually builds it.** Weekly: inspect one book in 45
minutes and write a five-sentence verdict — thesis, structure, quality of
evidence, who it's for, read-or-skip. Per literacy artifact: at least four
sources on one question, then write the synthesis. Feedback comes from three
places — writing your summary *before* reading anyone else's and then
comparing; teaching the material and noticing which questions you can't
answer; and revisiting notes at six months to see what you retained versus
merely recognized.

**Failure mode.** Confusing pages consumed with understanding gained.
Highlighting is the classic tell — it produces a strong feeling of
processing and near-zero retention. If a book left no artifact and no cards,
you did not read it, you visited it.

## Math & statistics (working level)

**Why it's load-bearing.** Half the ledger is closed without it: the entire
Formal cluster, physics, chemistry, genetics, economics, and the empirical
core of psychology, medicine, and public health. Statistical literacy in
particular is the difference between reading a study and being manipulated
by one, and you will read a lot of studies.

**What "strong" actually looks like.**
- You can read a graph, an effect size, and a confidence interval and say
  what the study does and does not establish.
- You can explain why correlation isn't causation *mechanically* —
  confounding, selection, reverse causation — with a concrete example each.
- Base rates are automatic: given a test's sensitivity, specificity, and
  prevalence, you can reason about a positive result without a calculator
  and get the order of magnitude right.
- You can estimate — Fermi-style — an unfamiliar quantity to within an order
  of magnitude, and you notice when a published number fails that check.
- Calculus and linear algebra are legible: you know what a derivative, an
  integral, and a matrix multiplication *mean*, even if you'd need a
  reference to compute one.

**Learn from**
- **3Blue1Brown (Grant Sanderson), *Essence of Linear Algebra* and *Essence
  of Calculus*** — start here. Free, visual, and the fastest route to
  meaning rather than mechanics.
- **Harvard Stat 110 (Joe Blitzstein)** — free lectures plus the free
  textbook *Introduction to Probability* (Blitzstein & Hwang). The best open
  probability course in existence, and probability is the load-bearing half.
- **Darrell Huff, *How to Lie with Statistics*** — ninety minutes, written in
  1954, and it inoculates you permanently against a large fraction of
  everything you will ever be shown.
- See **`formal.md`** for the deep versions when this gets promoted to T2.

**The practice that actually builds it.** Do problems — reading math is not
learning math. Work Stat 110's exercises with the solutions covered, then
check. The feedback here is unusually clean and you should exploit it: an
answer is right or wrong, and a ten-line simulation settles any probability
argument. Standing drill, one a week, written down: take a statistic you met
in the wild and reconstruct it — sample, comparison group, absolute rather
than relative risk, and what it would look like if it were noise.

**Failure mode.** Watching lectures. Mathematical exposition is designed to
feel comprehensible, and 3Blue1Brown is superb at producing understanding
that evaporates the moment you face a blank page. If you have not done
problems, you have watched a documentary about math.

## Speaking & presenting

**Why it's load-bearing.** Teaching is the final exam (principle 6), and
teaching is spoken. Every mechanism this agenda relies on downstream —
mentoring, talks, tutoring, collaboration, persuading anyone of anything —
runs through your ability to hold a room in real time without a text.

**What "strong" actually looks like.**
- You can give a 10-minute talk on a subject you know, with no notes, that
  lands one clear idea.
- You can answer a hostile or confused question without becoming defensive
  or waffling, including saying "I don't know" cleanly.
- You watch a recording of yourself and don't wince — no verbal tics every
  eight seconds, no rushing, no reading slides aloud.
- You can compress the same content into 2, 10, or 40 minutes on request.
- You can explain your specialty to a room with no shared vocabulary and
  hold their attention.

**Learn from**
- **A real venue, weekly or monthly** — this is the primary source, not a
  supplement. **Toastmasters** is the widest-available structured one:
  recurring speaking slots, timed, with immediate audience evaluation. Any
  equivalent works — a meetup you present at, a class you teach, a lab
  meeting, a local society.
- **Scott Berkun, *Confessions of a Public Speaker*** — honest, practical,
  and specifically good on fear and on rooms that go wrong.
- **Chris Anderson, *TED Talks: The Official TED Guide to Public
  Speaking*** — for structuring a talk around a single idea.

**The practice that actually builds it.** Speak in front of humans at least
monthly, and **record every time**. The recording is the feedback source and
it is non-negotiable — your in-the-moment perception of your own speaking is
systematically wrong, and nothing else exposes filler words, pace, and
collapsed structure so cheaply. Watch it once, take three notes, fix *one*
next time. Second feedback source: an evaluator or a friend briefed to
report what they remember 24 hours later. If they can't name your one idea,
the talk failed regardless of how it felt.

**Failure mode.** Reading books about speaking, and rehearsing alone. Both
feel like work and neither exposes you to the only variable that matters —
an actual audience, in real time, when it goes wrong.

## Programming / data literacy

**Why it's load-bearing.** Every domain is becoming computational: history
has corpora, biology has sequences, economics has panel data, literature has
distant reading. Beyond that, code is the single most reliable feedback
mechanism available — it either runs or it doesn't — which makes it a
uniquely honest learning environment, and it is how you automate the
bookkeeping this agenda otherwise generates.

**What "strong" actually looks like.**
- You can write a 100-line script that reads a messy real-world file, cleans
  it, and produces a number or a chart you trust.
- You can call an unfamiliar API from its documentation alone.
- You use version control on your own work without being told to.
- You can read a graph you made and defend the data pipeline behind it.
- When you hit an error you've never seen, you can debug it — read the
  trace, form a hypothesis, isolate, test — rather than randomly editing.

**Learn from**
- **Al Sweigart, *Automate the Boring Stuff with Python*** — start here.
  Free at automatetheboringstuff.com, and uniquely aimed at people who don't
  want to become programmers, just to stop doing things by hand.
- **Harvard CS50 (David Malan)** — free, famously well-taught, and the right
  next step if you want to understand what's underneath rather than just
  produce scripts. See `made-applied.md` for depth beyond it.
- **Hadley Wickham & Garrett Grolemund, *R for Data Science*** (free online)
  if your work is data-shaped rather than software-shaped.

**The practice that actually builds it.** Automate a real annoyance of your
own — renaming your files, scraping the thing you check daily, pulling review
statistics out of your Anki database. It must be *yours*: a tutorial's
problem gives you a tutorial's feedback, while your own problem tells you
honestly whether it worked. One small automation a month, in a git repo you
actually commit to. Feedback is immediate and threefold — it runs or it
doesn't, the output is right or it isn't, and a month later you're either
still using it or you're not.

**Failure mode.** Tutorial hell — an endless queue of courses, each
producing a working result you didn't design and can't reproduce. The test
is whether you can build something nobody handed you the spec for.

## Working with AI tools

**Why it's load-bearing.** Used well, this is the largest single multiplier
on breadth in the whole system: it makes T3 literacy dramatically cheaper by
compressing orientation, generating practice problems, critiquing your
drafts, and answering the naive questions you'd otherwise spend an evening
on. Used badly, it is the fastest available route to believing you know
things you do not.

The tools change every few months, so learn the layer that doesn't: what to
delegate, how to verify, and when not to use it at all.

**What "strong" actually looks like.**
- You can state, for a given task, whether AI should draft it, critique it,
  or stay out of it — and why.
- You verify load-bearing claims against a primary source *before* acting on
  or repeating them, as reflex.
- You can tell where a model is likely to be confidently wrong: specific
  citations, exact numbers, recent events, niche technical detail, anything
  where a plausible-sounding answer is cheap to generate.
- You use it to generate *practice* — quiz me, find the flaw in my argument,
  play the skeptical expert — not just to generate output.
- You can do the task without it. You've checked.

**Learn from**
- **Ethan Mollick, *Co-Intelligence: Living and Working with AI*** — the
  durable mental model; start here. Its framing of AI as an unreliable but
  capable collaborator is the right one and will outlast the specific
  systems it describes.
- **The vendors' own documentation and cookbooks** — they move faster than
  any book can, and are the only current source on what a given tool can do.
- **Your own logged failures** — keep a short running note of times a model
  was confidently wrong to you. Nothing else calibrates as fast.

**The practice that actually builds it.** **The real skill is verification:
an unverified AI answer is a rumor.** Adopt one rule and hold it — every
factual claim you will act on, cite, or repeat gets checked against a source
that exists. Weekly drill: take one AI-generated explanation in a field you
know well and find its errors; this keeps your model of the failure surface
current as the systems improve. Feedback source: the primary text, the
compiler, the dataset, the expert — the thing that can say no.

**The specific risk for this repo.** Using AI to summarize a book instead of
reading it produces the *feeling* of literacy without the substance. You get
the thesis, the vocabulary, and enough surface to talk convincingly — and
none of the argument, the objections the author anticipated, or the changed
intuitions that were the actual point. A summary is not a T3 pass, and a
checkmark earned that way is a lie you told yourself. Use AI to prepare for a
book, to argue with you afterward, to explain the paragraph you couldn't
parse. Not to replace the reading.

**Failure mode.** Fluent output you can't defend. The tell is being asked
one follow-up question and discovering you have nothing behind the answer.

## Memory technique (SRS discipline)

**Why it's load-bearing.** Breadth is unretainable without it. Forty domains
at literacy depth is a large factual surface, and the natural forgetting
curve will quietly reclaim nearly all of it — leaving you having *read*
everything and knowing nothing, which is exactly the outcome this map exists
to prevent. Retrieval practice also *builds* understanding, not just recall:
it's the mechanism, not the bookkeeping.

**What "strong" actually looks like.**
- You've sustained a daily review habit for over a year without a rescue
  reset.
- You write your own cards, from your own reading, in your own words.
- Your cards are atomic — one fact, one retrieval — and you can explain why a
  card you failed three times was badly written.
- You can recall the core claims of a book you read two years ago, unprompted.
- Your review load is stable and under 20 minutes, because you're selective
  about what earns a card.

**Learn from**
- **Peter Brown, Henry Roediger & Mark McDaniel, *Make It Stick*** — start
  here. The science of retrieval practice, spacing, and interleaving, and
  why the study methods that feel most effective are the least effective.
- **The Anki manual** (docs.ankiweb.net) — dull and complete. Read the
  scheduling and card-type sections properly once; it pays for years.
- **Piotr Woźniak, "Effective learning: Twenty rules of formulating
  knowledge"** (free at supermemo.com) — the single most useful document
  ever written about card design. Rules 1–4 alone (understand before you
  memorize, build on basics, be minimal, keep it simple) fix most of what
  goes wrong.

**The practice that actually builds it.** 15–20 minutes daily, including
travel days — principle 2, and the highest-leverage habit in the agenda.
Cards get written *during* study, as a byproduct of reading, not in a later
session that never happens. **Write your own cards from your own study**:
formulating the question is where much of the learning happens, because it
forces you to decide what actually mattered in what you just read.
**Downloaded decks teach recognition rather than understanding** — they
encode someone else's compression of a book you didn't read, and you'll pass
the card while failing the concept. The feedback is built in and brutally
honest: your failure rate tells you both what you don't know and which cards
you wrote badly. Any card failed three times is a *writing* problem —
rewrite it smaller, don't grind it.

**Failure mode.** Collecting cards instead of reviewing them, or bailing out
of a 900-review backlog by resetting the deck. Both feel like progress. Fix
the intake rate instead: fewer, better cards, forever.

## Personal finance

**Why it's load-bearing.** This agenda needs decades of discretionary time
and a temperament not dominated by money stress. Financial security is what
buys the option to take a sabbatical, to change fields, to work less in a
year you'd rather study — and to keep learning at 60. Handled badly, it
doesn't slow the plan down; it ends it.

**What "strong" actually looks like.**
- You know your annual spending to within 10%, from records, not memory.
- You hold several months of expenses in cash and can say why that number.
- You're investing automatically, monthly, in low-cost broad-market index
  funds, and you know your all-in expense ratio.
- You can explain compounding, expense-ratio drag over 30 years, and why
  most active management underperforms after fees.
- You did not change your allocation the last time markets dropped 20%.

**Learn from**
- **Morgan Housel, *The Psychology of Money*** — start here. The behavioral
  half, which is the half that actually determines outcomes; short, and it
  will change what you do rather than what you know.
- **Taylor Larimore, Mel Lindauer & Michael LeBoeuf, *The Bogleheads' Guide
  to Investing*** — the mechanics of a low-cost index strategy, stated
  plainly and without a product to sell you.
- **Ramit Sethi, *I Will Teach You to Be Rich*** — the setup manual: which
  accounts, in which order, automated how. Ignore the tone if it grates; the
  checklists are the value.

**The practice that actually builds it.** **Automate once, early, then stop
touching it.** Direct deposit splits into savings and investment on payday,
before you see the money; contributions to whatever tax-advantaged accounts
your country offers, maxed if you can; a fixed low-cost allocation you chose
deliberately and won't revisit. Then one annual review — an hour, same date
every year — to rebalance, check fees, update the numbers. Outcome feedback
here is decades-slow and therefore useless in the short run, which is
precisely why the system must be automatic: you are building against your
own future judgment. The available fast feedback is process feedback — did I
track spending, did I raise the contribution when income rose, did I leave
the allocation alone.

**Failure mode.** Substituting research for action. Reading about investing,
comparing brokers, optimizing an allocation you never fund. Ten years of
mediocre automated investing beats an unstarted perfect plan by a margin no
subsequent cleverness recovers — compounding needs decades and you have them
only if you start now.
