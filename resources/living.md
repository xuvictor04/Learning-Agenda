# Living

The five domains of the living world, written out at all three depth tiers.
Nothing here assumes you have studied any of it before. Read the T3 block for
any domain you're bringing to literacy; T2 and T1 are there so that when a
field earns promotion at an annual review, you already know what the real
curriculum looks like.

---

## Evolutionary & molecular biology

**The question it asks.** How does a process with no foresight — copying with
errors, filtered by differential survival — produce structures that look
designed? And why does everything alive run on the same molecular toolkit? The
field's problem is joining a statistical process acting on populations over
deep time to a chemistry of self-copying polymers acting in milliseconds.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Natural selection as an algorithm: variation, heritability, differential
  reproduction. Nothing else is required.
- Common descent and homology — the same bones in a bat wing and your hand.
- The central dogma (DNA → RNA → protein) and its real exceptions.
- Regulation, not gene content, is where most form comes from; evo-devo
  toolkit genes are shared across phyla.
- Drift and the neutral theory: most molecular change is not adaptive.
- Levels of selection and the gene's-eye view — what that argument is about.
- The major transitions (cells, endosymbiosis, sex, multicellularity,
  sociality), each a change in how information is transmitted.
- Phylogenies are inferences with error bars, not family portraits.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Reading evolution as directional. No ladder, no goal; complexity is not the
  trend line.
- Assuming everything is an adaptation. Much is drift, constraint, or history.
- Hearing "selfish gene" as a claim about selfish organisms. It is accounting,
  not psychology.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **The Selfish Gene** — Richard Dawkins. The door because it
  makes selection an algorithm you can run in your head; afterward you can
  predict what selection will and won't build. Chapters 1–6 and 11 carry it.
- *Survey:* **Evolution: Making Sense of Life** — Zimmer & Emlen. A textbook
  that reads like journalism; the best complete first pass. Pair with **The
  Vital Question** — Nick Lane for the energetic origin story.
- *Canon:* Darwin, **On the Origin of Species** (1859). Read ch. 1, ch. 4
  (natural selection), ch. 6 (Darwin is his own best critic) and ch. 14. Skip
  the pigeon-fancier detail. Then Monod, **Chance and Necessity**.
- *Course:* Open Yale **EEB 122, Principles of Evolution, Ecology and
  Behavior** (Stephen Stearns) — 36 free lectures, still the best online
  treatment. MIT OCW **7.01SC Fundamentals of Biology** for the molecular
  half. Sapolsky's Stanford **Human Behavioral Biology** lectures are the
  famous supplement — excellent, but a supplement.
- *Artifact:* Take one adaptation — lactase persistence, sickle-cell
  heterozygote advantage, the vertebrate eye — and write 2,000 words tracing
  the mutation, the selective regime, the population dynamics of its spread,
  and the evidence the story is true rather than merely plausible.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* **Molecular Biology of the Cell** — Alberts et al. (work it, don't
  read it) and **Evolution** — Futuyma & Kirkpatrick.
- *Practice:* Wet — a community bio lab or university short course: PCR, run
  a gel, transform bacteria with pGLO, culture something and kill it by
  accident. Dry — pull sequences from GenBank, align in MAFFT, build trees in
  IQ-TREE and learn what a bootstrap value does not mean; read a region in
  Ensembl; code a Wright–Fisher simulation from scratch until fixation
  probability stops surprising you.
- *Primary literature:* Nature, Science, Cell, eLife, Molecular Biology and
  Evolution. Read directly: Kimura 1968 (neutral theory, three pages);
  Margulis 1967, "On the origin of mitosing cells"; Blount, Borland & Lenski
  2008 (*PNAS*) on citrate use in the long-term E. coli experiment.
- *You've arrived when:* given an unfamiliar sequence you can find homologs,
  build a defensible tree, say which branches are contested and why, and
  explain any Methods section in your subfield to someone else.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Alberts; Futuyma & Kirkpatrick; Lynch & Walsh, **Genetics
  and Analysis of Quantitative Traits**; Charlesworth & Charlesworth,
  **Elements of Evolutionary Genetics**; Fisher, **The Genetical Theory of
  Natural Selection** (foundational, still hard); Carroll, Grenier &
  Weatherbee, **From DNA to Diversity**.
- *Frontier:* paleogenomics; single-cell and spatial transcriptomics; protein
  design after AlphaFold; experimental evolution at scale; minimal genomes;
  origin-of-life chemistry. Follow bioRxiv and specific labs, not pundits.
- *Contribution looks like:* a mechanism shown in a model system, an
  experiment discriminating two live hypotheses, a method others adopt.
- *Community:* SMBE; Society for the Study of Evolution; ASCB. The Cold Spring
  Harbor summer courses are the real apprenticeship if you can get in.

## Genetics

**The question it asks.** How is information carried across generations, and
how much of the difference between two organisms is attributable to it? The
hard part is the middle: genotype maps to phenotype only through development,
environment, and thousands of interacting small effects — which is why "the
gene for X" is almost always the wrong sentence shape.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Mendel's laws and the exceptions that matter: linkage, epistasis,
  imprinting, incomplete penetrance.
- Recombination — why genes travel in blocks, and how that lets you map
  anything.
- Hardy–Weinberg and the four forces: mutation, drift, migration, selection.
- Heritability is a statistic about variation in a population — not a fact
  about an individual, and not a measure of how fixed a trait is.
- Most traits are massively polygenic with tiny effect sizes. This is the
  central empirical finding of the last twenty years.
- The non-coding genome, where most disease-associated variants actually sit.
- Cheap sequencing and CRISPR changed the field's questions by changing its
  costs.
- Eugenics is not a footnote to this field's history; it is load-bearing.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- "A gene for" any behavior. Almost never one locus.
- Reading heritability as destiny or as immunity to environment.
- Epigenetics as revived Lamarckism. Transgenerational inheritance in humans
  is thin and contested; the marks themselves are ordinary regulation.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **The Gene: An Intimate History** — Siddhartha Mukherjee. The
  door because it braids mechanism with the eugenics history most popular
  accounts skip. Read the criticism of its epigenetics chapter afterward — the
  argument is itself an education in how the field polices claims.
- *Survey:* **A Brief History of Everyone Who Ever Lived** — Adam Rutherford,
  the numerate correction to genetic just-so stories, especially on ancestry
  and race. Then **Who We Are and How We Got Here** — David Reich.
- *Canon:* Mendel, **Experiments on Plant Hybridization** (1866) — free,
  short, startlingly modern; read it whole. Watson & Crick 1953 is one page,
  so read it once. **The Double Helix** — Watson alongside **Rosalind
  Franklin: The Dark Lady of DNA** — Brenda Maddox, because the pair is the
  honest version. **The Eighth Day of Creation** — Judson for the full saga.
- *Course:* Eric Lander's **Introduction to Biology — The Secret of Life**
  (MITx, also OCW 7.00x). Problem-set driven, taught by someone who led the
  human genome project; the best free course here by a wide margin. MIT OCW's
  genetics course (7.03) follows on.
- *Artifact:* 2,500 words answering a smart skeptic: "What does it mean to say
  height is 80% heritable?" You must handle twin studies, GWAS, missing
  heritability, population stratification, and why the number says nothing
  about any individual person.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* **Introduction to Genetic Analysis** — Griffiths et al. (do the
  problems; they are the course); **Principles of Population Genetics** —
  Hartl & Clark; **Human Molecular Genetics** — Strachan & Read.
- *Practice:* Get real genotype data — a consumer-test export or a public 1000
  Genomes sample — and take it through PLINK: QC, relatedness, a PCA that
  recovers continental ancestry, LD structure, and a small association test
  you then attack for confounds. Simulate drift until the coalescent feels
  physical. Wet: run a Drosophila or Arabidopsis cross two generations, count
  phenotypes, do the chi-square yourself.
- *Primary literature:* Nature Genetics, American Journal of Human Genetics,
  Genetics, PLOS Genetics. Read directly: Jinek et al. 2012 (*Science*) on
  programmable Cas9; the 1000 Genomes consortium papers, for what a resource
  paper is; the 2001 *Nature* human genome draft — the discussion, not the
  tables.
- *You've arrived when:* you can take raw genotype data to a defensible
  association result and name every way it could be wrong — stratification,
  batch effect, winner's curse, multiple testing — before anyone asks.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Hartl & Clark; Lynch & Walsh; Wakeley, **Coalescent
  Theory: An Introduction**; Yang, **Computational Molecular Evolution**;
  Charlesworth & Charlesworth.
- *Frontier:* biobank-scale statistical genetics (UK Biobank, All of Us);
  polygenic scores, their poor portability across ancestries and the ethics
  that follows; base and prime editing entering the clinic; regulatory
  genomics; single-cell eQTL mapping. This field publishes to bioRxiv and
  medRxiv first — read preprints.
- *Contribution looks like:* a statistical method others use, a causal
  mechanism for a disease locus, a resource dataset, or an architecture result
  that replicates.
- *Community:* ASHG; Genetics Society of America; Biology of Genomes (CSHL);
  ProbGen.

## Ecology

**The question it asks.** What sets where organisms live, in what numbers, and
why do some assemblages persist while others fall apart? The method problem
underneath is brutal: you usually cannot replicate, cannot control, and every
variable moves at once. Ecology is the science of extracting causal claims
from systems that refuse to hold still.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Population dynamics: exponential and logistic growth, density dependence,
  and why simple models still predict badly.
- Competition, predation, the niche, and modern coexistence theory's answer to
  why competitors don't all exclude each other.
- Energy flow and trophic structure; why food chains are short.
- Carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus cycles as the ecosystem's actual currency.
- Succession and disturbance: equilibrium is the exception, not the rule.
- Island biogeography and the species–area relationship — the quantitative
  backbone of conservation.
- Scale dependence: a pattern at one scale is noise at another, and most
  ecological disputes are really disputes about scale.
- Humans as an ecological force, with no pristine baseline to restore to.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- The "balance of nature." Systems are dynamic and path-dependent; stability
  is a claim that needs evidence.
- Confusing ecology with environmentalism. One is a science with error bars,
  the other a politics; the first does not settle the second.
- Expecting proportionate consequences from a species' removal. Keystone
  effects are wildly nonlinear.

**T3 — Literacy (~140 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **The Song of the Dodo** — David Quammen. The door because it
  teaches island biogeography as narrative; own the species–area relationship
  and you can reason about extinction, fragmentation and reserve design alone.
- *Survey:* **Ecology: From Individuals to Ecosystems** — Begon, Townsend &
  Harper. Unusually readable and genuinely complete. Lighter: **The Serengeti
  Rules** — Sean B. Carroll, on regulation and trophic cascades.
- *Canon:* **A Sand County Almanac** — Aldo Leopold; read "Thinking Like a
  Mountain" and "The Land Ethic" if nothing else. **Silent Spring** — Carson:
  ch. 1–3 and the pesticide-resistance chapters are the scientifically live
  ones. MacArthur & Wilson, **The Theory of Island Biogeography** — skim the
  mathematics, the argument is the point. Darwin's **The Formation of
  Vegetable Mould through the Action of Worms** is the original
  ecosystem-engineering study and a delight.
- *Course:* Open Yale **EEB 122** again; its second half is the ecology
  treatment. Beyond that, be honest — no free ecology MOOC is worth the hours
  against working through Begon plus Gotelli's **A Primer of Ecology** (short,
  math-forward, the fastest route to modelling competence). Spend the saved
  time outdoors.
- *Artifact:* Pick one patch of ground you can visit weekly for a season.
  Write 2,500 words of natural history: species present, energy flow,
  disturbance and land-use history, the human hand on it, and one falsifiable
  claim you could test next season.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Begon, Townsend & Harper; Gotelli, **A Primer of Ecology**; Krebs,
  **Ecological Methodology** for design; Legendre & Legendre, **Numerical
  Ecology** once datasets get wide.
- *Practice:* Not learnable indoors. Run quadrats and transects; do
  mark–recapture on something abundant (snails, ground beetles); set pitfall
  and camera traps; do point counts. Learn to key out one family properly
  until you are locally reliable in it. Then bring it into R — `vegan` for
  community data, `unmarked` for occupancy and N-mixture models, `lme4` for
  the nested structure your design will inevitably have. Join a long-running
  scheme (Breeding Bird Survey, Christmas Bird Count); identification under
  scrutiny is the training.
- *Primary literature:* Ecology, Ecology Letters, Journal of Animal Ecology,
  Oikos, Trends in Ecology & Evolution. Read directly: Paine 1966, "Food Web
  Complexity and Species Diversity" (*American Naturalist*) — the keystone
  paper, short, still the model of a clean field experiment; Hairston, Smith &
  Slobodkin 1960 (the "world is green" argument); Tilman's Cedar Creek
  biodiversity–productivity experiments.
- *You've arrived when:* you can design a sampling scheme for a real question,
  defend its power and confounds in advance, run it through a season, fit the
  right model, and state plainly what the result cannot show.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Bolker, **Ecological Models and Data in R**; Hubbell,
  **The Unified Neutral Theory of Biodiversity and Biogeography**; Chapin,
  Matson & Vitousek, **Principles of Terrestrial Ecosystem Ecology**; Leibold
  & Chase, **Metacommunity Ecology**. Chesson's coexistence reviews are the
  theoretical core.
- *Frontier:* eDNA and metabarcoding; remote sensing joined to macroecology;
  range shifts under climate change; rewilding as a tested intervention;
  microbiome ecology; near-term iterative forecasting (the Ecological
  Forecasting Initiative is where this is organizing).
- *Contribution looks like:* a long-term dataset nobody else has, a mechanism
  shown by manipulation rather than correlation, a theory unifying scattered
  patterns, or a management result that holds up.
- *Community:* Ecological Society of America; British Ecological Society; the
  LTER network; Society for Conservation Biology. Organization for Tropical
  Studies field courses are the classic immersive apprenticeship.

## Medicine & physiology

**The question it asks.** Physiology asks how a body holds itself steady
against physics and time — how coupled control loops keep pressure, volume,
solutes, temperature and glucose inside survivable bands. Medicine asks a
different question of the same material: given a broken loop, one person,
incomplete information and a clock running, what should you do? The two have
different standards of proof, and confusing them is the characteristic error.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Homeostasis and negative feedback — the organizing principle of the subject.
- Cardiovascular, respiratory and renal function as one coupled system for
  pressure, volume, gas and solute, not three organ chapters.
- Oxygen delivery and cellular energetics: the currency everything serves.
- The immune system as distributed pattern recognition with memory;
  autoimmunity as its failure mode.
- Endocrine control through hypothalamic–pituitary axes — slow, amplifying
  signals.
- Pathophysiology is broken regulation, not new machinery. You already know
  the parts.
- Pharmacology as dose, receptor and time: kinetics and dynamics before drug
  names.
- Clinical evidence: randomization, confounding, absolute vs. relative risk,
  number needed to treat, predictive value vs. sensitivity, regression to the
  mean.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Base rates. A positive test in a low-prevalence population usually means you
  do not have the disease, and this is intuitive to no one.
- Taking mechanistic plausibility for clinical benefit. The graveyard of
  reversed practices is enormous.
- Assuming more medicine means more health; overdiagnosis and screening harms
  are real and quantified.

**T3 — Literacy (~200 hrs — this domain is larger than the others)**
- *Start here:* **Complications** — Atul Gawande. The door because it is about
  the epistemics of practice: uncertainty, error, judgment. Read it before any
  mechanism, so what follows carries the right humility.
- *Survey:* **Human Physiology: An Integrated Approach** — Dee Unglaub
  Silverthorn. The best physiology text for someone working alone; it teaches
  integration rather than organ lists. Prioritize membrane transport,
  cardiovascular, respiratory, renal and endocrine — that sequence is most of
  the subject. **The Body: A Guide for Occupants** — Bryson is the enjoyable
  anatomical tour if you want one first.
- *Canon:* Claude Bernard, **An Introduction to the Study of Experimental
  Medicine** (1865) — the source of the *milieu intérieur* and still the best
  statement of what a physiological experiment is. Cannon, **The Wisdom of the
  Body** (1932) — homeostasis named. Harvey, **De Motu Cordis** (1628) —
  short, and you can follow the demonstration. Then Archie Cochrane,
  **Effectiveness and Efficiency** (1972), evidence-based medicine's founding
  document, and Lewis Thomas, **The Youngest Science**.
- *Course:* No free course rivals working through Silverthorn — don't hunt.
  Khan Academy's health and medicine sequence is good for gap-filling. For the
  evidence half, which matters as much, take **Understanding Clinical
  Research: Behind the Statistics** (University of Cape Town, Coursera).
- *Artifact:* Pick one common condition — heart failure, type 2 diabetes,
  hypertension. Write the whole chain in 2,500 words: normal physiology → what
  breaks → how it presents → what each first-line treatment does
  mechanistically → what the trials showed, in absolute risk and NNT.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* **Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology** (the standard,
  and unusually well written for its size); **Robbins and Cotran Pathologic
  Basis of Disease**; **Goodman & Gilman's** as reference, not cover to cover;
  **Gray's Anatomy for Students** (Drake) with a Netter atlas; Sackett et al.,
  **Clinical Epidemiology**.
- *Practice:* Without a clinical placement the honest routes are still real
  ones. Take an EMT-Basic certification — open to anyone, and it puts you in
  front of actual patients making actual decisions. Volunteer in an emergency
  department or hospice. Learn to take a blood pressure manually and read a
  12-lead ECG (Dubin's **Rapid Interpretation of EKG's** works). Dissect —
  sheep heart, cow eye, fetal pig kits are cheap and real. Then analyze data:
  NHANES for population physiology, MIMIC-IV for bedside critical-care data,
  both free.
- *Primary literature:* NEJM, The Lancet, JAMA, BMJ, Journal of Physiology.
  Read directly: ISIS-2 (1988, *Lancet*) for both the aspirin result and its
  famous astrological-subgroup lesson about data dredging; Ioannidis 2005,
  "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False"; the Women's Health
  Initiative hormone-therapy results against the observational studies they
  overturned.
- *You've arrived when:* you can read an unfamiliar trial cold and state
  population, comparator, absolute effect, NNT and NNH, risks of bias, and
  whether it should change practice — and separately derive from first
  principles why a drug class lowers blood pressure.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Guyton and Hall; **Boron & Boulpaep Medical Physiology**
  (the more rigorous alternative); Robbins and Cotran; **Harrison's Principles
  of Internal Medicine** as clinical reference; Rothman, Greenland & Lash,
  **Modern Epidemiology**. Be clear-eyed: T1 here means licensure or a
  research doctorate. There is no autodidact route to originality in clinical
  medicine, and that gate is not arbitrary.
- *Frontier:* cell and immune therapies; GLP-1 and metabolic medicine; aging
  biology; continuous phenotyping from wearables and multi-omics; causal
  inference from health records; diagnostic machine learning and its
  validation problem. Follow NEJM and Eric Topol's *Ground Truths* for the
  frontier, Ioannidis and Cochrane for the corrective.
- *Contribution looks like:* a mechanism established in a model system, a
  trial that moves a guideline, or a methodological correction that stops a
  practice — the last undervalued and badly needed.
- *Community:* American Physiological Society and its Summit; specialty
  societies (ACC, ADA, IDSA); the Cochrane Collaboration.

## Agriculture & food systems

**The question it asks.** How do you take a sustained caloric surplus out of
land and water without destroying the capacity to keep taking it — and how
does that surplus reach the people who need it? The field lives in the tension
between yield this season and soil, water, biodiversity and climate over
decades, with a political economy on top deciding who eats.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Soil as a living system: structure, organic matter, cation exchange, and a
  microbial economy you can manage but not command.
- Nitrogen. Legumes, then Haber–Bosch; roughly half the nitrogen in your body
  passed through an industrial reactor. That fact reorganizes the subject.
- Photosynthetic efficiency, the yield ceiling, and what "yield gap" means.
- The Green Revolution — dwarfing genes, fertilizer, irrigation — its enormous
  success and its water, nitrogen and equity costs.
- Pests and resistance: an evolutionary arms race you manage, never win.
- Water. Irrigation, aquifer depletion, salinization — the binding constraint
  in most of the world before land is.
- Livestock: feed conversion, land use, enteric methane. Know the numbers
  exactly rather than approximately.
- Famine is usually a failure of distribution and entitlement, not absolute
  scarcity. Sen's argument, and it holds up.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Organic vs. conventional as a moral binary. Measure per unit of output, not
  per hectare, and it becomes a set of tradeoffs.
- "Local means low-carbon." Transport is a small share of most footprints;
  what you eat dominates how far it travelled.
- Believing the current population could be fed without synthetic nitrogen. It
  could not; any serious proposal must say what replaces it.

**T3 — Literacy (~130 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **The Omnivore's Dilemma** — Michael Pollan. The door because
  it makes four complete food chains visible at once. Read it as journalism,
  then correct it with numbers — Pollan is excellent at seeing and unreliable
  at quantities.
- *Survey:* **Enriching the Earth** — Vaclav Smil, on what synthetic nitrogen
  did to the world, and **Should We Eat Meat?** for the livestock question
  done numerically. Smil is the antidote to every food argument conducted on
  vibes. **The Big Ratchet** — Ruth DeFries for the long-run framing.
- *Canon:* **An Agricultural Testament** — Albert Howard (the founding
  soil-and-compost text, and the origin of "organic"); **Farmers of Forty
  Centuries** — F. H. King (1911; the first four chapters); **The One-Straw
  Revolution** — Fukuoka; **Dirt: The Erosion of Civilizations** — David
  Montgomery. Then Amartya Sen, **Poverty and Famines** — the introduction and
  the Bengal 1943 chapter, where the entitlement argument is made.
- *Course:* Wageningen's edX offerings are the serious science; check the
  current catalogue rather than trusting a course code. But the strongest free
  material here is not a MOOC — it is SARE and land-grant extension
  literature, above all **Building Soils for Better Crops** — Magdoff & van
  Es, free as a PDF and genuinely excellent. Read that, and call your local
  extension office.
- *Artifact:* Pick one crop in one region. Write its whole system in 2,500
  words — agronomy, inputs, water, labor, subsidy regime, supply chain,
  environmental cost per kilogram — then model what a 30% yield shortfall or
  price spike does to each link.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* **The Nature and Properties of Soils** — Brady & Weil, the standard
  and worth owning; **Building Soils for Better Crops** — Magdoff & van Es;
  **Crop Ecology: Productivity and Management in Agricultural Systems** —
  Connor, Loomis & Cassman for quantitative agronomy.
- *Practice:* Grow at a scale that keeps books, not a windowsill. Run a
  market-garden bed sequence through a full season with records: yield per
  bed-foot, inputs, irrigation, labor hours, failures. Get a soil test and act
  on it. Build a compost pile and monitor it with a thermometer. Run
  germination tests; keep an amendment and pest log. Then work a season of
  someone else's ground — a CSA, a WWOOF placement, a research farm — because
  your own plot will never teach you scale. On data: build a yield-trend
  analysis from FAOSTAT and USDA NASS, and run a crop model (DSSAT or APSIM)
  against a site you know.
- *Primary literature:* Field Crops Research, Agronomy Journal, Agriculture
  Ecosystems & Environment, Nature Food, Soil Biology & Biochemistry. Read
  directly: Poore & Nemecek 2018 (*Science*), the global food life-cycle
  meta-analysis and probably the single most useful paper in the field; Foley
  et al. 2011, "Solutions for a Cultivated Planet" (*Nature*); Cassman 1999
  (*PNAS*) on ecological intensification and yield potential.
- *You've arrived when:* you can walk a field, read the soil and the crop,
  diagnose the limiting factor, propose a change, and quantify its cost, yield
  effect and environmental effect per kilogram of output — not per hectare.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Brady & Weil; Paul, **Soil Microbiology, Ecology and
  Biochemistry**; Connor, Loomis & Cassman, **Crop Ecology**; Chapin, Matson &
  Vitousek, **Principles of Terrestrial Ecosystem Ecology** for the
  biogeochemistry; a standard agricultural development economics text for the
  systems half.
- *Frontier:* climate-resilient and gene-edited cultivars; photosynthesis
  engineering (the RIPE project's C4 and rubisco work); nitrogen-use
  efficiency and biological nitrification inhibition; precision agriculture
  and remote sensing; alternative proteins; soil carbon measurement and its
  genuinely contested accounting. Follow the CGIAR centers (CIMMYT, IRRI),
  Our World in Data's food section, and Smil.
- *Contribution looks like:* multi-year multi-site field trials, a released
  cultivar, a crop model validated outside the site it was built on, or a
  policy analysis that survives adversarial scrutiny. Agriculture punishes
  single-season results harder than almost any field.
- *Community:* the Tri-Societies — American Society of Agronomy, Crop Science
  Society of America, Soil Science Society of America — and their joint annual
  meeting; CGIAR; SARE; the land-grant extension network, the most underused
  free expertise in this domain.
