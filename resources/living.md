# Living

The five domains of the living world, each written out at all three depth
tiers. Nothing here assumes you have studied any of it before. Read the T3
block for any domain you're bringing to literacy; the T2/T1 blocks are there
so that when a field earns promotion at an annual review, you already know
what the real curriculum looks like.

---

## Evolutionary & molecular biology

**The question it asks.** How does a process with no foresight — copying with
errors, filtered by differential survival — produce structures that look
designed? And why does every living thing, from a thermophile to a whale, run
on the same molecular toolkit? The field's central problem is connecting a
statistical process operating over populations and deep time to a chemistry of
self-copying polymers operating in milliseconds.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Natural selection as an algorithm: variation + heritability + differential
  reproduction, and nothing else required.
- Common descent and homology — why the same bones appear in a bat wing and
  your hand.
- The central dogma (DNA → RNA → protein) and its real exceptions.
- Gene regulation, not gene content, is where most form comes from; the
  evo-devo toolkit genes are shared across phyla.
- Drift and the neutral theory: most molecular change isn't adaptive.
- Levels of selection and the gene's-eye view — what "the unit of selection"
  argument is actually about.
- The major transitions: cells, endosymbiosis, sex, multicellularity,
  sociality — each a change in how information is transmitted.
- Phylogenetics: trees are inferences with error bars, not family portraits.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Treating evolution as directional or progressive. There is no ladder and no
  goal; complexity is not the trend line.
- Assuming everything is an adaptation. Much is drift, constraint, or history.
- Reading "the selfish gene" as a claim about selfish organisms. It's a claim
  about accounting, not psychology.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **The Selfish Gene** — Richard Dawkins. The door because it
  makes selection an algorithm you can run in your head; after it you can
  predict what selection will and won't build. Chapters 1–6 and 11 are the
  load-bearing ones.
- *Survey:* **Evolution: Making Sense of Life** — Carl Zimmer & Douglas Emlen.
  A textbook that reads like Zimmer's journalism; the best complete first
  pass. Pair with **The Vital Question** — Nick Lane for the energetic and
  molecular origin story.
- *Canon:* Darwin, **On the Origin of Species** (1st ed., 1859). Read ch. 1
  (variation under domestication), ch. 4 (natural selection), ch. 6
  (difficulties on the theory — Darwin is his own best critic) and ch. 14, the
  recapitulation. Skip the pigeon-fancier detail unless it delights you. Then
  Jacques Monod, **Chance and Necessity** for molecular biology's own
  philosophical statement.
- *Course:* Open Yale **EEB 122, Principles of Evolution, Ecology and
  Behavior** (Stephen Stearns) — 36 free lectures, still the best complete
  online treatment. For the molecular half, MIT OCW **7.01SC Fundamentals of
  Biology**. Robert Sapolsky's Stanford **Human Behavioral Biology** lectures
  (free on YouTube) are the famous behavioral supplement — excellent, but a
  supplement, not a substitute.
- *Artifact:* Take one specific adaptation — lactase persistence, the
  vertebrate eye, sickle-cell heterozygote advantage — and write 2,000 words
  tracing it end to end: the mutation at the molecular level, the selective
  regime, the population dynamics of its spread, and the evidence that this
  story is true rather than plausible.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* **Molecular Biology of the Cell** — Alberts et al. (the standard;
  work it, don't read it) and **Evolution** — Futuyma & Kirkpatrick, the
  standard evolution text. **Molecular Biology of the Gene** — Watson et al.
  if you want mechanism in more depth.
- *Practice:* Get your hands wet and your hands on data. Wet: a community bio
  lab (Genspace-type) or a university short course — do PCR, run a gel,
  transform bacteria with pGLO, culture something and kill it by accident.
  Dry: pull sequences from GenBank, align with MAFFT, build trees in IQ-TREE
  or MEGA and learn what a bootstrap value does and doesn't mean; browse a
  genomic region in Ensembl or UCSC; code a Wright–Fisher drift simulation
  from scratch in Python until fixation probability stops surprising you.
- *Primary literature:* Nature, Science, Cell, eLife, Molecular Biology and
  Evolution, Evolution. Read directly: Kimura 1968 (*Nature*, "Evolutionary
  rate at the molecular level" — neutral theory in three pages); Margulis 1967
  ("On the origin of mitosing cells", *J. Theor. Biol.*); Blount, Borland &
  Lenski 2008 (*PNAS*) on the citrate innovation in the long-term E. coli
  evolution experiment.
- *You've arrived when:* handed an unfamiliar gene sequence, you can find its
  homologs, build a defensible tree, say which branches are contested and why,
  and explain any Methods section in your subfield to someone else.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Alberts, **Molecular Biology of the Cell**;
  Futuyma & Kirkpatrick, **Evolution**; Lynch & Walsh, **Genetics and Analysis
  of Quantitative Traits**; Charlesworth & Charlesworth, **Elements of
  Evolutionary Genetics**; Fisher, **The Genetical Theory of Natural
  Selection** (still the foundation, still hard); Carroll, Grenier &
  Weatherbee, **From DNA to Diversity** for evo-devo.
- *Frontier:* ancient DNA and paleogenomics; single-cell and spatial
  transcriptomics; protein structure and design after AlphaFold; experimental
  evolution at scale; synthetic and minimal genomes; origin-of-life chemistry.
  Follow bioRxiv, and specific labs rather than specific people.
- *Contribution looks like:* a mechanism demonstrated in a model system, an
  experiment that discriminates between two live hypotheses, a method others
  adopt, or a phylogeny/dataset that changes what the field believes.
- *Community:* Society for Molecular Biology and Evolution (SMBE), Society for
  the Study of Evolution (Evolution meetings), ASCB. The Cold Spring Harbor
  Laboratory summer courses are the real apprenticeship if you can get in.

## Genetics

**The question it asks.** How is information carried across generations, and
how much of the difference between two organisms is attributable to it? The
hard part is the middle: genotype does not map onto phenotype directly but
through development, environment, and thousands of small effects interacting —
so "the gene for X" is almost always the wrong sentence shape.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Particulate inheritance and Mendel's laws — plus the exceptions that matter
  (linkage, epistasis, imprinting, incomplete penetrance).
- Recombination and linkage: why genes travel in blocks, and how that lets you
  map anything.
- Hardy–Weinberg and the basic population-genetic forces: mutation, drift,
  migration, selection.
- Heritability is a statistic about variation in a population, not a statement
  about an individual and not a measure of how fixed a trait is.
- Most traits are massively polygenic; effect sizes are tiny; this is the
  central empirical finding of the last twenty years.
- The non-coding genome and regulation — where most disease-associated
  variants actually sit.
- The sequencing cost curve and CRISPR: why the field's questions changed
  because its tools got cheap.
- Genetics' own history: eugenics is not a footnote, it is a load-bearing part
  of the field's story.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- "A gene for" anything behavioral. Almost never a single locus.
- Reading heritability as destiny, or as meaning a trait is unchangeable by
  environment.
- Epigenetics as revived Lamarckism. Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance
  in humans is thin and contested; the marks themselves are ordinary
  regulation.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **The Gene: An Intimate History** — Siddhartha Mukherjee. The
  door because it braids the mechanism with the eugenics history, which most
  popular accounts skip. Read the criticism of its epigenetics chapter
  afterward — that argument is itself an education in how the field polices
  claims.
- *Survey:* **A Brief History of Everyone Who Ever Lived** — Adam Rutherford,
  the numerate correction to genetic just-so stories, especially on race and
  ancestry. Then **Who We Are and How We Got Here** — David Reich for what
  ancient DNA actually showed.
- *Canon:* Mendel, **Experiments on Plant Hybridization** (1866) — free,
  short, and startlingly modern; read it in full. Watson & Crick's 1953
  *Nature* paper is one page, so read it once. Then **The Double Helix** —
  Watson, alongside **Rosalind Franklin: The Dark Lady of DNA** — Brenda
  Maddox, because the pair is the honest version. **The Eighth Day of
  Creation** — Horace Freeland Judson is the full saga if the story grips you.
- *Course:* Eric Lander's **Introduction to Biology — The Secret of Life**
  (MITx on edX, also on OCW as 7.00x). Problem-set driven, taught by someone
  who led the human genome project; the best free course in this domain by a
  wide margin. MIT OCW's genetics course (7.03) is the follow-on.
- *Artifact:* Write 2,000–3,000 words answering, for a smart skeptic: "What
  does it mean to say height is 80% heritable?" You must handle twin studies,
  GWAS, missing heritability, population stratification, and why the number
  tells you nothing about any individual person.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* **Introduction to Genetic Analysis** — Griffiths et al. (the
  standard undergraduate text; do the problems, they are the course);
  **Principles of Population Genetics** — Hartl & Clark; **Human Molecular
  Genetics** — Strachan & Read for the human side.
- *Practice:* Get real genotype data — your own export from a consumer test,
  or a public 1000 Genomes sample — and take it through PLINK: quality
  control, relatedness, a PCA that recovers continental ancestry, LD
  structure, and a small association test that you then attack for confounds.
  Simulate drift and selection until the coalescent feels physical. Wet-lab:
  run a Drosophila or Arabidopsis cross for two generations, count phenotypes,
  and do the chi-square yourself; Bio-Rad's transformation and CRISPR kits are
  legitimate home/classroom routes.
- *Primary literature:* Nature Genetics, American Journal of Human Genetics,
  Genetics, PLOS Genetics, Genome Research. Read directly: the 1000 Genomes
  Project consortium papers (for what a resource paper is); Jinek et al. 2012
  (*Science*) on programmable Cas9; the 2001 *Nature* human genome draft
  (Lander et al.) — read the discussion, not the tables.
- *You've arrived when:* you can take raw genotype data from file to a
  defensible association result, and name every way it could be wrong —
  stratification, batch effect, winner's curse, multiple testing, ascertainment
  — before anyone asks.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Hartl & Clark, **Principles of Population
  Genetics**; Lynch & Walsh, **Genetics and Analysis of Quantitative Traits**;
  Wakeley, **Coalescent Theory: An Introduction**; Yang, **Computational
  Molecular Evolution**; Charlesworth & Charlesworth, **Elements of
  Evolutionary Genetics**.
- *Frontier:* biobank-scale statistical genetics (UK Biobank, All of Us);
  polygenic scores and their poor portability across ancestries, plus the
  ethics that follows; base and prime editing moving into the clinic;
  regulatory genomics and enhancer grammar; single-cell eQTL mapping. Follow
  bioRxiv and medRxiv preprints directly — this field publishes there first.
- *Contribution looks like:* a new statistical method others use, a
  causal mechanism for a disease locus, a resource dataset, or a result about
  the genetic architecture of a trait that survives replication.
- *Community:* American Society of Human Genetics (ASHG), Genetics Society of
  America, the Biology of Genomes meeting at Cold Spring Harbor, ProbGen.

## Ecology

**The question it asks.** What sets where organisms live, in what numbers, and
why some assemblages persist while others fall apart? The methodological
problem underneath is brutal: you usually cannot replicate, cannot control,
and every variable moves at once — so ecology is the science of extracting
causal claims from systems that refuse to hold still.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Population dynamics: exponential and logistic growth, density dependence,
  and why simple models still predict badly.
- Competition, predation, and coexistence — the niche, and modern coexistence
  theory's answer to why competitors don't all exclude each other.
- Energy flow and trophic structure; why food chains are short.
- Biogeochemical cycles — carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus — as the ecosystem's
  actual currency.
- Succession and disturbance: equilibrium is the exception, not the rule.
- Island biogeography and the species–area relationship — the quantitative
  backbone of conservation.
- Scale dependence: a pattern at one scale is noise at another, and most
  ecological arguments are really arguments about scale.
- Humans as an ecological force, and the fact that there is no pristine
  baseline to restore to.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- The "balance of nature." Ecosystems are dynamic, contingent, and often
  path-dependent; stability is a claim requiring evidence.
- Confusing ecology with environmentalism. One is a science with error bars,
  the other is a politics. Knowing the first does not settle the second.
- Assuming a species' removal has proportionate consequences — keystone
  effects are wildly nonlinear in both directions.

**T3 — Literacy (~140 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **The Song of the Dodo** — David Quammen. The door because it
  teaches island biogeography as narrative, and once you own the species–area
  relationship you can reason about extinction, reserve design, and habitat
  fragmentation on your own.
- *Survey:* **Ecology: From Individuals to Ecosystems** — Begon, Townsend &
  Harper. Unusually readable for a textbook and genuinely complete; this is
  the full first pass. Lighter alternative: **The Serengeti Rules** — Sean B.
  Carroll, on regulation and trophic cascades.
- *Canon:* **A Sand County Almanac** — Aldo Leopold; read "Thinking Like a
  Mountain" and "The Land Ethic" even if you read nothing else. **Silent
  Spring** — Rachel Carson: chapters 1–3 and the chapters on pesticide
  resistance are the scientifically live ones. MacArthur & Wilson, **The
  Theory of Island Biogeography** — skim the mathematics, the argument is the
  point. Darwin's **The Formation of Vegetable Mould through the Action of
  Worms** is the original ecosystem-engineering study and a delight.
- *Course:* Open Yale **EEB 122** (Stearns) again — its second half is the
  ecology treatment. Beyond that, be honest with yourself: there is no
  flagship free ecology MOOC worth the hours against simply working through
  Begon and Gotelli's **A Primer of Ecology** (short, math-forward, and the
  fastest route to modelling competence). Spend the saved time outdoors.
- *Artifact:* Choose one patch of ground you can visit weekly for a season —
  a park, a verge, a shoreline. Write 2,500 words of natural history: the
  species present, the energy flow, the disturbance and land-use history, the
  human hand on it, and one falsifiable claim about it you could test next
  season.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Begon, Townsend & Harper, **Ecology**; Gotelli, **A Primer of
  Ecology**; Krebs, **Ecological Methodology** for field design; Legendre &
  Legendre, **Numerical Ecology** once the datasets get wide.
- *Practice:* This field is not learnable indoors. Run quadrats and transects;
  do a mark–recapture study on something abundant (snails, ground beetles);
  set pitfall traps and camera traps; do point counts. Learn to key out one
  taxon properly — pick a single family and become locally reliable in it.
  Then bring it into R: `vegan` for community data, `unmarked` for occupancy
  and N-mixture models, `lme4` for the nested structure your design will
  inevitably have. Join a long-running monitoring scheme (Breeding Bird
  Survey, Christmas Bird Count) and contribute verified records to eBird or
  iNaturalist — the discipline of identification under scrutiny is the training.
- *Primary literature:* Ecology, Ecology Letters, Journal of Animal Ecology,
  Oikos, Trends in Ecology & Evolution, Global Change Biology. Read directly:
  Paine 1966 ("Food Web Complexity and Species Diversity", *American
  Naturalist*) — the keystone species paper, short, still the model of a clean
  field experiment; Hairston, Smith & Slobodkin 1960 (the "world is green"
  argument); Tilman's Cedar Creek biodiversity–productivity experiments.
- *You've arrived when:* you can design a sampling scheme for a real question,
  defend its statistical power and its confounds in advance, collect the data
  through a full season, fit the appropriate model, and state plainly what
  your result cannot show.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Bolker, **Ecological Models and Data in R**;
  Hubbell, **The Unified Neutral Theory of Biodiversity and Biogeography**;
  Chapin, Matson & Vitousek, **Principles of Terrestrial Ecosystem Ecology**;
  Leibold & Chase, **Metacommunity Ecology**; Legendre & Legendre, **Numerical
  Ecology**. Chesson's coexistence-theory reviews are the theoretical core.
- *Frontier:* environmental DNA and metabarcoding; remote sensing joined to
  macroecology; range shifts under climate change; rewilding and trophic
  restoration as tested interventions; microbiome ecology; near-term iterative
  ecological forecasting (the Ecological Forecasting Initiative is where this
  is organizing).
- *Contribution looks like:* a long-term dataset nobody else has, a mechanism
  demonstrated by manipulation rather than correlation, a theory that unifies
  scattered patterns, or a management intervention whose effect holds up.
- *Community:* Ecological Society of America, British Ecological Society, the
  LTER network, Society for Conservation Biology. Organization for Tropical
  Studies field courses are the classic immersive apprenticeship.

## Medicine & physiology

**The question it asks.** Physiology asks how a body holds itself steady
against physics, entropy and time — how coupled control loops keep pressure,
volume, solutes, temperature and glucose within survivable bands. Medicine
asks a different question with the same material: given a broken loop, one
person, incomplete information and a clock running, what should you do? The
two questions have different standards of proof, and confusing them is the
characteristic error.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Homeostasis and negative feedback — the organizing principle of the entire
  subject.
- Cardiovascular, respiratory and renal function as one coupled system for
  pressure, volume, gas and solute control, not three organ chapters.
- Oxygen delivery and cellular energetics: the currency everything else serves.
- The immune system as distributed pattern recognition with memory, and
  autoimmunity as its failure mode.
- Endocrine control through hypothalamic–pituitary axes; hormones as slow,
  amplifying signals.
- Pathophysiology is broken regulation, not new machinery — you already know
  the parts.
- Pharmacology as dose, receptor and time: pharmacokinetics and
  pharmacodynamics before any drug name.
- Clinical evidence: randomization, confounding, absolute versus relative risk,
  number needed to treat, sensitivity/specificity versus predictive value, and
  regression to the mean.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Base rates. A positive test in a low-prevalence population usually means you
  do not have the disease, and this is not intuitive to anyone.
- Assuming mechanistic plausibility implies clinical benefit. The graveyard of
  reversed practices is enormous — antiarrhythmics after myocardial
  infarction, hormone replacement for cardiac prevention.
- Assuming more medicine means more health. Overdiagnosis and screening harm
  are real and quantified.

**T3 — Literacy (~200 hrs — this domain is larger than the others)**
- *Start here:* **Complications** — Atul Gawande. The door because it is about
  the epistemics of practice: uncertainty, error, and judgment under
  incomplete information. Read it before any mechanism, so that everything
  after has the right humility attached.
- *Survey:* **Human Physiology: An Integrated Approach** — Dee Unglaub
  Silverthorn. The best physiology text for someone working alone; it teaches
  integration rather than organ lists. Prioritize the membrane transport,
  cardiovascular, respiratory, renal and endocrine chapters — that sequence is
  most of the subject. **The Body: A Guide for Occupants** — Bill Bryson is
  the enjoyable anatomical tour if you want one first.
- *Canon:* Claude Bernard, **An Introduction to the Study of Experimental
  Medicine** (1865) — where the *milieu intérieur* comes from, and still the
  best statement of what a physiological experiment is. Walter Cannon, **The
  Wisdom of the Body** (1932) — homeostasis named. William Harvey, **De Motu
  Cordis** (1628) — short, and you can follow the demonstration. Then Archie
  Cochrane, **Effectiveness and Efficiency** (1972), the founding document of
  evidence-based medicine, and Lewis Thomas, **The Youngest Science** for the
  profession's own voice.
- *Course:* No free course rivals working through Silverthorn — say so and
  don't hunt. Khan Academy's health and medicine sequence is genuinely good
  for filling gaps and is free. For the evidence half, which matters at least
  as much, take **Understanding Clinical Research: Behind the Statistics**
  (University of Cape Town, Coursera).
- *Artifact:* Pick one common condition — heart failure, type 2 diabetes,
  hypertension. Write the whole chain in 2,500 words: normal physiology → what
  breaks → how it presents → what each first-line treatment does
  mechanistically → what the trials actually showed, in absolute risk and
  number needed to treat.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* **Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology** — the standard,
  and unusually well-written for its size. **Robbins and Cotran Pathologic
  Basis of Disease** for mechanism of disease. **Goodman & Gilman's The
  Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics** as reference, not cover-to-cover.
  **Gray's Anatomy for Students** (Drake) with a Netter atlas. For evidence:
  Sackett et al., **Clinical Epidemiology**.
- *Practice:* Without a clinical placement, the honest routes are real ones.
  Take an EMT-Basic or equivalent certification — it puts you in front of
  actual patients making actual decisions, and it is open to anyone. Volunteer
  in an emergency department or hospice. Take a Wilderness First Responder
  course. Learn to take a blood pressure manually and to read a 12-lead ECG
  (Dubin's **Rapid Interpretation of EKG's** is the classic self-teacher and
  works). Dissect — sheep heart, cow eye, fetal pig kits are real and cheap;
  a university prosection short course if one is open to the public. Then
  analyze real data: NHANES for population physiology, the MIMIC-IV critical
  care database for bedside data, both free.
- *Primary literature:* New England Journal of Medicine, The Lancet, JAMA,
  BMJ, Journal of Physiology, Circulation. Read directly: ISIS-2 (1988,
  *Lancet*) for both the aspirin result and its famous astrological-subgroup
  lesson about data dredging; Ioannidis 2005, "Why Most Published Research
  Findings Are False" (*PLoS Medicine*); the Women's Health Initiative
  hormone-therapy results set against the observational studies they
  overturned.
- *You've arrived when:* you can read an unfamiliar clinical trial cold and
  state its population, comparator, absolute effect size, number needed to
  treat and harm, its risks of bias, and whether it should change practice —
  and, separately, derive from first principles why a given drug class lowers
  blood pressure.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Guyton and Hall; **Boron & Boulpaep Medical
  Physiology** (the more rigorous alternative); **Robbins and Cotran**;
  **Harrison's Principles of Internal Medicine** as the clinical reference;
  Rothman, Greenland & Lash, **Modern Epidemiology** for the methods side.
  Be clear-eyed: T1 here effectively means licensure or a research doctorate.
  There is no autodidact path to originality in clinical medicine, and the
  gate is not arbitrary.
- *Frontier:* cell and immune therapies; incretin/GLP-1 metabolic medicine;
  the biology of aging; continuous phenotyping from wearables and multi-omics;
  causal inference from electronic health records; diagnostic machine learning
  and its validation problem. Follow NEJM and *Ground Truths* (Eric Topol) for
  the frontier, and Ioannidis and the Cochrane reviews for the corrective.
- *Contribution looks like:* a mechanism established in a model system, a
  trial that moves a guideline, or a methodological correction that stops a
  practice — the last of which is undervalued and badly needed.
- *Community:* American Physiological Society and its Summit; specialty
  societies (ACC, ADA, IDSA); the Cochrane Collaboration for evidence
  synthesis.

## Agriculture & food systems

**The question it asks.** How do you take a sustained caloric surplus out of
land and water without destroying the capacity to keep taking it — and how
does that surplus reach the people who need it? The field lives in the tension
between yield this season and soil, water, biodiversity and climate over
decades, with a political economy layered on top that decides who eats.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Soil as a living system: structure, organic matter, cation exchange
  capacity, and a microbial economy you can manage but not command.
- Nitrogen. Legumes, then Haber–Bosch; roughly half the nitrogen in your body
  passed through an industrial reactor. This single fact reorganizes the whole
  subject.
- Photosynthetic efficiency and the yield ceiling — why yields plateau and
  what "yield gap" means.
- The Green Revolution: dwarfing genes plus fertilizer plus irrigation, its
  enormous success and its water, nitrogen and equity costs.
- Pests, resistance, and integrated pest management as an evolutionary arms
  race you can only manage, never win.
- Water: irrigation, aquifer depletion, salinization — the binding constraint
  in most of the world before land is.
- Livestock: feed conversion, land use, and enteric methane; the numbers are
  stark and worth knowing exactly rather than approximately.
- Famine is usually a failure of distribution and entitlement, not absolute
  scarcity (Sen's argument, and it holds up).

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Organic versus conventional as a moral binary. Measure per unit of output,
  not per hectare, and the picture becomes a set of tradeoffs.
- "Local means low-carbon." Transport is a small share of most foods'
  footprints; what you eat dominates how far it travelled.
- Believing the current population could be fed without synthetic nitrogen. It
  could not, and any serious proposal has to say what replaces it.

**T3 — Literacy (~130 hrs)**
- *Start here:* **The Omnivore's Dilemma** — Michael Pollan. The door because
  it makes four complete food chains visible at once. Read it as journalism,
  then immediately correct it with numbers — Pollan is excellent at seeing and
  unreliable at quantities.
- *Survey:* **Enriching the Earth** — Vaclav Smil, on Haber–Bosch and what
  synthetic nitrogen did to the world; **Should We Eat Meat?** for the
  livestock question done numerically. Smil is the antidote to every food
  argument conducted on vibes. **The Big Ratchet** — Ruth DeFries is the
  broader long-run framing.
- *Canon:* **An Agricultural Testament** — Albert Howard (the founding
  soil-and-compost text, and the origin of "organic"); **Farmers of Forty
  Centuries** — F. H. King (1911, East Asian nutrient cycling — read the
  first four chapters); **The One-Straw Revolution** — Masanobu Fukuoka;
  **Dirt: The Erosion of Civilizations** — David Montgomery. Then Amartya Sen,
  **Poverty and Famines** — read the introduction and the Bengal 1943 chapter,
  which is where the entitlement argument is made.
- *Course:* Wageningen's edX offerings are the serious science; check the
  current catalogue rather than trusting a course code. But the strongest free
  material in this domain is not a MOOC — it is the SARE and land-grant
  extension literature, above all **Building Soils for Better Crops** —
  Magdoff & van Es, free as a PDF and genuinely excellent. Read that, and call
  your local extension office.
- *Artifact:* Pick one crop in one region. Write its whole system in 2,500
  words: agronomy, inputs, water, labor, subsidy regime, supply chain, and
  environmental cost per kilogram — then model what a 30% yield shortfall or
  price spike would do to each link.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* **The Nature and Properties of Soils** — Brady & Weil, the standard
  and worth owning; **Building Soils for Better Crops** — Magdoff & van Es;
  **Crop Ecology: Productivity and Management in Agricultural Systems** —
  Connor, Loomis & Cassman for the quantitative agronomy.
- *Practice:* Grow at a scale that keeps books, not a windowsill. Run a
  market-garden bed sequence through a full season with records: yield per
  bed-foot, inputs, irrigation, labor hours, failures. Get a soil test and act
  on its recommendations. Build a compost pile and monitor it with a
  thermometer. Run germination tests; keep an amendment and pest log. Then do
  a season of someone else's work — a CSA, a WWOOF placement, a research farm
  — because your own plot will never teach you scale. On the data side: build
  a yield-trend analysis from FAOSTAT and USDA NASS, and run a crop model
  (DSSAT or APSIM) against a site you know.
- *Primary literature:* Field Crops Research, Agronomy Journal, Agriculture
  Ecosystems & Environment, Nature Food, Soil Biology & Biochemistry. Read
  directly: Poore & Nemecek 2018 (*Science*), the global food life-cycle
  meta-analysis — probably the single most useful paper in the field; Foley et
  al. 2011, "Solutions for a Cultivated Planet" (*Nature*); Cassman 1999
  (*PNAS*) on ecological intensification and yield potential.
- *You've arrived when:* you can walk a field, read the soil and the crop,
  diagnose the limiting factor, propose a change, and quantify its cost, its
  yield effect, and its environmental effect per kilogram of output — not per
  hectare.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Brady & Weil, **The Nature and Properties of
  Soils**; Paul, **Soil Microbiology, Ecology and Biochemistry**; Connor,
  Loomis & Cassman, **Crop Ecology**; Chapin, Matson & Vitousek, **Principles
  of Terrestrial Ecosystem Ecology** for the biogeochemistry; a standard
  agricultural development economics text for the systems half.
- *Frontier:* climate-resilient and gene-edited cultivars; photosynthesis
  engineering (the RIPE project's C4 and rubisco work); nitrogen-use
  efficiency and biological nitrification inhibition; precision agriculture
  and remote sensing; alternative proteins; soil carbon measurement and its
  genuinely contested accounting. Follow the CGIAR centers (CIMMYT, IRRI),
  Our World in Data's food section, and Vaclav Smil.
- *Contribution looks like:* multi-year multi-site field trials, a released
  cultivar, a crop model validated outside the site it was built on, or a
  policy analysis that survives adversarial scrutiny. Agriculture punishes
  single-season results harder than almost any field.
- *Community:* the Tri-Societies — American Society of Agronomy, Crop Science
  Society of America, Soil Science Society of America — and their joint annual
  meeting; CGIAR; SARE; the land-grant extension network, which is the most
  underused free expertise in this domain.
