# Physical

Five domains at all three tiers. T3 is the literacy pass every field enters
at; T2 and T1 are what promotion costs. Substitution is fine — skipping a slot
is not.

---

## Physics

**The question it asks.** What is the smallest set of rules from which
everything that happens follows — and does such a set exist? Physics holds two
descriptions, quantum field theory and general relativity, each superb in its
own domain and mutually inconsistent where they meet. The live problem is not
more particles; it is that our two best theories cannot both be right.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Symmetry implies conservation (Noether's theorem) — the deepest structural
  fact in the subject.
- Least action, not F=ma, is the real formulation of mechanics, and it
  survives into quantum mechanics as the path integral.
- Fields are primary; forces are what fields do locally. Nothing acts at a
  distance.
- Entropy is a counting statement; the arrow of time is a claim about initial
  conditions, not about the laws.
- Quantum mechanics: amplitudes, superposition, and a measurement problem
  still genuinely unsolved.
- Relativity: no absolute simultaneity, spacetime as geometry, gravity as
  curvature.
- Effective field theory — scales decouple, which is why chemistry works
  without quantum gravity, and why science is possible at all.
- Dimensional analysis gets most of the answer before any calculation.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Quantum indeterminacy is not permission for "anything goes" — the
  wavefunction evolves deterministically and the theory constrains ruthlessly.
- Physics is not mostly colliders; most physicists work on condensed matter.
- A theory of everything would not predict a protein fold. Emergence is real.

**T3 — Literacy (~200 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Feynman, *The Character of Physical Law* (1964 Messenger
  Lectures; book and video online). Short, and it teaches what a physical law
  *is* — prerequisite for everything else.
- *Survey:* Sean Carroll, *The Biggest Ideas in the Universe*, vols. 1–2 —
  real equations, no prerequisites. With calculus, skip to Feynman.
- *Canon:* *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, free at
  feynmanlectures.caltech.edu. Don't read Vol. I straight through first time:
  Ch. 1–7, 26–27 (least time), 37–38 (quantum), 44–46 (thermodynamics, the
  ratchet). Then Einstein's 1905 "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies,"
  §1–5 — free, and astonishingly readable.
- *Course:* Susskind's Stanford *Theoretical Minimum* series, free at
  theoreticalminimum.com — Classical Mechanics, then Quantum Mechanics, then
  Special Relativity. Furthest for least pain. Walter Lewin's MIT 8.01/8.02
  lectures (YouTube; MIT pulled them from OCW in 2014) are still the best
  filmed intro to E&M.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words on why least action, not Newton's second law, is the
  real formulation of classical mechanics, and what it means that the same
  principle reappears in quantum mechanics.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Kleppner & Kolenkow, *An Introduction to Mechanics*; Purcell &
  Morin, *Electricity and Magnetism*; Taylor, *Classical Mechanics*;
  Griffiths, *Introduction to Electrodynamics* and *Introduction to Quantum
  Mechanics*; Schroeder, *An Introduction to Thermal Physics*; Boas,
  *Mathematical Methods in the Physical Sciences*, open alongside all of them.
- *Practice:* problems, with no substitute — 20–30 per chapter, realistically
  800–1,500 written out fully across the sequence. Plus an N-body integrator,
  a numerical 1D Schrödinger solver, an Ising Monte Carlo. Kleppner is where
  people stall; it is harder than it looks. Do it anyway.
- *Primary literature:* *Reviews of Modern Physics* is the best door into any
  subfield, then *Physical Review Letters* and daily arXiv. Read directly:
  Einstein (1905); Einstein, Podolsky & Rosen (1935); Bell, "On the Einstein
  Podolsky Rosen Paradox" (1964), six pages that changed the field; Anderson,
  "More Is Different" (*Science*, 1972).
- *You've arrived when:* you can derive the hydrogen spectrum from the
  Schrödinger equation without notes, and solve an unfamiliar Lagrangian
  problem from scratch.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Goldstein, *Classical Mechanics*; Jackson, *Classical
  Electrodynamics* (the field's shared trauma); Sakurai & Napolitano, *Modern
  Quantum Mechanics*; Peskin & Schroeder, *An Introduction to Quantum Field
  Theory*, with Weinberg's three-volume *The Quantum Theory of Fields* as
  reference; Wald, *General Relativity*, or Misner, Thorne & Wheeler,
  *Gravitation*; Ashcroft & Mermin, *Solid State Physics*; Landau & Lifshitz
  for the volumes you need.
- *Frontier:* quantum information as a shared language for condensed matter
  and gravity (holography, error correction); topological phases;
  gravitational-wave astronomy; muon g−2 and precision tests; quantum
  simulation. Follow arXiv hep-th and cond-mat, and PIRSA, Perimeter's free
  seminar archive.
- *Contribution looks like:* a calculation no one has done, a measurement no
  one has made, or a reframing that makes an old problem tractable.
  Experimental physics is effectively closed to solo work; theory and
  computation less so, but a PhD or real collaboration is the normal road.
- *Community:* American Physical Society (March Meeting for condensed matter,
  April for particles and astrophysics); KITP, Perimeter, Aspen Center for
  Physics.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* condensed matter — Chaikin & Lubensky, *Principles of
  Condensed Matter Physics*: how rigidity and order appear in systems whose
  parts have neither. Particle physics — Halzen & Martin, *Quarks and
  Leptons*: why the Standard Model has the exact shape it has. General
  relativity — Schutz, *A First Course in General Relativity*, then Carroll,
  *Spacetime and Geometry*: what geometry does once it carries energy.
  Statistical mechanics — Kardar, *Statistical Physics of Particles* and *…of
  Fields* (MIT OCW 8.333/8.334, free with problem sets): how many becomes
  different. Optics and photonics — Hecht, *Optics*, then Boyd, *Nonlinear
  Optics*: light as instrument rather than subject. Fluid dynamics — Acheson,
  *Elementary Fluid Dynamics*, then Batchelor: the great unfinished classical
  problem. Biophysics — Philip Nelson, *Biological Physics*: what physics
  forbids a cell. Quantum information — Nielsen & Chuang, *Quantum Computation
  and Quantum Information*, with Preskill's free Caltech notes: entanglement
  as a resource with an exchange rate. If you take only one, Strogatz's
  *Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos* pays back fastest.
- *The long canon:* Thorne & Blandford, *Modern Classical Physics* — a
  thousand pages of the physics that never gets its own course. Zee, *Quantum
  Field Theory in a Nutshell*, for the physicist's-eye view Peskin withholds.
  Anderson, *Basic Notions of Condensed Matter Physics*. Bell, *Speakable and
  Unspeakable in Quantum Mechanics* — the collected papers, funnier and
  sharper than the folklore. Peierls, *Surprises in Theoretical Physics*.
  Jaynes, *Probability Theory: The Logic of Science*, heterodox and clarifying
  about entropy. Pais, *Subtle Is the Lord* and *Inward Bound* — the best
  scientific biography and the best history of particle physics, both
  demanding. Jammer, *The Conceptual Development of Quantum Mechanics*. Kuhn,
  *Black-Body Theory and the Quantum Discontinuity*, arguing Planck did not
  mean what everyone says he meant. Galison, *Image and Logic*, on how
  experiment actually works. Hossenfelder, *Lost in Math*, with Smolin, *The
  Trouble with Physics* — take the string-theory quarrel from both sides.
  Gleick, *Genius*, for the cost of the Feynman legend.
- *Re-foundation watch:* the core is close to permanent — Lagrangian and
  Hamiltonian mechanics, Maxwell, thermodynamics, Noether, the renormalization
  group. What moves is what can be measured. Expect the next two decades to be
  reshaped by quantum simulators and error-corrected processors doing physics
  rather than demos, by gravitational-wave catalogues growing by two orders of
  magnitude, by precision tabletop work (atomic clocks, electron EDM, atom
  interferometry) hunting new physics from below rather than above, and by
  lattice QCD settling quantities that used to be experimental questions. Dark
  matter is the live bet: another twenty years of nulls would be a genuine
  re-foundation. You have gone stale when your list of open problems is the one
  you learned — if you cannot name a bound tightened in the last five years,
  you are reciting.
- *A lifetime practice:* keep a physics notebook and a bench. The notebook is
  for derivations from scratch, one a week, no references open — the hydrogen
  atom, the harmonic oscillator, the Lorentz transformation, a Fermi estimate
  of something you saw that day. Thirty years of that is the difference between
  having studied physics and knowing it. The bench is for measurement: *g* to
  three figures, the speed of light with a microwave and cheese, Planck's
  constant from LEDs, and the MIT CosmicWatch muon detector — whose count rate
  you then log against barometric pressure for years, watching the atmosphere
  breathe.
- *Rabbit holes:* Maxwell's demon and Landauer's principle, where
  thermodynamics turns out to be about information. The Casimir effect and
  what it does and does not prove about vacuum energy. The Berry phase — a
  geometric fact hiding in every quantum system. The physics of musical
  instruments, which is real acoustics with an audible answer key.
  Sonoluminescence: a bubble that emits light and nobody fully agrees why.

## Cosmology & astronomy

**The question it asks.** How did a nearly uniform hot beginning produce a
universe with galaxies, stars, and planets — and what is the ~95% of its
contents that is not atoms? Astronomy's parallel problem is harder than it
sounds: reconstruct the life histories of objects you can never touch, visit,
or repeat, from arriving light alone.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Space expands; cosmological redshift is not Doppler, and there is no center.
- The CMB is a photograph of the universe at 380,000 years; its acoustic peaks
  encode geometry, baryon fraction, and dark matter density.
- Dark matter is overdetermined — rotation curves, lensing, cluster dynamics,
  the CMB, and structure formation each demand it independently.
- Dark energy: the 1998 supernova result, corroborated by CMB and baryon
  acoustic oscillations.
- Stellar nucleosynthesis — everything past lithium has a stellar or
  compact-object origin. The HR diagram organizes the field.
- Collapse and its endpoints: white dwarfs, the Chandrasekhar limit, neutron
  stars, black holes.
- The distance ladder is fragile; the Hubble tension is live and unresolved.
- Inflation explains flatness, the horizon problem, and the fluctuation
  spectrum — and is still a framework, not a confirmed theory.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- The Big Bang was not an explosion *in* space and had no location.
- Dark matter is not a fudge factor; a fudge factor would not fit five
  independent datasets with one number.
- Professional astronomy is almost never eyepieces — it is photometry,
  spectroscopy, and pipelines.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Sagan, *Cosmos* (book or the 1980 series). Still the best
  invitation ever made, and it models reasoning rather than reporting results.
- *Survey:* OpenStax *Astronomy 2e* — free, complete, genuinely good; skim it
  whole in a month. Pair with Kip Thorne, *Black Holes and Time Warps*.
- *Canon:* Weinberg, *The First Three Minutes*. Then two papers of two pages
  each: Hubble (1929), "A Relation Between Distance and Radial Velocity Among
  Extra-Galactic Nebulae," and Penzias & Wilson (1965), "A Measurement of
  Excess Antenna Temperature at 4080 Mc/s." The modern picture is born in four
  pages.
- *Course:* Open Yale ASTR 160, *Frontiers and Controversies in Astrophysics*
  (Charles Bailyn) — free, uses real arithmetic, honest about what is
  unsettled. Then MIT OCW 8.282J, *Introduction to Astronomy*.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words on how we know the age, composition, and geometry of
  the universe — naming the measurements and their error bars, not the
  conclusions.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Carroll & Ostlie, *An Introduction to Modern Astrophysics* — the
  standard undergraduate brick, lived in rather than read linearly. Ryden,
  *Introduction to Cosmology*, is short, clean, and the best at this level.
- *Practice:* the field hands you its data. Reduce your own images (small
  telescope with CCD, or SDSS SkyServer, MAST, ESO archives). Do aperture
  photometry on a variable star, build a light curve, submit to the AAVSO.
  Classify galaxy spectra by hand. Fit the Pantheon supernova compilation in
  Python with astropy and recover acceleration yourself. Target four to six
  finished analyses on real data.
- *Primary literature:* *The Astrophysical Journal*, *MNRAS*, *Astronomy &
  Astrophysics*, arXiv astro-ph daily. Read directly: Burbidge, Burbidge,
  Fowler & Hoyle (1957), "Synthesis of the Elements in Stars" (B²FH); Rubin &
  Ford (1970) on the Andromeda rotation curve; Riess et al. (1998) and
  Perlmutter et al. (1999) on cosmic acceleration.
- *You've arrived when:* you can take a raw FITS frame through calibration to
  a magnitude with a defensible uncertainty, and read an astro-ph abstract and
  say what would change if the result held.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Binney & Tremaine, *Galactic Dynamics*; Peebles,
  *Principles of Physical Cosmology*, with Dodelson & Schmidt, *Modern
  Cosmology*, for the calculational treatment; Kippenhahn & Weigert, *Stellar
  Structure and Evolution*; Rybicki & Lightman, *Radiative Processes in
  Astrophysics* (the one everyone uses); Shapiro & Teukolsky, *Black Holes,
  White Dwarfs, and Neutron Stars*.
- *Frontier:* JWST high-redshift galaxies; gravitational-wave populations from
  LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA; Rubin Observatory's LSST; Euclid and DESI; the Hubble
  tension; 21cm cosmology; exoplanet atmospheres. Follow astro-ph and the AAS
  journals.
- *Contribution looks like:* astronomy is unusually open to outsiders —
  archives are public and amateurs still co-author real papers on transit
  timing, occultations, supernova discovery, and variable stars. Original work
  is a new measurement, a new analysis of existing survey data, or a model
  predicting something observable.
- *Community:* American Astronomical Society, the IAU, the AAVSO, the Royal
  Astronomical Society. Zooniverse is a legitimate front door.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* stellar astrophysics — Prialnik, *An Introduction to the
  Theory of Stellar Structure and Evolution*: why a ball of gas has a life
  story at all. Galaxy formation — Mo, van den Bosch & White, *Galaxy
  Formation and Evolution*: how baryons behave inside a dark matter skeleton.
  Planetary science — de Pater & Lissauer, *Planetary Sciences*: the one
  branch where you get samples. Exoplanets — Perryman, *The Exoplanet
  Handbook*, with Seager, *Exoplanet Atmospheres*: what you can learn from a
  0.01% dip in brightness. The interstellar medium and star formation —
  Draine, *Physics of the Interstellar and Intergalactic Medium*: where the
  next generation of stars is decided. High-energy astrophysics — Longair,
  *High Energy Astrophysics*: accretion, jets, and everything that ends
  badly. Gravitational-wave astronomy — Maggiore, *Gravitational Waves*, two
  volumes: a channel opened in 2015 and still mostly unexploited.
  Astrostatistics — Ivezić, Connolly, VanderPlas & Gray, *Statistics, Data
  Mining, and Machine Learning in Astronomy*: the survival skill for the
  survey era.
- *The long canon:* Peebles, *Cosmology's Century* — the field's history by
  the person who built much of it. Overbye, *Lonely Hearts of the Cosmos*, the
  same story as human comedy, and still the best book about how cosmology is
  actually done. Harwit, *Cosmic Discovery*, whose thesis is that discoveries
  follow instruments rather than theories — test it against everything since.
  Koestler, *The Sleepwalkers*, brilliant and unreliable on Kepler; correct it
  with Gingerich, *The Book Nobody Read*, and Kuhn, *The Copernican
  Revolution*. Chandrasekhar, *Truth and Beauty*, on aesthetics as method.
  Levin, *Black Hole Blues and Other Songs from Outer Space*, on forty years
  of building LIGO before it worked. Levesque, *The Last Stargazers*, on
  observing as a craft that is disappearing. Ferris, *Coming of Age in the
  Milky Way*, for the whole arc. Hirshfeld, *Parallax*, on the two-century
  fight to measure one stellar distance. Burnham's *Celestial Handbook*, three
  volumes of obsessive object-by-object description — the data are dated, the
  eye is not. Hoyle's *Home Is Where the Wind Blows*, the losing side argued
  by its most formidable holder.
- *Re-foundation watch:* the most instrument-driven field here, and the
  instruments are all arriving at once. Rubin Observatory's LSST turns the sky
  into a decade-long movie with millions of alerts a night; Euclid, DESI, Roman
  and SKA reshape cosmology's error bars; JWST has already forced a rethink of
  early galaxy assembly; pulsar timing arrays opened the nanohertz band in
  2023. Assume that in twenty years the Hubble tension has either resolved or
  become new physics, that the exoplanet census includes atmospheric chemistry
  for rocky worlds, and that dark matter is detected or effectively abandoned
  as a WIMP. Durable: stellar structure, radiative transfer, orbital dynamics,
  spectroscopy, the logic of the distance ladder. The stale signal is
  numerical — if the values you quote for H₀, the exoplanet count or the merger
  tally are the ones you first learned, you are out of date by construction.
- *A lifetime practice:* keep an observing log, on paper, for decades. Not
  pretty pictures — measurements. Estimate variable star magnitudes and submit
  them to the AAVSO, where a fifty-year light curve assembled by amateurs is
  the normal unit of contribution. Image the same field annually and watch
  Barnard's Star walk across it. Track sunset azimuth from one fixed spot
  until you have built your own horizon calendar, the observation that founded
  the science. Sketch what you see at the eyepiece even in the era of
  cameras; drawing forces attention that photography lets you skip.
- *Rabbit holes:* the transits of Venus and the eighteenth-century campaign to
  measure the astronomical unit — the first global scientific collaboration.
  The Antikythera mechanism. The 1967 discovery of pulsars and how nearly it
  was mistaken for signals. SN 1987A, whose neutrinos arrived three hours
  before the light. The Maunder minimum and what a quiet Sun did to Europe.

## Chemistry

**The question it asks.** Why does matter have the properties it has, and how
do you make substances that do not yet exist? The situation is peculiar:
physics supplies the exact equation, and that equation is uncomputable for
anything bigger than a few atoms. Chemistry therefore runs on a hierarchy of
models that are all strictly wrong and reliably predictive — and knowing each
one's domain of validity *is* the expertise.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- The periodic table falls out of orbital structure: periodicity, effective
  nuclear charge, electronegativity.
- The ladder of bonding models — Lewis, VSEPR, hybridization, molecular
  orbital theory. Each is a better approximation and a worse intuition pump.
- Thermodynamics says what is favorable, kinetics what is fast; catalysts
  change rates, never equilibria.
- Equilibrium, Le Chatelier, and pKa — the most useful number in practical
  chemistry.
- Mechanism and the curly arrow: nucleophile, electrophile, electron flow.
  This is chemistry's actual language, not its notation.
- You don't know what you made until you've characterized it — NMR, mass
  spectrometry, IR, X-ray crystallography.
- Stereochemistry and chirality decide whether a drug works or poisons.
- Entropy-driven organization: the hydrophobic effect, micelles,
  self-assembly.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- "Chemical" versus "natural" is not a distinction chemistry recognizes.
- Chemistry is not memorization. People stall in organic precisely by
  memorizing reactions instead of learning arrow-pushing — reactions are
  infinite, mechanisms are about a dozen.
- A balanced equation is bookkeeping; it says nothing about what molecules do.

**T3 — Literacy (~180 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Primo Levi, *The Periodic Table* — chemistry as a way of
  seeing, by a working chemist who was also one of the century's great
  writers. If you read two chapters, read "Iron" and "Carbon."
- *Survey:* Peter Atkins, *Chemistry: A Very Short Introduction*, then
  *Atkins' Molecules*. He wrote the standard physical chemistry text and
  compresses without lying. Sam Kean's *The Disappearing Spoon* is the fun
  supplement, not the substitute.
- *Canon:* Pauling, *The Nature of the Chemical Bond* — read Ch. 1–3 and the
  resonance chapters, skip the crystal-radii tables. It built modern chemistry
  and is still readable. Pauling's *General Chemistry* (Dover, cheap) is the
  same mind writing a textbook.
- *Course:* MIT OCW 5.111, *Principles of Chemical Science* (Catherine
  Drennan) — full video, among the best-taught on OCW. Then MIT OCW 5.12,
  *Organic Chemistry I*.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words tracing Haber–Bosch end to end — the thermodynamics
  that makes it hard, the catalysis that makes it possible, the real
  temperatures and pressures, and the fact that roughly half the nitrogen
  atoms in your body passed through it.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Clayden, Greeves & Warren, *Organic Chemistry* — arguably the best
  science textbook of the last thirty years in any field; start here even if
  you think you want physical chemistry. Then Atkins & de Paula, *Physical
  Chemistry*; Housecroft & Sharpe, *Inorganic Chemistry*; Harris,
  *Quantitative Chemical Analysis*.
- *Practice:* work Clayden cover to cover with the solutions manual — 30-plus
  problems a chapter, about a year of evenings. Then bench work, and be honest
  that this is the hardest domain here to do at home legitimately: take a
  community-college lab course or volunteer in one. Minimum credible set: one
  multi-step synthesis, one column chromatography, one recrystallization, and
  a product you characterized yourself by NMR. With no lab reachable,
  substitute computation — Avogadro with Psi4 or ORCA (both free), checked
  against literature values.
- *Primary literature:* *JACS*, *Angewandte Chemie*, *Nature Chemistry*,
  *Organic Letters*; *Chemical Reviews* to enter a subfield. Read directly:
  Pauling (1931, *JACS*) on the chemical bond; Woodward & Hoffmann on
  conservation of orbital symmetry (1965 *JACS* papers, or the 1969
  *Angewandte* review); Watson & Crick (1953), a structural chemistry paper
  before it is anything else.
- *You've arrived when:* given an unfamiliar transformation you can propose a
  mechanism with arrows and defend it, and given an unknown's ¹H NMR, IR, and
  mass spectrum you can assign the structure.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Anslyn & Dougherty, *Modern Physical Organic Chemistry*;
  Carey & Sundberg, *Advanced Organic Chemistry*, Parts A and B; Szabo &
  Ostlund, *Modern Quantum Chemistry* (or Levine, *Quantum Chemistry*, for a
  gentler route); McQuarrie, *Statistical Mechanics*; Cotton & Wilkinson,
  *Advanced Inorganic Chemistry*.
- *Frontier:* machine-learned potentials and retrosynthesis planning;
  automated and flow synthesis; photoredox and electrocatalysis; CO₂
  reduction; computational enzyme design; ultrafast and single-molecule
  spectroscopy. Follow *Nature Chemistry* and *C&EN*.
- *Contribution looks like:* a molecule nobody has made, a reaction or
  catalyst nobody has run, a new measurement method, or a mechanism resolved.
  This needs a lab — chemistry is the least autodidact-friendly domain on this
  page. Computational chemistry is the real exception and needs only a
  machine.
- *Community:* American Chemical Society national meetings, the Royal Society
  of Chemistry, and Gordon Research Conferences, where unpublished work is
  actually discussed.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* total synthesis — Nicolaou & Sorensen, *Classics in Total
  Synthesis*, with Corey & Cheng, *The Logic of Chemical Synthesis*: how you
  plan a twenty-step route backwards. Organometallic chemistry and catalysis —
  Crabtree, *The Organometallic Chemistry of the Transition Metals*: how metals
  make impossible bonds routine. Electrochemistry — Bard & Faulkner,
  *Electrochemical Methods*: the discipline behind batteries, corrosion and
  most of decarbonisation. Polymer chemistry — Odian, *Principles of
  Polymerization*, then Rubinstein & Colby, *Polymer Physics*: why a chain
  behaves unlike anything else. Biochemistry and chemical biology — Berg,
  Tymoczko & Stryer, *Biochemistry*: catalysis a hundred million times better
  than yours. Spectroscopy as its own craft — Silverstein, Webster & Kiemle,
  *Spectrometric Identification of Organic Compounds*: reading structure off
  signals. Supramolecular chemistry — Steed & Atwood, *Supramolecular
  Chemistry*: bonding that isn't bonding. Computational chemistry — Jensen,
  *Introduction to Computational Chemistry*: the branch you can genuinely
  master alone with a laptop, and the only escape from the lab problem.
- *The long canon:* Roald Hoffmann, *The Same and Not the Same* — a Nobel
  laureate on the dualities the field lives inside; the best writing about what
  chemistry *is*. Oliver Sacks, *Uncle Tungsten*, chemistry as a childhood.
  Atkins, *The Second Law*, still the clearest exposition of entropy in print.
  Pauling & Wilson, *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics with Applications to
  Chemistry* (Dover, cheap), the bridge book. Eric Scerri, *The Periodic
  Table: Its Story and Its Significance*, the scholarly account, which will
  unsettle what you think the table is. Bensaude-Vincent & Stengers, *A
  History of Chemistry*, and Ihde, *The Development of Modern Chemistry*, for
  the long arc. Lavoisier, *Elements of Chemistry* (1789), free, where
  quantitative chemistry begins. Philip Ball, *H2O: A Biography of Water*, on
  the substance everyone assumes is simple. John D. Clark, *Ignition!*, a free
  history of liquid rocket propellants — funny, alarming, and the best account
  of working with chemicals that hate you. Nicolaou & Montagnon, *Molecules
  That Changed the World*. Djerassi, *The Pill, Pygmy Chimps, and Degas'
  Horse*. Derek Lowe's *In the Pipeline* (free, running since 2002) is the
  field's honest daily commentary.
- *Re-foundation watch:* the mechanistic core is durable — thermodynamics,
  kinetics, pKa, arrow-pushing, stereochemistry and separations will read the
  same in forty years. What will not is how you *find* and *check* a molecule.
  Machine-learned potentials and retrosynthesis planners went from curiosity to
  default in a few years; automated and flow synthesis are changing what one
  person can execute; microcrystal electron diffraction solves structures from
  powder where a crystal was once mandatory; benchtop NMR has put
  characterisation on ordinary desks. Assume that in twenty years route
  planning is machine-first and the chemist's job is judging what to trust. The
  check on return: take a target you once planned by hand, run it through the
  current tools, and see whether their output surprises you.
- *A lifetime practice:* two things, weekly. First, a bound lab notebook kept
  properly for decades — dated, numbered, never torn out — even for kitchen
  chemistry. Second, one unknown a week: take a published ¹H and ¹³C NMR, IR
  and mass spectrum for a compound you have not seen, assign the structure
  cold, and check. Do that for thirty years and structure elucidation becomes
  reflex. For hands, pick a craft that is real chemistry with a legal home
  supply chain — natural dyeing and mordanting, pigment grinding, soap and
  saponification, cyanotype and silver-gelatin photography, fermentation,
  electroplating. Cook seriously; it is the only daily chemistry lab most
  people will ever run.
- *Rabbit holes:* the history of pigments — Prussian blue, discovered by
  accident, and mauveine, which created the chemical industry. The Maillard
  reaction, which nobody has fully mapped. The ferrocene structure story, where
  the answer was a sandwich and no one saw it. Thalidomide and what chirality
  cost. The Oklo natural fission reactor in Gabon, which ran for a few hundred
  thousand years without anyone's help.

## Earth science & climate

**The question it asks.** How does a rocky planet with a molten interior, an
ocean, and a thin atmosphere self-organize into the system we live inside,
across timescales from seconds to billions of years? Climate science is the
subfield forced to answer on a deadline: how much warming per unit of CO₂, and
how would you know, given one planet and no control group?

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Plate tectonics as the unifying theory — ridges, subduction, the rock cycle.
  Solid-earth science is conceptually post-1965.
- Deep time and how it is read: stratigraphy, isotopes, radiometric dating;
  uniformitarianism as method, with catastrophes admitted back in.
- The carbon cycle on two clocks — fast (biosphere and ocean, years to
  centuries) and slow (silicate weathering, the planet's thermostat, hundreds
  of thousands of years).
- The greenhouse effect done properly: it raises the *altitude* at which the
  planet radiates to space. It is not a blanket trapping heat.
- Feedbacks — water vapour (strong, positive), ice–albedo, and clouds, the
  dominant remaining uncertainty. Climate sensitivity is the number the
  argument hangs from.
- Circulation: Coriolis, Hadley cells, the thermohaline conveyor, ENSO.
- Paleoclimate: ice cores, Milankovitch forcing, and the PETM as the nearest
  analogue to what we are doing.
- Weather is an initial-value problem that goes chaotic in about two weeks;
  climate is a boundary-value problem and is predictable. Different questions.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- "The climate has always changed" is true and is the *reason* for concern —
  the paleo record is how sensitivity was constrained, and it shows the
  current rate is anomalous.
- Models are not the primary evidence; the radiative physics dates to
  Arrhenius in 1896, and the instrumental record is model-independent.
- Geology is not rock identification. It is inferring a history from a
  fragmentary, deformed, partly erased record.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* John McPhee, *Annals of the Former World*; read "Basin and
  Range" first. Nothing else installs deep time in your bones, and it won a
  Pulitzer for good reason.
- *Survey:* David Archer, *Global Warming: Understanding the Forecast* —
  short, quantitative, honest about uncertainty. For solid earth, Marcia
  Bjornerud's *Timefulness* or Walter Alvarez's *T. rex and the Crater of
  Doom*.
- *Canon:* the IPCC AR6 Working Group I report, free. Read the Summary for
  Policymakers to see what was agreed, then the Technical Summary, where the
  science lives — the SPM is negotiated line by line and the TS is not.
  Historical: Lyell, *Principles of Geology*, Vol. 1, Ch. 1–4 (what Darwin
  read on the *Beagle*), and Arrhenius (1896), "On the Influence of Carbonic
  Acid in the Air upon the Temperature of the Ground."
- *Course:* David Archer's *Global Warming: The Science and Modeling of
  Climate Change* (University of Chicago, free on Coursera) — you run real
  models rather than watch. His free models at forecast.uchicago.edu,
  especially MODTRAN, let you change CO₂ and watch the outgoing spectrum
  change. For solid earth, MIT OCW 12.001, *Introduction to Geology*.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words deriving the greenhouse effect from first principles
  — blackbody balance, why the surface exceeds the effective temperature, what
  doubling CO₂ does before feedbacks, and exactly where the uncertainty lives.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Marshall & Plumb, *Atmosphere, Ocean and Climate Dynamics*;
  Hartmann, *Global Physical Climatology*; Ruddiman, *Earth's Climate: Past
  and Future*; Grotzinger & Jordan, *Understanding Earth*; Turcotte &
  Schubert, *Geodynamics*, for the solid-earth half.
- *Practice:* the most data-open field here. Rebuild the global temperature
  anomaly record from raw GHCN station data in Python and confront every
  homogenization choice yourself. Code a 1D radiative–convective model. Pull
  CMIP6 output from ESGF and analyze it with xarray. Decompose the Keeling
  curve into trend and seasonal cycle. Then get outdoors: log a stratigraphic
  section, map an outcrop, learn to read a geologic map — a field-methods
  course is worth the money. Target four to six finished analyses plus one
  field project.
- *Primary literature:* *Nature Geoscience*, *Journal of Climate*, *JGR*
  (Atmospheres and Solid Earth), *GRL*, *Geology*. Read directly: Hess (1962),
  "History of Ocean Basins," which is seafloor spreading; Keeling (1960) on
  atmospheric CO₂; Manabe & Wetherald (1967), "Thermal Equilibrium of the
  Atmosphere with a Given Distribution of Relative Humidity," the paper behind
  the 2021 Nobel; Hansen et al. (1981) in *Science*, whose projections you can
  now grade.
- *You've arrived when:* you can reconstruct a global temperature series from
  raw station data and justify every adjustment, and read a geologic map cold
  and narrate the region's history from it.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Pierrehumbert, *Principles of Planetary Climate* — the
  serious one, and it makes the physics general rather than Earth-specific;
  Vallis, *Atmospheric and Oceanic Fluid Dynamics*; Holton & Hakim, *An
  Introduction to Dynamic Meteorology*; Sarmiento & Gruber, *Ocean
  Biogeochemical Dynamics*; White, *Geochemistry*.
- *Frontier:* cloud feedback and convective aggregation; kilometre-scale
  storm-resolving global models; ML emulators and downscaling; extreme-event
  attribution; carbon dioxide removal and its verification; tipping elements
  such as AMOC and the West Antarctic ice sheet; climate physics applied to
  exoplanets.
- *Contribution looks like:* a new observational dataset, a mechanism
  explained, a model component improved, or the paleo record extended. Because
  data and model output are public, the field is unusually reachable from
  outside.
- *Community:* AGU Fall Meeting (the largest earth-science gathering in the
  world), EGU, the American Meteorological Society, the Geological Society of
  America. RealClimate for commentary by working scientists.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* structural geology — Fossen, *Structural Geology*: how rock
  deforms, and how to read the deformation backwards. Petrology and mineralogy
  — Winter, *Principles of Igneous and Metamorphic Petrology*, with Klein &
  Dutrow, *Manual of Mineral Science*: a rock is a thermodynamic record of its
  own history. Sedimentology and stratigraphy — Nichols, *Sedimentology and
  Stratigraphy*: reconstructing environments from grains. Geomorphology —
  Anderson & Anderson, *Geomorphology: The Mechanics and Chemistry of
  Landscapes*: why landscapes have the shapes they have. Seismology — Stein &
  Wysession, *An Introduction to Seismology, Earthquakes, and Earth
  Structure*: the only direct look inside the planet. Physical oceanography —
  Talley, Pickard, Emery & Swift, *Descriptive Physical Oceanography*: the
  slow half of the climate system. Glaciology — Cuffey & Paterson, *The
  Physics of Glaciers*: the fastest-moving uncertainty in sea level.
  Geochemistry — Albarède, *Geochemistry: An Introduction*: isotopes as clocks
  and tracers. Paleobiology — Benton & Harper, *Introduction to Paleobiology
  and the Fossil Record*: reading biology out of a fragmentary archive.
- *The long canon:* Spencer Weart, *The Discovery of Global Warming* — free
  online from the American Institute of Physics, continuously updated, and the
  best history of any science on this page. Naomi Oreskes, *The Rejection of
  Continental Drift*, on how a correct theory was resisted for fifty years for
  reasons that were not stupid; then Oreskes & Conway, *Merchants of Doubt*.
  Wegener, *The Origin of Continents and Oceans*, in the man's own words.
  Richard Fortey, *Earth: An Intimate History*, and *Trilobite!* — geology
  written by someone who loves it. Andrew Knoll, *Life on a Young Planet*, on
  the first three billion years. Peter Brannen, *The Ends of the World*, the
  mass extinctions as climate experiments already run. Imbrie & Imbrie, *Ice
  Ages: Solving the Mystery*, a model of how a hypothesis gets confirmed by
  data nobody expected. Broecker, *The Great Ocean Conveyor*. Simon Winchester,
  *The Map That Changed the World*, on William Smith and the invention of
  stratigraphy. Bjornerud, *Reading the Rocks*. Ruddiman, *Plows, Plagues, and
  Petroleum*, whose early-Anthropocene hypothesis is contested and worth
  arguing with. Mike Hulme, *Why We Disagree About Climate Change*, which takes
  the disagreement seriously as something other than ignorance. For the
  contrarian case at its most competent, Koonin's *Unsettled* and Pielke's work
  on disaster trends — read them alongside the published responses, not instead
  of them; the exercise is learning to grade an argument.
- *Re-foundation watch:* the physics is durable — radiative transfer,
  geostrophy, isostasy, superposition, radiometric decay. Almost everything
  about *prediction* is in motion. Machine-learned weather models went from
  novelty to operational parity in about three years; kilometre-scale
  storm-resolving climate models are arriving and will settle or reframe the
  cloud feedback question that dominates sensitivity; methane and CO₂
  satellites are making emissions independently verifiable for the first time;
  InSAR and continuous GNSS turned deformation into a monitored quantity.
  Expect sensitivity, sea-level projections and attribution methods all to have
  moved when you return, and the emissions scenarios you learned to have been
  retired. The stale signal is citing a scenario as business-as-usual, or
  quoting a sensitivity range without knowing which assessment produced it.
- *A lifetime practice:* become an observer of record. Run a weather station
  and report daily precipitation to CoCoRaHS or your national network — a
  thirty-year single-site series is a real dataset and almost nobody has one.
  Keep a repeat-photography station: one fixed spot, one bearing, one photo a
  year, of a glacier terminus, a shoreline, a gully, a treeline. Run a Raspberry
  Shake or similar seismometer and maintain your own earthquake catalogue.
  Keep a field notebook with sketched sections and measured strikes and dips
  from every trip; geologists are made by accumulated outcrops, not by books.
- *Rabbit holes:* J Harlen Bretz and the Missoula floods — thirty years of
  being called a catastrophist and then being right. The Messinian salinity
  crisis, when the Mediterranean dried out. Snowball Earth and the argument
  about how the planet got out. The Carrington event of 1859 and what a repeat
  would do to a wired civilisation. Tambora, 1815, and the year without a
  summer.

## Materials science

**The question it asks.** Why do identical atoms arranged differently give you
graphite or diamond — and can you design a structure, from atoms to
millimetres, that delivers properties you specify in advance? The organizing
insight is that real materials are governed by their *defects*, not their
perfect lattices, and that everything flows along one chain: processing →
structure → properties → performance.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- The processing–structure–properties–performance tetrahedron: every question
  in the field is a link in that chain.
- Crystal structure and close packing — then the correction that matters.
  Vacancies, dislocations, and grain boundaries carry most real behaviour; a
  perfect crystal would be ~100× stronger than the metal you actually hold.
- Plasticity *is* dislocation motion. Every strengthening mechanism — work
  hardening, grain refinement (Hall–Petch), solid solution, precipitation — is
  another way to pin dislocations.
- Phase diagrams and the lever rule; iron–carbon is the most consequential
  chart in the history of engineering.
- Kinetics versus equilibrium: diffusion, nucleation and growth, TTT diagrams,
  quenching and tempering. Glass is a liquid that ran out of time.
- Failure: Griffith's criterion, stress concentration, fracture toughness,
  fatigue. Things break from cracks, not from being insufficiently strong.
- Band structure — why metals conduct and semiconductors are controllable.
- Composites and hierarchical natural materials (bone, wood, nacre) reach
  property combinations no monolithic material can.
- Ashby maps turn property space into a navigable design space.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- "Stronger" is not one property. Strength, stiffness, hardness, and toughness
  are distinct and trade against each other — glass is strong and useless. The
  strong/tough confusion is the classic tell.
- A *Nature* paper is not a product; materials take one to three decades from
  lab to production, and most never arrive.
- Nanotechnology is not tiny machines. It is what happens when a dimension
  shrinks to meet a physical length scale.

**T3 — Literacy (~130 hrs)**
- *Start here:* J.E. Gordon, *Structures: Or Why Things Don't Fall Down* —
  funny, physical, and better on fracture than most textbooks. Then his *The
  New Science of Strong Materials*, which created the genre.
- *Survey:* Mark Miodownik, *Stuff Matters* — ten materials, superbly told. If
  you want the real thing early, Ashby & Jones, *Engineering Materials 1*, is
  a textbook that reads like a book.
- *Canon:* Cyril Stanley Smith, *A Search for Structure* — essays by the
  metallurgist who worked on the Manhattan Project and became a historian of
  materials and art. Read the title essay and "Art, Technology and Science";
  his argument is that materials advanced through aesthetics and craft long
  before theory arrived.
- *Course:* MIT OCW 3.091, *Introduction to Solid-State Chemistry* (Donald
  Sadoway) — one of the great lecture performances on OCW, and it grounds the
  atomic basis properly. Then MIT OCW 3.032, *Mechanical Behavior of
  Materials*.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words on steel down the full chain: the iron–carbon phase
  diagram, what quenching and tempering do to microstructure, and why a razor
  blade, a bridge cable, and a sword need three different microstructures from
  nearly the same alloy system.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Callister & Rethwisch, *Materials Science and Engineering: An
  Introduction* — the universal undergraduate text; Ashby & Jones,
  *Engineering Materials* 1 and 2; Porter, Easterling & Sherif, *Phase
  Transformations in Metals and Alloys*; Kittel, *Introduction to Solid State
  Physics*, for the electronic side; Ashby, *Materials Selection in Mechanical
  Design*, for how the field is actually used.
- *Practice:* metallography is the most accessible genuine lab work in any
  domain on this page, and you can do it at home. Mount, polish, etch, and
  image steel under an inexpensive metallurgical microscope; heat-treat and
  quench coupons and measure the hardness change. Get tensile testing at a
  makerspace or community college. Computationally: run DFT with Quantum
  ESPRESSO, compute phase diagrams with pycalphad, mine the Materials Project
  database (free, enormous). Target twenty to thirty characterized samples
  with documented thermal histories, plus two or three computational studies.
- *Primary literature:* *Acta Materialia*, *Nature Materials*, *Advanced
  Materials*, *Scripta Materialia*; *Progress in Materials Science* for
  reviews. Read directly: Griffith (1921), "The Phenomena of Rupture and Flow
  in Solids," the origin of fracture mechanics; Hall (1951) and Petch (1953)
  on grain size; Iijima (1991) on carbon nanotubes; Novoselov et al. (2004) on
  graphene, two pages that won a Nobel.
- *You've arrived when:* handed a micrograph you can identify the phases and
  infer the thermal history that produced them, and given a design requirement
  you can select a material from an Ashby chart and defend the tradeoff.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Hull & Bacon, *Introduction to Dislocations*, then Hirth &
  Lothe, *Theory of Dislocations*, for the full apparatus; Balluffi, Allen &
  Carter, *Kinetics of Materials*; T.L. Anderson, *Fracture Mechanics:
  Fundamentals and Applications*; Cahn & Haasen, *Physical Metallurgy*, as the
  standing reference; Ashcroft & Mermin for the solid-state physics.
- *Frontier:* self-driving laboratories and high-throughput screening;
  machine-learned interatomic potentials and generative structure prediction;
  solid-state battery electrolytes; high-entropy alloys; microstructure
  control in additive manufacturing; 2D materials beyond graphene; mechanical
  metamaterials.
- *Contribution looks like:* a new material or processing route, a mechanism
  resolved by characterization, or a predictive model that survives
  experimental test. Characterization is the real barrier — TEM, atom probe,
  synchrotron beamlines. Worth knowing: US Department of Energy light sources
  and nanoscience centers award beamtime free by competitive proposal, a
  genuine path for a well-prepared outsider.
- *Community:* TMS (The Minerals, Metals & Materials Society), MRS (Materials
  Research Society, spring and fall meetings), ASM International, and Gordon
  Research Conferences.

**Beyond T1 — the long shelf**

- *Branch into:* physical metallurgy — Bhadeshia & Honeycombe, *Steels:
  Microstructure and Properties*: the alloy system that carries civilisation.
  Ceramics and glass — Kingery, Bowen & Uhlmann, *Introduction to Ceramics*,
  with Shelby, *Introduction to Glass Science and Technology*: strength without
  plasticity, and what that costs. Polymers and soft matter — Rubinstein &
  Colby, *Polymer Physics*, with Jones, *Soft Condensed Matter*: entropy as the
  dominant elastic force. Composites — Hull & Clyne, *An Introduction to
  Composite Materials*: getting property combinations no single phase offers.
  Electronic materials — Sze & Ng, *Physics of Semiconductor Devices*: the
  branch that pays for the field. Biomaterials and biological materials —
  Vincent, *Structural Biomaterials*, with Ratner et al., *Biomaterials
  Science*: how nature reaches toughness with weak ingredients. Corrosion —
  Jones, *Principles and Prevention of Corrosion*: the failure mode that
  destroys a few percent of GDP annually and is taught almost nowhere.
  Characterisation as its own discipline — Cullity & Stock, *Elements of X-Ray
  Diffraction*, then Williams & Carter, *Transmission Electron Microscopy*.
  Computational materials — LeSar, *Introduction to Computational Materials
  Science*, the branch open to you without a lab.
- *The long canon:* Robert Cahn, *The Coming of Materials Science* — the
  field's own history, by an insider, explaining how metallurgy, ceramics and
  polymer science became one subject. More Cyril Stanley Smith: *A History of
  Metallography* and *From Art to Science*, which argue that materials
  knowledge came out of ornament and craft long before use. Alan Williams,
  *The Knight and the Blast Furnace*, a metallurgical study of hundreds of
  pieces of medieval armour — extraordinary, obsessive, and unlike anything
  else. Verhoeven, *Steel Metallurgy for the Non-Metallurgist*, and his free
  *Metallurgy of Steel for Bladesmiths*, which is where the wootz-steel
  literature actually lives. Wertime & Muhly, *The Coming of the Age of Iron*.
  D'Arcy Thompson, *On Growth and Form*, the ancestor of all thinking about
  structural hierarchy. Petroski, *To Engineer Is Human*, on failure as the
  engine of design knowledge. Ashby, *Materials and the Environment*, and
  Allwood & Cullen, *Sustainable Materials: With Both Eyes Open* (free
  online), for the material-flow half of the century's problem. Smil, *Making
  the Modern World* and *Still the Iron Age*, for the tonnages. Feynman's 1959
  "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom," free, and worth reading against what
  nanotechnology actually became. Philip Ball, *Made to Measure*.
- *Re-foundation watch:* the fundamentals are unusually safe — phase
  equilibria, diffusion, dislocation theory, fracture mechanics, crystallography
  and Ashby selection logic are not going to be overturned. Discovery is what
  is being rebuilt. Machine-learned interatomic potentials went from research
  topic to standard tool in about five years and now do molecular dynamics at
  near-DFT accuracy on systems DFT cannot touch; generative structure
  prediction has proposed candidate compounds by the hundreds of thousands;
  autonomous laboratories that synthesise and characterise without a human in
  the loop are being built, and the arguments about whether their outputs are
  genuinely new are themselves worth following. On the measurement side, 4D-STEM
  and ptychography, atom probe tomography and in-situ TEM keep converting
  inference into direct observation. When you return, the question is not what
  new materials exist but who and what proposed them. The stale signal: you
  still think structure determination needs a good crystal, or you evaluate a
  new material by reading a paper rather than by asking what the screening
  pipeline searched.
- *A lifetime practice:* build a metallography archive. Mount, polish, etch,
  photograph and label a sample every month or two, with its full thermal and
  mechanical history written down; after thirty years you own a reference
  collection with provenance that no book can give you and that you can
  actually query. Alongside it, keep a failure cabinet — broken tools, snapped
  fasteners, fatigued springs, corroded fittings — each with a fractography
  note on what the surface says about how it died. And take up one craft that
  is materials science with your hands: bladesmithing, pottery with glazes you
  formulate yourself, casting, or glasswork. Glaze chemistry in particular is
  real phase-diagram work with a fired answer at the end of every week.
- *Rabbit holes:* wootz and pattern-welded Damascus steel — the reconstruction
  is contested and the literature is a pleasure. Roman concrete and the
  hot-mixing hypothesis for its self-healing. Prince Rupert's drops, which
  survive a hammer and explode from a scratch. Tin whiskers, a spontaneous
  crystal growth that has destroyed satellites and nuclear plant controls.
  Nacre, and how a brittle mineral plus a little protein becomes three
  thousand times tougher than the mineral alone.
