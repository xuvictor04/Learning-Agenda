# Physical

Five domains, each laid out at all three tiers. T3 is the literacy pass every
field enters at; T2 and T1 are what promotion actually costs. Substitution is
fine — skipping a slot is not.

---

## Physics

**The question it asks.** What is the smallest set of rules from which
everything that happens follows — and does such a set exist? Physics currently
holds two descriptions, quantum field theory and general relativity, each
superb in its own domain and mutually inconsistent where they meet. The live
problem is not "more particles"; it is that our two best theories cannot both
be right.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Symmetry implies conservation (Noether's theorem) — the deepest structural
  fact in the subject.
- Least action, not F=ma, is the real formulation of mechanics; it survives
  into quantum mechanics as the path integral.
- Fields are primary; forces are what fields do locally. Nothing acts at a
  distance.
- Entropy is a counting statement, and the arrow of time is a statement about
  initial conditions, not about the laws.
- Quantum mechanics: amplitudes, superposition, and a measurement problem that
  is still genuinely unsolved.
- Relativity: no absolute simultaneity, spacetime as a geometry, gravity as
  curvature.
- Effective field theory — why you can do chemistry without knowing quantum
  gravity. Scales decouple, and that is why science is possible at all.
- Dimensional analysis and order-of-magnitude estimation get you most of the
  answer before any calculation.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Quantum indeterminacy is not permission for "anything goes." The
  wavefunction evolves deterministically and the theory constrains ruthlessly.
- Physics is not mostly particle colliders. Most physicists work on condensed
  matter, optics, and materials.
- A "theory of everything" would not let you predict a protein fold. Emergence
  is real (see Anderson below).

**T3 — Literacy (~200 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Feynman, *The Character of Physical Law* (the 1964 Messenger
  Lectures; book plus video findable online). It is short and it teaches you
  what a physical law *is* — the prerequisite for everything else.
- *Survey:* Sean Carroll, *The Biggest Ideas in the Universe*, vols. 1–2 — real
  equations, no prerequisites assumed. If you have calculus, substitute
  Feynman Vol. I directly.
- *Canon:* *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, free in full at
  feynmanlectures.caltech.edu. Do not read Volume I cover to cover on a first
  pass. Read Ch. 1–7 (what physics is, energy, gravitation), 26–27 (least
  time), 37–38 (quantum), 44–46 (thermodynamics and the ratchet). Then
  Einstein's 1905 paper "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies," §1–5 — it
  is astonishingly readable, and free.
- *Course:* Leonard Susskind's Stanford *Theoretical Minimum* lecture series,
  free at theoreticalminimum.com — Classical Mechanics, then Quantum
  Mechanics, then Special Relativity. It goes further with less pain than any
  other free option. Walter Lewin's MIT 8.01/8.02 lectures (on YouTube; MIT
  pulled them from OCW in 2014) remain the best filmed intro to E&M.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words on why least action, not Newton's second law, is the
  real formulation of classical mechanics — and what it means that the same
  principle reappears in quantum mechanics.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Kleppner & Kolenkow, *An Introduction to Mechanics*; Purcell &
  Morin, *Electricity and Magnetism*; Taylor, *Classical Mechanics*; Griffiths,
  *Introduction to Electrodynamics* and *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics*;
  Schroeder, *An Introduction to Thermal Physics*. Keep Boas, *Mathematical
  Methods in the Physical Sciences*, open alongside all of them.
- *Practice:* problems, and there is no substitute. 20–30 per chapter,
  realistically 800–1,500 solved across the sequence, written out fully. Add
  computation: write an N-body integrator, solve the 1D Schrödinger equation
  numerically, run an Ising-model Monte Carlo. Kleppner is where most people
  stall — it is deliberately harder than it looks. Do it anyway.
- *Primary literature:* start with *Reviews of Modern Physics*, the best door
  into any subfield; then *Physical Review Letters* and the daily arXiv
  listings. Read directly: Einstein (1905) on special relativity; Einstein,
  Podolsky & Rosen (1935); Bell, "On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen Paradox"
  (1964) — six pages that changed the field; Anderson, "More Is Different"
  (*Science*, 1972).
- *You've arrived when:* you can derive the hydrogen spectrum from the
  Schrödinger equation without notes, and set up and solve an unfamiliar
  Lagrangian-mechanics problem from scratch.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Goldstein, *Classical Mechanics*; Jackson, *Classical
  Electrodynamics* (the field's shared trauma); Sakurai & Napolitano, *Modern
  Quantum Mechanics*; Peskin & Schroeder, *An Introduction to Quantum Field
  Theory*, with Weinberg's three-volume *The Quantum Theory of Fields* as the
  reference; Wald, *General Relativity*, or Misner, Thorne & Wheeler,
  *Gravitation*; Ashcroft & Mermin, *Solid State Physics*; Landau & Lifshitz,
  *Course of Theoretical Physics*, for the ones you need.
- *Frontier:* quantum information as a language for both condensed matter and
  gravity (holography, error correction); topological phases; gravitational-
  wave astronomy after LIGO; precision tests such as muon g−2; programmable
  quantum simulation. Follow arXiv hep-th and cond-mat, and PIRSA — Perimeter
  Institute's free recorded-seminar archive.
- *Contribution looks like:* a calculation no one has done, a measurement no
  one has made, or a reframing that makes an old problem tractable.
  Experimental physics is effectively closed to solo work; theory and
  computation are less so, but a PhD or a real collaboration is the normal
  road.
- *Community:* American Physical Society (March Meeting for condensed matter,
  April for particles and astrophysics), arXiv, and program-based institutes —
  KITP, Perimeter, Aspen Center for Physics.

## Cosmology & astronomy

**The question it asks.** How did a nearly uniform hot beginning produce a
universe with galaxies, stars, and planets in it — and what is the roughly 95%
of its contents that is not atoms? Astronomy's parallel problem is harder than
it sounds: you must reconstruct the life histories of objects you can never
touch, visit, or repeat, from light alone.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Space expands; cosmological redshift is not a Doppler shift, and there is no
  center.
- The cosmic microwave background is a photograph of the universe at 380,000
  years, and its acoustic peaks encode the geometry, the baryon fraction, and
  the dark matter density.
- Dark matter is overdetermined — rotation curves, lensing, cluster dynamics,
  the CMB, and structure formation all demand it independently.
- Dark energy: the 1998 supernova result, now corroborated by CMB and baryon
  acoustic oscillations.
- Stellar nucleosynthesis — every element heavier than lithium has a stellar or
  compact-object origin. The HR diagram is the organizing chart of the field.
- Gravitational collapse and its endpoints: white dwarfs, the Chandrasekhar
  limit, neutron stars, black holes.
- The cosmic distance ladder, and why it is fragile — the Hubble tension is a
  live and unresolved disagreement.
- Inflation explains flatness, the horizon problem, and the fluctuation
  spectrum — and remains a framework, not a confirmed theory.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- The Big Bang was not an explosion *in* space and did not happen at a point.
- Dark matter is not a fudge factor; a fudge factor would not fit five
  independent datasets with one number.
- Professional astronomy is almost never eyepieces. It is photometry,
  spectroscopy, and data pipelines.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Carl Sagan, *Cosmos* — book or the 1980 series. Still the best
  invitation ever made to the subject, and it models scientific reasoning
  rather than just reporting results.
- *Survey:* OpenStax *Astronomy 2e* — free, complete, and a genuinely good
  first textbook; skim the whole thing in a month. Pair with Kip Thorne,
  *Black Holes and Time Warps*, for the relativistic half told as history.
- *Canon:* Steven Weinberg, *The First Three Minutes*. Then read two short
  papers directly, both about two pages: Hubble (1929), "A Relation Between
  Distance and Radial Velocity Among Extra-Galactic Nebulae," and Penzias &
  Wilson (1965), "A Measurement of Excess Antenna Temperature at 4080 Mc/s."
  You will see the entire modern picture born in four pages.
- *Course:* Open Yale ASTR 160, *Frontiers and Controversies in Astrophysics*
  (Charles Bailyn) — free, uses real arithmetic, and is honest about what is
  unsettled. Follow with MIT OCW 8.282J, *Introduction to Astronomy*.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words on how we know the age, composition, and geometry of
  the universe — naming the actual measurements and their error bars, not the
  conclusions.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Carroll & Ostlie, *An Introduction to Modern Astrophysics* (the
  standard undergraduate brick — you will not read it linearly, you will live
  in it); Ryden, *Introduction to Cosmology*, which is short, clean, and the
  best cosmology text at this level.
- *Practice:* this field hands you its data. Reduce your own images (a small
  telescope with a CCD, or the SDSS SkyServer, MAST, and ESO archives). Do
  aperture photometry on a variable star and build a light curve; submit
  observations to the AAVSO. Classify galaxy spectra by hand. Fit the Pantheon
  supernova compilation and recover the acceleration yourself in Python with
  astropy. Target four to six finished analyses on real data.
- *Primary literature:* *The Astrophysical Journal*, *MNRAS*, *Astronomy &
  Astrophysics*, and the arXiv astro-ph listing daily. Read directly: Burbidge,
  Burbidge, Fowler & Hoyle (1957), "Synthesis of the Elements in Stars" — the
  B²FH paper; Rubin & Ford (1970) on the Andromeda rotation curve; Riess et al.
  (1998) and Perlmutter et al. (1999) on cosmic acceleration.
- *You've arrived when:* you can take a raw FITS frame through calibration to a
  magnitude with a defensible uncertainty — and read an astro-ph abstract and
  say what would have to change if the result held.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Binney & Tremaine, *Galactic Dynamics*; Peebles,
  *Principles of Physical Cosmology*, with Dodelson & Schmidt, *Modern
  Cosmology*, for the calculational modern treatment; Kippenhahn & Weigert,
  *Stellar Structure and Evolution*; Rybicki & Lightman, *Radiative Processes
  in Astrophysics* (the one everyone actually uses); Shapiro & Teukolsky,
  *Black Holes, White Dwarfs, and Neutron Stars*.
- *Frontier:* JWST's high-redshift galaxy population; gravitational-wave
  populations from LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA; the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's LSST;
  Euclid and DESI; the Hubble tension; 21cm cosmology; exoplanet atmospheres.
  Follow astro-ph daily and the AAS journals.
- *Contribution looks like:* astronomy is unusually open to outsiders. Archives
  are public, and amateurs still co-author real papers on transit timing,
  occultations, supernova discovery, and variable stars. Original work means a
  new measurement, a new analysis of existing survey data, or a model that
  predicts something observable.
- *Community:* American Astronomical Society, the IAU, the AAVSO, the Royal
  Astronomical Society. Zooniverse is a legitimate front door.

## Chemistry

**The question it asks.** Why does matter have the properties it has, and how
do you make substances that do not yet exist? The intellectual situation is
peculiar: physics supplies the exact equation, and that equation is
uncomputable for anything larger than a few atoms. So chemistry runs on a
hierarchy of models that are all strictly wrong and reliably predictive, and
knowing each one's domain of validity *is* the expertise.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- The periodic table is a consequence of orbital structure — periodicity,
  effective nuclear charge, electronegativity all fall out of it.
- The ladder of bonding models: Lewis structures → VSEPR → hybridization →
  molecular orbital theory. Each is a better approximation and a worse
  intuition-generator.
- Thermodynamics tells you what is favorable; kinetics tells you what is fast.
  Catalysts change rates, never equilibria.
- Equilibrium, Le Chatelier, and pKa — pKa is the single most useful number in
  practical chemistry.
- Mechanism and the curly arrow: nucleophile, electrophile, electron flow. This
  is chemistry's actual language, not its notation.
- You do not know what you made until you have characterized it — NMR, mass
  spectrometry, IR, X-ray crystallography.
- Stereochemistry and chirality, and why they decide whether a drug works or
  poisons.
- Entropy-driven organization: the hydrophobic effect, micelles, self-assembly.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- "Chemical" versus "natural" is not a distinction chemistry recognizes.
- Chemistry is not memorization. People stall in organic chemistry precisely by
  memorizing reactions instead of learning to push arrows — the reactions are
  infinite, the mechanisms are about a dozen.
- A balanced equation is bookkeeping. It says nothing about what molecules do.

**T3 — Literacy (~180 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Primo Levi, *The Periodic Table*. Chemistry as a way of seeing,
  written by a working chemist who was also one of the century's great writers.
  If you read only two chapters, read "Iron" and "Carbon."
- *Survey:* Peter Atkins, *Chemistry: A Very Short Introduction*, then his
  *Atkins' Molecules* — Atkins wrote the standard physical chemistry text and
  can compress without lying. Sam Kean's *The Disappearing Spoon* is the fun
  supplement, not the substitute.
- *Canon:* Linus Pauling, *The Nature of the Chemical Bond*. Read Ch. 1–3 and
  the resonance chapters; skip the crystal-radii tables. This is the book that
  built modern chemistry and it is still readable. Pauling's *General
  Chemistry* (Dover, cheap) is the same mind writing a textbook.
- *Course:* MIT OCW 5.111, *Principles of Chemical Science* (Catherine
  Drennan) — full video, and among the best-taught courses on OCW. Follow with
  MIT OCW 5.12, *Organic Chemistry I*.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words tracing the Haber–Bosch process end to end — the
  thermodynamics that makes it hard, the catalysis that makes it possible, the
  pressures and temperatures it actually runs at, and the fact that roughly
  half the nitrogen atoms in your body passed through it.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Clayden, Greeves & Warren, *Organic Chemistry* — arguably the best
  science textbook written in the last thirty years in any field; start here
  even if you think you want physical chemistry. Then Atkins & de Paula,
  *Physical Chemistry*; Housecroft & Sharpe, *Inorganic Chemistry*; Harris,
  *Quantitative Chemical Analysis* for the analytical side.
- *Practice:* work Clayden cover to cover with the solutions manual — 30-plus
  problems per chapter, about a year of evenings. Then bench work, and be
  honest that this is the hardest domain on this page to do at home
  legitimately: find a community-college lab course or volunteer in one.
  Minimum credible set: one multi-step synthesis, one purification by column
  chromatography and one by recrystallization, and a product you characterized
  yourself by NMR. Computational substitute if no lab is reachable: Avogadro
  with Psi4 or ORCA (both free) for geometry optimizations and spectra you can
  check against literature values.
- *Primary literature:* *JACS*, *Angewandte Chemie*, *Nature Chemistry*,
  *Organic Letters*; use *Chemical Reviews* to enter any subfield. Read
  directly: Pauling (1931, *JACS*) on the nature of the chemical bond;
  Woodward & Hoffmann on conservation of orbital symmetry (1965 *JACS* papers,
  or the 1969 *Angewandte* review); Watson & Crick (1953), which is a
  structural chemistry paper before it is anything else.
- *You've arrived when:* given an unfamiliar transformation you can propose a
  mechanism with arrows and defend it — and given an unknown's ¹H NMR, IR, and
  mass spectrum, you can assign the structure.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Anslyn & Dougherty, *Modern Physical Organic Chemistry*;
  Carey & Sundberg, *Advanced Organic Chemistry*, Parts A and B; Szabo &
  Ostlund, *Modern Quantum Chemistry* (or Levine, *Quantum Chemistry*, for a
  gentler route); McQuarrie, *Statistical Mechanics*; Cotton & Wilkinson,
  *Advanced Inorganic Chemistry*.
- *Frontier:* machine-learned interatomic potentials and retrosynthesis
  planning; automated and flow synthesis; photoredox and electrocatalysis; CO₂
  reduction; computational enzyme design; ultrafast and single-molecule
  spectroscopy. Follow *Nature Chemistry*, *C&EN*, and specific groups' sites.
- *Contribution looks like:* a molecule nobody has made, a reaction or catalyst
  nobody has run, a method of measurement, or a mechanism finally resolved.
  This requires a lab — chemistry is the least autodidact-friendly domain here.
  Computational chemistry is the genuine exception and needs only a machine.
- *Community:* American Chemical Society national meetings, the Royal Society
  of Chemistry, and Gordon Research Conferences — where unpublished work is
  actually discussed.

## Earth science & climate

**The question it asks.** How does a rocky planet with a molten interior, an
ocean, and a thin atmosphere self-organize into the system we live inside,
across timescales from seconds to billions of years? Climate science is the
subfield forced to answer a question with consequences on a deadline: how much
warming per unit of CO₂ — and how would you know, given exactly one planet and
no control group?

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Plate tectonics as the unifying theory: ridges, subduction, the rock cycle.
  Everything in solid-earth science postdates 1965 conceptually.
- Deep time, and how it is read — stratigraphy, isotopes, radiometric dating.
  Uniformitarianism as method, with catastrophes admitted back in.
- The carbon cycle on two clocks: fast (biosphere and ocean, years to
  centuries) and slow (silicate weathering, the planet's thermostat, hundreds
  of thousands of years).
- The greenhouse effect done properly: it is about raising the *altitude* at
  which the planet radiates to space, not a blanket trapping heat.
- Feedbacks: water vapour (strong, positive), ice–albedo, and clouds — the
  dominant remaining uncertainty. Climate sensitivity is the number the whole
  argument hangs from.
- Circulation: Coriolis, Hadley cells, the thermohaline conveyor, ENSO.
- Paleoclimate: ice cores, Milankovitch orbital forcing, and the PETM as the
  nearest analogue to what we are doing.
- Weather is an initial-value problem and goes chaotic in about two weeks;
  climate is a boundary-value problem and is predictable. They are different
  kinds of question.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- "The climate has always changed" is true and is the *reason* for concern —
  the paleo record is how sensitivity was constrained, and it shows the current
  rate is anomalous.
- Models are not the primary evidence. The radiative physics is from 1896
  (Arrhenius) and the instrumental record is independent of any model.
- Geology is not rock identification. It is inferring a history from a
  fragmentary, deformed, partially erased record.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* John McPhee, *Annals of the Former World*. Read "Basin and
  Range" first. Nothing else installs deep time in your bones like this, and
  it won a Pulitzer for good reason.
- *Survey:* David Archer, *Global Warming: Understanding the Forecast* — short,
  quantitative, and honest about uncertainty. For the solid earth, Marcia
  Bjornerud's *Timefulness* or Walter Alvarez's *T. rex and the Crater of Doom*.
- *Canon:* the IPCC AR6 Working Group I report, free. Read the Summary for
  Policymakers to see what was agreed, then the Technical Summary, which is
  where the actual science lives — the SPM is negotiated line by line and the
  TS is not. Historical canon: Lyell, *Principles of Geology*, Vol. 1, Ch. 1–4
  (what Darwin read on the *Beagle*), and Arrhenius (1896), "On the Influence
  of Carbonic Acid in the Air upon the Temperature of the Ground."
- *Course:* David Archer's *Global Warming: The Science and Modeling of Climate
  Change* (University of Chicago, free on Coursera) — you run real models, not
  just watch. His free online models at forecast.uchicago.edu, especially
  MODTRAN, let you change CO₂ and watch the outgoing spectrum change. For solid
  earth, MIT OCW 12.001, *Introduction to Geology*.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words deriving the greenhouse effect from first
  principles — blackbody balance, why the surface exceeds the effective
  temperature, what doubling CO₂ does before feedbacks, and precisely where the
  remaining uncertainty lives.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Marshall & Plumb, *Atmosphere, Ocean and Climate Dynamics*;
  Hartmann, *Global Physical Climatology*; Ruddiman, *Earth's Climate: Past and
  Future*; Grotzinger & Jordan, *Understanding Earth*; Turcotte & Schubert,
  *Geodynamics*, for the solid-earth half.
- *Practice:* this is the most data-open field on this page. Rebuild the global
  temperature anomaly record from raw GHCN station data in Python and confront
  every homogenization choice yourself. Code a one-dimensional
  radiative–convective model. Pull CMIP6 output from ESGF and analyze it with
  xarray. Decompose the Keeling curve into trend and seasonal cycle. On the
  geology side, get outdoors: log a stratigraphic section, map an outcrop,
  learn to read a geologic map — a field-methods course is worth the money.
  Target four to six finished analyses plus one field project.
- *Primary literature:* *Nature Geoscience*, *Journal of Climate*, *JGR*
  (Atmospheres and Solid Earth), *Geophysical Research Letters*, *Geology*.
  Read directly: Hess (1962), "History of Ocean Basins," which is seafloor
  spreading; Keeling (1960) on atmospheric CO₂ concentration; Manabe &
  Wetherald (1967), "Thermal Equilibrium of the Atmosphere with a Given
  Distribution of Relative Humidity" — the paper behind the 2021 Nobel; Hansen
  et al. (1981) in *Science*, whose projections you can now grade.
- *You've arrived when:* you can reconstruct a global temperature series from
  raw station data and justify every adjustment — and read a geologic map cold
  and narrate the region's history from it.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Pierrehumbert, *Principles of Planetary Climate* — the
  serious one, and the one that makes the physics general rather than
  Earth-specific; Vallis, *Atmospheric and Oceanic Fluid Dynamics*; Holton &
  Hakim, *An Introduction to Dynamic Meteorology*; Sarmiento & Gruber, *Ocean
  Biogeochemical Dynamics*; White, *Geochemistry*.
- *Frontier:* cloud feedback and convective aggregation; kilometre-scale global
  storm-resolving models; machine-learning emulators and downscaling; extreme-
  event attribution; carbon dioxide removal and its verification; tipping
  elements such as AMOC and the West Antarctic ice sheet; applying climate
  physics to exoplanets.
- *Contribution looks like:* a new observational dataset, a mechanism
  explained, a model component improved, or the paleo record extended. Because
  data and model output are public, this field is unusually reachable from
  outside — several influential analyses have come from non-institutional
  authors.
- *Community:* AGU Fall Meeting (the largest earth-science gathering in the
  world), EGU, the American Meteorological Society, the Geological Society of
  America. RealClimate for commentary written by working scientists.

## Materials science

**The question it asks.** Why do identical atoms arranged differently give you
graphite or diamond — and can you design a structure, from atoms up to
millimetres, that delivers properties you specify in advance? The field's
organizing insight is that real materials are governed by their *defects*, not
their perfect lattices, and that everything flows along one chain: processing →
structure → properties → performance.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- The processing–structure–properties–performance tetrahedron. Every question
  in the field is a link in that chain.
- Crystal structure and close packing — and then the correction that matters:
  vacancies, dislocations, and grain boundaries carry most real behaviour. A
  perfect crystal would be roughly a hundred times stronger than the metal you
  actually hold.
- Plasticity *is* dislocation motion. Every strengthening mechanism — work
  hardening, grain refinement (Hall–Petch), solid solution, precipitation — is
  a different way to pin dislocations.
- Phase diagrams and the lever rule. The iron–carbon diagram is the most
  consequential chart in the history of engineering.
- Kinetics versus equilibrium: diffusion, nucleation and growth, TTT diagrams,
  quenching and tempering. Glass is a liquid that ran out of time.
- Failure: Griffith's criterion, stress concentration, fracture toughness,
  fatigue. Things break from cracks, not from being insufficiently strong.
- Band structure: why metals conduct, why semiconductors can be controlled, and
  what doping actually does.
- Composites and hierarchical natural materials — bone, wood, nacre — achieve
  property combinations no monolithic material can.
- Ashby maps turn property space into a design space you can actually navigate.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- "Stronger" is not one property. Strength, stiffness, hardness, and toughness
  are distinct and frequently trade against each other — glass is strong and
  useless; the confusion between strong and tough is the classic tell.
- A *Nature* paper is not a product. Materials take one to three decades to go
  from lab to production, and most never arrive.
- Nanotechnology is not tiny machines. It is what happens when a dimension
  shrinks to meet a physical length scale.

**T3 — Literacy (~130 hrs)**
- *Start here:* J.E. Gordon, *Structures: Or Why Things Don't Fall Down*. It is
  funny, physical, and explains fracture better than most textbooks; then his
  *The New Science of Strong Materials*, which created the popular genre.
- *Survey:* Mark Miodownik, *Stuff Matters* — ten materials, superbly told. If
  you want the real thing early, Ashby & Jones, *Engineering Materials 1*, is a
  textbook that reads like a book.
- *Canon:* Cyril Stanley Smith, *A Search for Structure* — essays by the
  metallurgist who worked on the Manhattan Project and became a historian of
  materials and art. It is the field's deepest reflection on itself, and it
  argues that materials advanced through aesthetics and craft long before
  theory. Read the title essay and "Art, Technology and Science."
- *Course:* MIT OCW 3.091, *Introduction to Solid-State Chemistry* (Donald
  Sadoway) — one of the great lecture performances on OCW, and it teaches the
  atomic basis properly. Follow with MIT OCW 3.032, *Mechanical Behavior of
  Materials*.
- *Artifact:* 2,000 words on steel, following the full chain: the iron–carbon
  phase diagram, what quenching and tempering do to microstructure, and why a
  razor blade, a bridge cable, and a sword need three different microstructures
  from nearly the same alloy system.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Callister & Rethwisch, *Materials Science and Engineering: An
  Introduction* — the universal undergraduate text; Ashby & Jones, *Engineering
  Materials* 1 and 2; Porter, Easterling & Sherif, *Phase Transformations in
  Metals and Alloys*; Kittel, *Introduction to Solid State Physics*, for the
  electronic side; Ashby, *Materials Selection in Mechanical Design*, for how
  the field is actually used.
- *Practice:* metallography is the most accessible genuine lab work in any
  domain on this page and you can do it at home. Mount, polish, etch, and image
  steel samples under an inexpensive metallurgical microscope; heat-treat and
  quench coupons and measure the hardness change. Get tensile testing at a
  makerspace or community college. Computationally: run DFT with Quantum
  ESPRESSO, compute phase diagrams with pycalphad, and mine the Materials
  Project database (free, enormous). Target twenty to thirty characterized
  samples with documented thermal histories, plus two or three computational
  studies.
- *Primary literature:* *Acta Materialia*, *Nature Materials*, *Advanced
  Materials*, *Scripta Materialia*; *Progress in Materials Science* for
  reviews. Read directly: Griffith (1921), "The Phenomena of Rupture and Flow
  in Solids" — the origin of fracture mechanics; Hall (1951) and Petch (1953)
  on grain size; Iijima (1991) on carbon nanotubes; Novoselov et al. (2004) on
  graphene, which is two pages and won a Nobel.
- *You've arrived when:* handed a micrograph, you can identify the phases and
  infer the thermal history that produced them — and given a stated design
  requirement, you can select a material from an Ashby chart and defend the
  tradeoff you made.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts:* Hull & Bacon, *Introduction to Dislocations*, then Hirth &
  Lothe, *Theory of Dislocations*, for the full apparatus; Balluffi, Allen &
  Carter, *Kinetics of Materials*; T.L. Anderson, *Fracture Mechanics:
  Fundamentals and Applications*; Cahn & Haasen, *Physical Metallurgy*, as the
  standing reference; Ashcroft & Mermin for the solid-state physics.
- *Frontier:* self-driving laboratories and high-throughput screening;
  machine-learned interatomic potentials and generative structure prediction;
  solid-state battery electrolytes; high-entropy alloys; microstructure control
  in additive manufacturing; 2D materials beyond graphene; mechanical
  metamaterials.
- *Contribution looks like:* a new material or processing route, a mechanism
  resolved by characterization, or a predictive model that survives
  experimental test. Characterization is the real barrier — TEM, atom probe,
  synchrotron beamlines. Worth knowing: national user facilities such as the
  US Department of Energy's light sources and nanoscience centers award
  beamtime free by competitive proposal, which is a genuine path for a
