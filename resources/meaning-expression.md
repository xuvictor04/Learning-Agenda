# Meaning & Expression

Seven domains where the canon *is* the primary material — dialogues, scriptures,
novels, scores, paintings, films. Read *about* them at T3 only enough to know
what to look at; from T2 on, the works and the practice are the curriculum.
Translation choices are named where they matter, because in these fields a bad
translation is a different book.

## Philosophy (history of, plus the core problems)

**The question it asks.** What can be settled by argument alone, when
observation and calculation run out? Philosophy works the questions that
resist empirical decision — what exists, what we can know, what we owe each
other, what words mean — and its real subject is the standards by which such
questions get argued at all. Every science that found a method left and took
its questions with it; the conceptual and normative residue is not shrinking.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- The a priori / a posteriori and analytic / synthetic distinctions, and
  Hume's fork — the machinery behind most modern epistemology.
- Skepticism and its replies: Descartes' demon, Hume's problem of induction,
  the Gettier problem's wreck of "justified true belief."
- The mind–body problem: dualism, physicalism, functionalism, qualia, the
  explanatory gap.
- Free will and determinism, and why compatibilism is the majority position
  among philosophers rather than a dodge.
- Three normative families — consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics —
  plus the metaethical layer beneath them and the is/ought gap.
- Political legitimacy: the social contract, liberty, distributive justice,
  the veil of ignorance.
- Meaning: sense vs reference (Frege), meaning as use (Wittgenstein), rigid
  designation (Kripke).
- Non-Western load-bearers: Buddhist *anatta* and dependent origination;
  Nāgārjuna's emptiness; Confucian *ren* and *li*; Daoist *wu wei*; the
  Nyāya theory of *pramāṇas* (valid means of knowledge).

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- It is not opinion-sharing. Positions die to counterexamples, and a good
  philosopher can state the strongest version of the view they reject.
- Science did not replace it — physics, psychology and linguistics are its
  successful emigrants, and each still has open foundational questions.
- Reading summaries instead of texts. The primary arguments are usually
  short; the paraphrase is where the interesting move gets lost.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Plato, *Euthyphro / Apology / Crito* in the Grube
  translation (Hackett, *The Trial and Death of Socrates*). Sixty pages, and
  the door because it shows philosophy as an activity — a man cross-examining
  confident people — before it shows any doctrine.
- *Survey:* Anthony Kenny, *A New History of Western Philosophy* — more
  reliable than Russell, nearly as readable. Then Peter Adamson's *History of
  Philosophy Without Any Gaps* (free podcast, also books) for the Indian,
  Islamic, Africana and Chinese traditions the Western survey drops.
- *Canon:* Plato, *Republic* (Books I–II, VI–VII — the Cave and the argument
  it serves); Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I–II, X; Descartes,
  *Meditations*; Hume, *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*; Mill, *On
  Liberty*; Nietzsche, *On the Genealogy of Morals*. Alongside them: the
  *Analects* (Slingerland's Hackett edition, for the commentary), the *Tao Te
  Ching* (D.C. Lau for accuracy — Le Guin's beloved version is a rendition,
  not a translation, and should be read second), and Nāgārjuna via Jay
  Garfield's *The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way*. Read for the
  argument structure, not the conclusions.
- *Course:* Peter Millican's *General Philosophy* (Oxford, free lectures) is
  the best free introduction to the core problems. Michael Sandel's *Justice*
  (Harvard, free at justiceharvard.org) for ethics and political philosophy.
- *Artifact:* take one problem — free will is ideal — and reconstruct hard
  determinism, libertarianism and compatibilism each as numbered
  premise–conclusion arguments. Then name the single premise you deny and
  defend denying it. 2,000 words.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* is the real spine —
  free, peer-reviewed, current. Around it: Michael Loux, *Metaphysics: A
  Contemporary Introduction*; Robert Audi, *Epistemology*; Peter Smith's free
  *An Introduction to Formal Logic* and his *Teach Yourself Logic* guide;
  Graham Priest, *An Introduction to Non-Classical Logic*.
- *Practice:* argument reconstruction, daily. Take a journal article and
  render it as numbered premises until the hidden ones surface. Write
  objection-and-reply papers on positions you hold — the discipline is
  attacking your own view. Do the logic problem sets to completion; the
  formal skill is what separates readers from participants. Join or run a
  seminar where someone is obliged to disagree with you every week.
- *Primary literature:* *Mind*, *Noûs*, *The Journal of Philosophy*,
  *Philosophical Review*, *Analysis*, *Philosophy East and West*. Landmarks:
  Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951); Gettier, "Is Justified True
  Belief Knowledge?" (1963); Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974);
  Anscombe, "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958); Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*.
- *You've arrived when:* handed an unfamiliar paper in a subfield you don't
  work in, you can state its argument in numbered form and produce an
  objection the author would concede needs answering.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Kripke, *Naming and Necessity*; Lewis, *On the
  Plurality of Worlds*; Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*; Parfit,
  *Reasons and Persons*; Sellars, "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind";
  Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason* (Guyer & Wood) with a commentary; on the
  continental side, Hegel's *Phenomenology of Spirit* (Pinkard's translation)
  and Heidegger's *Being and Time* (Macquarrie & Robinson).
- *Frontier:* formal epistemology, philosophy of mind meeting AI, experimental
  philosophy, meta-metaphysics, and the fast institutionalization of Chinese,
  Indian and Africana philosophy as fields rather than curiosities. Follow
  PhilPapers alerts in your subfield and two or three working philosophers'
  drafts.
- *Contribution looks like:* a novel argument, defended in a refereed journal,
  that other philosophers must now answer. Secondary channels that count:
  a new critical translation, a synthetic monograph, and genuine public
  philosophy. There is no creative-production track here — philosophy's
  output is discursive all the way down.
- *Community:* the American Philosophical Association (Eastern, Central,
  Pacific), the Aristotelian Society, PhilPapers/PhilArchive for
  distribution, and the subfield-specific societies where the real
  conversation happens.

## World religions & mythology

**The question it asks.** What are these systems, as human phenomena — how do
they arise, transmit, schism, and what work do ritual and narrative do that
argument cannot? The scholarly question is never which tradition is true; it
is what people do, believe and become through them, and what it means that
the category "religion" itself is a relatively recent Western construction
that maps badly onto much of the world. Mythology asks the parallel question
about stories that a culture treats as foundational rather than optional.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- The insider/outsider problem and methodological agnosticism — describing a
  tradition without adjudicating its claims, and why that is hard.
- "Religion" as a contested category (Talal Asad; J.Z. Smith's line that
  religion is "solely the creation of the scholar's study").
- Myth as charter and cosmology rather than failed science — it explains why
  the world is *owed* something, not merely how it works.
- Ritual theory: van Gennep's rites of passage, Turner's liminality and
  communitas, Durkheim's sacred/profane and the critiques of it.
- Textual criticism as a method: the documentary hypothesis, the synoptic
  problem, oral-formulaic transmission, and how canons get closed.
- Orthodoxy vs orthopraxy — belief-centered and practice-centered traditions
  are structurally different animals.
- Lived religion vs elite textual religion; the gap is the field's most
  productive finding.
- Secularization theory, its failure to predict the present, and Charles
  Taylor's reframing of what secularity actually changed.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Treating a tradition as its texts. Most practitioners of most traditions do
  things no scripture prescribes, and that is the normal case, not a lapse.
- Assuming all religions are saying the same thing in different idioms.
  Their questions differ — liberation, obedience, harmony, and salvation are
  not variants of one goal.
- Taking Campbell's monomyth as current scholarship. The hero's journey is a
  screenwriting tool; comparative mythology has moved toward particularity
  and away from universal patterns.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Stephen Prothero, *God Is Not One*. The door because it
  inoculates against the single most common error before you read anything
  else — and it organizes each tradition around the problem that tradition
  thinks it is solving.
- *Survey:* Ninian Smart, *The World's Religions* — the standard secular
  survey, built on his seven dimensions (doctrinal, mythic, ethical, ritual,
  experiential, institutional, material). Huston Smith's book of the same
  name is warmer and more idealizing; read it knowing that.
- *Canon:* go to the sources. Hebrew Bible — Genesis, Exodus, Job,
  Ecclesiastes in Robert Alter's translation (his notes are the education).
  The Gospel of Mark and Paul's Letter to the Romans. The Qur'an in M.A.S.
  Abdel Haleem's Oxford translation, the most readable in English. The
  *Bhagavad Gita* (Barbara Stoler Miller). The *Dhammapada*, then Bhikkhu
  Bodhi's *In the Buddha's Words* for the Pali canon in structure. The
  *Analects* and *Tao Te Ching*. For myth: the *Epic of Gilgamesh* (Andrew
  George), Hesiod's *Theogony*, the *Popol Vuh* (Dennis Tedlock). Read for
  genre and audience: who was this addressed to, and what did it ask of them?
- *Course:* Open Yale RLST 145, *Introduction to the Old Testament (Hebrew
  Bible)* with Christine Hayes, and RLST 152, *Introduction to New Testament
  History and Literature* with Dale Martin. Both free, both models of what
  rigorous, non-devotional teaching about scripture looks like.
- *Artifact:* pick one ritual — Ramadan's fast, a Passover seder, Theravada
  ordination, a Día de Muertos ofrenda — and describe it three times: as
  participants explain it, as a Durkheimian functionalist would, and as a
  textual historian would. Then say which description you think is missing
  the most. 2,000 words.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Smart for structure, plus tradition-specific standards: Bart
  Ehrman, *The New Testament: A Historical Introduction*; Gavin Flood, *An
  Introduction to Hinduism*; Rupert Gethin, *The Foundations of Buddhism*;
  the *Encyclopedia of Religion* (2nd ed., Lindsay Jones) as reference; *The
  Norton Anthology of World Religions* (ed. Jack Miles) for primary texts at
  scale.
- *Practice:* learn a source language — Biblical Hebrew, Koine Greek,
  Classical Arabic, Sanskrit or Pali. This is the actual gate; without one
  you are reading other people's decisions. Work a Gospel synopsis column by
  column and account for every divergence. Do fieldwork: attend services in a
  tradition not your own across a full liturgical year and keep ethnographic
  notes. Memorize chronologies and geography with SRS — the field's atoms.
- *Primary literature:* *Journal of the American Academy of Religion*,
  *History of Religions*, *Numen*, *Journal of Biblical Literature*.
  Landmarks: Durkheim, *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*; Clifford
  Geertz, "Religion as a Cultural System"; Talal Asad, *Genealogies of
  Religion*; Charles Taylor, *A Secular Age*.
- *You've arrived when:* you can teach a semester-long introduction to a
  tradition not your own such that a practitioner recognizes their religion
  in it and a historian catches no errors.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Asad, *Formations of the Secular*; J.Z. Smith,
  *Imagining Religion*; Wendy Doniger on Hindu textual traditions;
  Metzger & Ehrman, *The Text of the New Testament*; and fluency with
  critical editions themselves (Nestle-Aland, BHS, the Pali Text Society).
- *Frontier:* the cognitive science of religion (Boyer, Whitehouse); the
  lived-religion and material-religion turns; the decolonial critique of the
  "world religions" paradigm; digital philology and manuscript imaging.
- *Contribution looks like:* a critical edition, a new translation, or an
  archival or ethnographic monograph that changes what the field thinks
  happened. Translation here is genuinely both scholarship and craft — a
  major new rendering of a scripture is a career-defining creative act.
- *Community:* the American Academy of Religion and Society of Biblical
  Literature annual meetings (co-located, the field's center of gravity), the
  European Association for the Study of Religions, and the IAHR.

## Literature (the world canon)

**The question it asks.** How does arranged language produce meaning and
effect that paraphrase destroys — and why do some texts keep yielding under
rereading while others empty out on the first pass? Underneath sits a second
question the field has fought over for a century: what is the relation among
a work, the conditions that made it, and the readers who keep remaking it,
and who gets to decide what counts as literature at all?

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Form is not decoration on content. Brooks's "heresy of paraphrase": the
  poem's meaning is inseparable from its shape.
- Genre as a historical technology — epic, tragedy, lyric and the novel each
  became possible under particular conditions (Bakhtin on the novel's
  heteroglossia is the sharpest version of this).
- Narratology's toolkit: story vs discourse, focalization, free indirect
  discourse, unreliable narration, temporal order and duration.
- Prosody: meter, the line as a unit of meaning, and why enjambment is an
  argument.
- Intertextuality and influence — every strong work is arguing with earlier
  ones (Bloom's *anxiety of influence*, whether or not you buy it).
- The interpretive schools and what each makes visible: New Criticism,
  structuralism, deconstruction, Marxist, psychoanalytic, feminist,
  postcolonial, book history, distant reading.
- The death of the author (Barthes) against intentionalism — a live dispute,
  not a settled result.
- Translation as interpretation, and "world literature" as a system with
  centers and peripheries (Damrosch, Casanova).

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Hunting for a hidden meaning the author encoded and disguised. The work is
  a machine for producing effects; multiple readings are defensible, but not
  all readings are — the text is the constraint.
- Believing difficulty is snobbery. Difficulty is usually a device that slows
  you to the speed of the sentence, which is the speed the work needs.
- Skipping poetry. Prose habits do not transfer, and poetry is where the
  technical vocabulary of the whole field is learned most cheaply.

**T3 — Literacy (~250 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Homer, *The Odyssey*, in Emily Wilson's translation — plain,
  fast, in strict iambic pentameter, line-for-line with the Greek. The door
  because nearly everything after is arguing with it, and because Wilson's
  introduction is a short education in what a translator decides. (Lattimore
  if you want the literal Greek shape; Fagles for grandeur.)
- *Survey:* *The Norton Anthology of World Literature* (Puchner, gen. ed.) —
  a survey that consists of actually reading the works. Pair it with Erich
  Auerbach's *Mimesis*, which is a survey and an argument at once: how
  reality got represented across three thousand years, one passage at a time.
- *Canon:* Homer; Sophocles, *Oedipus Tyrannus*; Euripides, *Bacchae*; Dante,
  *Inferno* (Hollander for the notes, Pinsky for the verse); Murasaki
  Shikibu, *The Tale of Genji* (Royall Tyler); Cao Xueqin, *The Story of the
  Stone* (David Hawkes); *The Arabian Nights* (Husain Haddawy); Shakespeare's
  *Hamlet* and *King Lear*, read aloud; Cervantes, *Don Quixote* (Edith
  Grossman); Austen, *Pride and Prejudice*; Flaubert, *Madame Bovary* (Lydia
  Davis); Tolstoy, *War and Peace* (Anthony Briggs or the Maudes read better
  in English than Pevear & Volokhonsky); Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*
  (here P&V's roughness is the right choice); Proust, *Swann's Way* (Davis);
  Woolf, *To the Lighthouse*; Kafka; García Márquez, *One Hundred Years of
  Solitude* (Rabassa); Achebe, *Things Fall Apart*; Morrison, *Beloved*;
  Bashō, *The Narrow Road to the Deep North*; Rumi in Jawid Mojaddedi's
  *Masnavi* — not Coleman Barks, whose popular versions are English poems
  written from other people's translations. Look for: how each work manages
  time, and where its narrator stands.
- *Course:* Open Yale ENGL 300, *Introduction to Theory of Literature* (Paul
  Fry) — the schools, taught fairly. ModPo (Al Filreis, Coursera) — free,
  enormous, and the rare online course with real communal close reading. Open
  Yale ENGL 310, *Modern Poetry* (Langdon Hammer) if poetry is the gap.
- *Artifact:* a 2,000-word close reading of one poem or one three-page
  passage, defending a claim that collapses if the details don't support it.
  No biography, no context — the words only.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* *The Princeton Encyclopedia of Poetry and Poetics* (4th ed.);
  *The Norton Anthology of Theory and Criticism*; Wayne Booth, *The Rhetoric
  of Fiction*; Gérard Genette, *Narrative Discourse*; Northrop Frye, *Anatomy
  of Criticism*; Derek Attridge, *Poetic Rhythm* for scansion you can hear.
- *Practice:* scan a hundred lines of verse by hand, marking stress, until
  meter becomes audible rather than theoretical. Memorize and recite five
  hundred lines — memory changes what you notice. Keep a commonplace book.
  Write pastiches of five distinct styles until you can forge a paragraph.
  Read at least one major work in its original language. Do a complete-works
  pass on one author across a year. Write a 6,000-word seminar paper that
  engages real secondary criticism and survives someone attacking it.
- *Primary literature:* *PMLA*, *Critical Inquiry*, *New Literary History*,
  *Representations*; and for criticism that is actually alive, the *LRB*,
  *NYRB* and *TLS*. Landmarks: Auerbach, *Mimesis*; Bakhtin, *The Dialogic
  Imagination*; Said, *Orientalism*; Barthes, *S/Z*.
- *You've arrived when:* given an unseen passage, you can date it within
  fifty years, name its formal features precisely, and teach an hour on it
  with no preparation beyond the page.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Genette; Jameson, *The Political Unconscious*;
  Sedgwick, *Epistemology of the Closet*; Moretti, *Graphs, Maps, Trees*;
  D.F. McKenzie, *Bibliography and the Sociology of Texts*; a scholarly
  editing manual, plus paleography or bibliography if your period demands it.
- *Frontier:* world literature and translation studies; computational and
  distant reading; book history and the material text; ecocriticism; and the
  post-critique argument (Rita Felski, *The Limits of Critique*) over whether
  suspicious reading has exhausted itself.
- *Contribution looks like:* two legitimate channels. Scholarship — a
  monograph, a critical edition, an authoritative new translation. Or
  creative production — your own novels, poems, essays, judged by readers and
  time rather than referees. A major translation is both at once, which is
  why translators occupy an odd, high place in this field.
- *Community:* MLA, ACLA, ALSCW, and the period societies. For the creative
  channel: literary magazines, small presses, and residencies or workshops
  (Iowa, Bread Loaf, Clarion, Cave Canem) where the criticism is unsparing.

## Music (theory + history)

**The question it asks.** Why does organized sound move us in time — what
makes a succession of pitches feel like it is going somewhere, arriving, or
lying to you? Theory asks how music is constructed; history and
ethnomusicology ask why it was constructed *this* way here and some other way
there. The deep answer is that almost nothing in music is universal: tonality
is one culturally elaborated solution among many, and the interesting work is
in the comparison.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Pitch, interval and the overtone series — the physical substrate of
  consonance, and the cultural override that decides what counts as harsh.
- Scale and mode systems as different technologies: major/minor tonality,
  church modes, maqām, rāga, slendro and pelog, pentatonics.
- Functional harmony: tonic–dominant polarity, cadence, voice leading,
  modulation — the engine of Western music's sense of arrival.
- Rhythm and meter beyond the barline: syncopation, polyrhythm, additive
  meters, tāla cycles, clave.
- Form as a listener's map: binary, sonata form, theme and variations, fugue,
  the twelve-bar blues, verse–chorus, cyclic structures.
- Texture and timbre — monophony, homophony, polyphony, heterophony — and
  orchestration as the art of choosing colors.
- Notation as a technology with blind spots: it cannot capture ornamentation,
  microtonality, or most of what oral traditions carry.
- Temperament: just intonation vs equal temperament, the compromise every
  Western keyboard sits on, and what it costs.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- "I don't have an ear." Relative pitch, meter and structural hearing are
  trainable skills; absolute pitch is a party trick largely irrelevant to
  musicianship.
- Thinking theory prescribes. It is descriptive, and it mostly describes
  European practice from roughly 1700 to 1900. Applied to rāga or the blues
  it distorts both.
- Assuming listening more is ear training. It isn't. Structural hearing comes
  from effortful, targeted drilling against feedback, like everything else.

**T3 — Literacy (~200 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Aaron Copland, *What to Listen For in Music*. The door
  because it converts passive listening into attention with a target — you
  finish it hearing form rather than mood.
- *Survey:* Taruskin & Gibbs, *The Oxford History of Western Music: College
  Edition* for the Western line; Alex Ross, *The Rest Is Noise* for the
  twentieth century as narrative; Titon (ed.), *Worlds of Music* for the
  global picture, which the other two do not attempt.
- *Canon:* listen structurally, with a score when you can. Hildegard;
  Josquin; Monteverdi, *L'Orfeo*; Bach, the C-major prelude and fugue from
  *The Well-Tempered Clavier* Book I, then the *Mass in B Minor*; Mozart,
  Symphony No. 40; Beethoven, Symphony No. 5 (track the four-note cell
  everywhere) and the Op. 131 quartet; Schubert, *Winterreise*; the *Tristan*
  prelude; Debussy, *Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune*; Stravinsky, *The
  Rite of Spring*; Ligeti, *Atmosphères*. Then: Ellington; Miles Davis, *Kind
  of Blue* (modal, not functional — hear the difference); Coltrane, *A Love
  Supreme*; Robert Johnson; Aretha Franklin. And beyond the West: a full
  Ravi Shankar rāga performance (follow ālāp into jor into jhālā); Umm
  Kulthūm; Javanese gamelan; Ali Farka Touré. Ask of each: where is the
  tension, and what resolves it?
- *Course:* Open Yale MUSI 112, *Listening to Music* (Craig Wright) — free,
  and the best structural-listening course available anywhere. Coursera's
  *Fundamentals of Music Theory* (Edinburgh) for the mechanics; musictheory.net
  and teoria.com for drills. Bernstein's *The Unanswered Question* Norton
  Lectures are free and remain worth the six hours.
- *Artifact:* 1,500 words on a single four-minute piece: what happens, in
  order, section by section — and identify the moment the music makes a
  promise and the moment it keeps or breaks it.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Kostka & Payne, *Tonal Harmony*, or Laitz, *The Complete
  Musician*; Gauldin, *Harmonic Practice in Tonal Music*; Fux's *Gradus ad
  Parnassum* or Piston's *Counterpoint* for species work; Burkholder, Grout &
  Palisca, *A History of Western Music*, read against the *Norton Anthology
  of Western Music* scores.
- *Practice:* this is the most practice-heavy domain on the map, and it is
  daily. Solfège and sight-singing from Ottman's *Music for Sight Singing*,
  movable-do. Dictation: melodic, then two-part, then full harmonic — fifteen
  minutes a day, no exceptions. Keyboard harmony: every major and minor scale,
  I–IV–V–I in all twelve keys, then harmonize a chorale melody at the
  instrument. Species counterpoint through fifth species, written out.
  Harmonize thirty Bach chorales and compare each to what Bach did.
  Transcribe five improvised solos by ear, note for note. Play in an ensemble
  every week, where being wrong is audible to others.
- *Primary literature:* *Journal of Music Theory*, *Music Theory Spectrum*,
  *Journal of the American Musicological Society*, *Ethnomusicology*.
  Landmarks: Schenker, *Free Composition* (trans. Oster); Leonard Meyer,
  *Emotion and Meaning in Music*; Dahlhaus, *Foundations of Music History*;
  Nettl, *The Study of Ethnomusicology*.
- *You've arrived when:* you can take dictation of a four-part chorale phrase
  at tempo, sight-sing a chromatic melody cold, and produce a Roman-numeral
  or Schenkerian analysis of a Mozart sonata exposition another analyst would
  accept.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Schenker; Allen Forte, *The Structure of
  Atonal Music*; David Lewin, *Generalized Musical Intervals and
  Transformations*; Lerdahl & Jackendoff, *A Generative Theory of Tonal
  Music*; Taruskin's full six-volume *Oxford History of Western Music*.
- *Frontier:* transformational and neo-Riemannian theory; corpus and
  computational musicology; music cognition (Huron's *Sweet Anticipation*);
  performance-as-research; and the ongoing argument over globalizing the
  theory curriculum that Philip Ewell's 2020 critique forced into the open.
- *Contribution looks like:* scholarship — analysis, critical editions,
  archival musicology, fieldwork — or creative production: composition, a
  recorded body of performance, a repertoire made newly audible. Performance
  at this level is a research output, not a hobby, and the field treats it
  that way.
- *Community:* the Society for Music Theory, the American Musicological
  Society, the Society for Ethnomusicology, ICMPC for cognition. For the
  creative channel: commissions, competitions, residencies, and the ensembles
  who will actually play your score.

## Visual art & architecture

**The question it asks.** How does a made object organize seeing — and how is
looking itself trained by period, place and power? Art history's core problem
is recovering what a work meant to the people who made and used it, and
explaining why forms change when they do. Architecture asks all of that under
gravity, budget and use, which makes it the hardest case: the building must
work as an argument and as a roof.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- The formal vocabulary — line, value, color, composition, figure/ground,
  negative space, the picture plane — because you cannot argue about what you
  cannot describe.
- Systems of representation as conventions, not nature: linear perspective is
  an invention with a date, and hierarchical scale or isometric projection
  are answers to the same problem, not failures at it.
- Panofsky's three levels: what is depicted, what it signifies
  (iconography), and what it reveals about a worldview (iconology).
- Medium determines look. Fresco, tempera, oil, woodblock, bronze and
  photography each impose their own possibilities.
- Patronage, workshop and market: who paid, for what setting, under what
  contract, is evidence — not a footnote.
- Modernism's break — flatness, abstraction, the readymade, medium
  specificity — and the collapse of that framework after 1960.
- Architecture's fundamentals: load path (post-and-lintel, arch, vault, dome,
  frame, shell), program, circulation, site, tectonics, and the long ornament
  argument from Ruskin to Loos to Venturi.
- Global traditions on their own terms: Chinese literati painting and the
  brush as a trace of the person; Islamic geometry, calligraphy and
  aniconism; ukiyo-e; West African sculpture and its real effect on European
  modernism.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- "My kid could do that." Most modern art is a move in an argument about what
  art is; the object is the smallest part of the work.
- Judging buildings from photographs. Architecture is experienced in
  sequence, at body scale, in weather, over time. The hero shot lies.
- Assuming Western periodization is universal. "Renaissance" means nothing in
  Beijing or Benin, and forcing it produces nonsense.

**T3 — Literacy (~200 hrs)**
- *Start here:* John Berger, *Ways of Seeing* — the book and the four free
  BBC episodes. Short, and the door because it makes visible the ideology in
  how you already look, before you have learned any names.
- *Survey:* E.H. Gombrich, *The Story of Art*, for one great voice moving
  through the Western tradition (and honest about being that). Then Stokstad's
  *Art History* or Gardner's *Art Through the Ages* for the global coverage
  Gombrich doesn't attempt. For buildings: Spiro Kostof, *A History of
  Architecture: Settings and Rituals*, plus Ching's *Architecture: Form,
  Space and Order*, which teaches spatial thinking by drawing.
- *Canon:* look, in person wherever possible. Lascaux; Egyptian tomb
  painting; the Parthenon sculptures; the Pantheon, standing under the
  oculus; Hagia Sophia; the Great Mosque of Córdoba; Angkor Wat; Chartres;
  Giotto's Arena Chapel; Masaccio's *Trinity* (perspective's proof of
  concept); Fan Kuan's *Travelers Among Mountains and Streams*;
  Michelangelo's unfinished *Prisoners*; Velázquez, *Las Meninas*;
  Rembrandt's late self-portraits; Hokusai; Turner; Manet's *Olympia*;
  Cézanne; Picasso's *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*; Duchamp's *Fountain*;
  Rothko; Kahlo; El Anatsui. Buildings: Brunelleschi's dome; Palladio's Villa
  Rotonda; Katsura Imperial Villa; the Crystal Palace; Wright's Fallingwater;
  Le Corbusier's Villa Savoye and Ronchamp; Mies's Farnsworth House; Kahn's
  Salk Institute; Lina Bo Bardi's MASP; Kéré's school at Gando. Look for what
  the work does with your eye's path and your body's position.
- *Course:* Smarthistory (free, with Khan Academy) is the best free
  art-history resource that exists — hundreds of works, two experts talking
  in front of each one. MoMA's free Coursera courses for modern art. MIT
  OpenCourseWare's global history of architecture course for the non-Western
  half most surveys skip.
- *Artifact:* 1,500 words on one object you saw in person. Five hundred words
  of pure description with no interpretation, then what it was for, then what
  you claim it does to a viewer — with the description as your evidence.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Stokstad or Gardner as reference; *Art Since 1900* (Foster,
  Krauss, Bois, Buchloh) for the modern and contemporary; Panofsky, *Studies
  in Iconology*; Wölfflin, *Principles of Art History*; Kostof and Ching's
  *Building Construction Illustrated* if the built environment is your side.
- *Practice:* draw, daily. Betty Edwards's *Drawing on the Right Side of the
  Brain* to break symbol-drawing, then Nicolaïdes's *The Natural Way to Draw*
  on its own schedule. A hundred hours of life drawing. Copy master drawings
  line for line — copying teaches what looking alone cannot. Learn one medium
  properly: oil, etching, or clay. Drill slide identification on three
  hundred works: artist, region, date within twenty-five years. Write formal
  analyses to museum-label discipline. Measure and hand-draw a real building
  in plan and section.
- *Primary literature:* *The Art Bulletin*, *October*, *Art History*, and the
  *Journal of the Society of Architectural Historians*. Landmarks: Baxandall,
  *Painting and Experience in Fifteenth-Century Italy*; T.J. Clark, *The
  Painting of Modern Life*; Alpers, *The Art of Describing*; Krauss, *The
  Originality of the Avant-Garde and Other Modernist Myths*.
- *You've arrived when:* shown an unlabeled work, you can place it by period,
  region, medium and function, defend the attribution from visual evidence
  alone, and lecture on it for forty-five minutes.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Riegl, *Late Roman Art Industry*; Warburg and
  the *Mnemosyne Atlas*; Belting, *Likeness and Presence*; the Krauss/Foster
  line in full; for architecture, Frampton's *Studies in Tectonic Culture*
  and *Modern Architecture: A Critical History*, and Tafuri.
- *Frontier:* global and decolonial art history and the restitution debates;
  technical art history (imaging, pigment and dendro analysis); the material
  and ecological turns; digital reconstruction; and architecture's reckoning
  with embodied carbon and adaptive reuse.
- *Contribution looks like:* scholarship — a monograph, a catalogue
  raisonné, conservation research, or an exhibition mounted as an argument
  (curating is genuine scholarship here, not publicity). Or creative
  production: a sustained body of work, exhibited and written about, or
  buildings that get built.
- *Community:* the College Art Association, the Society of Architectural
  Historians, the Renaissance Society of America, the AAH. For practice:
  galleries, juried exhibitions and biennials; for architects, the RIBA/AIA
  world and open competitions.

## Theater, film & narrative media

**The question it asks.** How does a story told through bodies, images and
time produce belief and feeling in an audience that knows perfectly well it
is watching a fabrication? And how do a medium's material constraints — a
stage's liveness and shared air, a cut's instantaneity, a series' years of
duration — determine what stories can be told in it at all? Dramatic theory
is the oldest continuous branch of this: Aristotle's questions are still the
ones a script doctor asks.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- Dramatic structure: action, reversal (*peripeteia*), recognition
  (*anagnorisis*); want vs need; obstacle, stakes, and the scene as the unit
  in which something changes.
- The founding dispute over where cinematic meaning lives: montage
  (Kuleshov, Eisenstein) vs mise-en-scène and the long take (Bazin).
- Continuity editing as an invisible convention — the 180-degree rule,
  shot/reverse-shot, eyeline match — and what deliberately breaking it does
  to a viewer.
- The shot's variables: framing and scale, lens, movement, lighting key,
  depth of field, duration, and sound (diegetic and non-diegetic).
- Suspense vs surprise — Hitchcock's bomb under the table — as the clearest
  demonstration that information management *is* the craft.
- Genre as a contract with the audience; the auteur theory and its limit,
  which is that film is collaborative industrial labor.
- Theater's plural traditions: the Greek chorus, Sanskrit *rasa* theory,
  Noh's restraint, commedia's types, Stanislavski's identification against
  Brecht's alienation effect.
- Industry as form: studio systems, star systems, censorship codes,
  festivals, and now algorithmic distribution all shape what gets made.

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Treating film as a delivery mechanism for plot. Most of the meaning is in
  duration, framing, performance and sound — precisely what a summary loses.
- "The book was better." Adaptation is not transcription; the question worth
  asking is what each change buys in the new medium.
- Mistaking screenwriting heuristics — three acts, the hero's journey, beat
  sheets — for descriptive theory. They are useful industrial craft, and they
  are not how films actually work.

**T3 — Literacy (~200 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Aristotle, *Poetics* (Malcolm Heath's Penguin translation).
  Forty pages, and the door because every story manual sold today is a
  diluted restatement of it — reading the source lets you see what they left
  out.
- *Survey:* Bordwell & Thompson, *Film Art: An Introduction* — the standard,
  and it genuinely teaches you to see rather than to summarize. Their *Film
  History* for the timeline; Brockett & Hildy's *History of the Theatre* for
  the stage.
- *Canon:* watch with the remote in hand and rewatch. Lumière and Méliès
  shorts; Keaton's *Sherlock Jr.*; Eisenstein's *Battleship Potemkin* (the
  Odessa Steps, shot by shot); Renoir's *La Règle du jeu*; Welles's *Citizen
  Kane* for deep focus; Ozu's *Tokyo Story*; De Sica's *Bicycle Thieves*;
  Kurosawa's *Rashomon* and *Seven Samurai*; Ray's *Pather Panchali*;
  Bergman's *Persona*; Godard's *Breathless* for the jump cuts; Hitchcock's
  *Vertigo*; Kubrick's *2001*; Tarkovsky's *Stalker*; Sembène's *Black Girl*;
  Akerman's *Jeanne Dielman*; Kiarostami's *Close-Up*; Wong Kar-wai's *In the
  Mood for Love*; Miyazaki's *Spirited Away*; Bong's *Parasite*. Theater —
  and see it live, not filmed: Sophocles' *Oedipus*, Kālidāsa's *Shakuntala*,
  Zeami's Noh plays, *Hamlet*, Ibsen's *A Doll's House*, Chekhov's *The
  Cherry Orchard*, Brecht's *Mother Courage*, Beckett's *Waiting for Godot*,
  Hansberry's *A Raisin in the Sun*, Kushner's *Angels in America*.
- *Course:* Tony Zhou's *Every Frame a Painting* video essays are free and
  better than most paid courses at teaching you to see a cut. David
  Bordwell's *Observations on Film Art* blog is a working scholar thinking in
  public. Beyond those, no free film-studies course is worth choosing over
  simply watching films with the *Film Art* vocabulary in hand.
- *Artifact:* a shot-by-shot analysis of one three-minute sequence. Log every
  shot — scale, angle, movement, duration, sound — then argue what the
  pattern accomplishes. 1,500 words plus the log.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* Bordwell, *Narration in the Fiction Film*; Bordwell, Staiger &
  Thompson, *The Classical Hollywood Cinema*; Michel Chion, *Audio-Vision*;
  Walter Murch, *In the Blink of an Eye*. For theater: the *Nāṭyaśāstra*
  (Rangacharya), Stanislavski's *An Actor's Work* (Benedetti), *Brecht on
  Theatre* (Willett), Peter Brook's *The Empty Space*.
- *Practice:* shot-log an entire feature — every cut, timed. Then make
  things: shoot a five-shot sequence with no dialogue that reads clearly to a
  stranger; cut the same footage two ways and show both to someone; direct or
  act in one scene with real rehearsal and real notes; write a ten-minute
  play and sit through a table read; memorize and perform a soliloquy; block
  a scene on paper before you block it on your feet. Rewatching is the
  analytic act — the second and third viewings are where the work is.
- *Primary literature:* *Screen*, *Film Quarterly*, *JCMS*, *TDR: The Drama
  Review*, *Theatre Journal*; and the critics who shaped seeing — Bazin's
  *What Is Cinema?*, Pauline Kael, Manny Farber. Landmarks: Bazin; Laura
  Mulvey, "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" (1975); Tom Gunning, "The
  Cinema of Attractions."
- *You've arrived when:* you can watch an unfamiliar film once and afterwards
  reconstruct its structure — act turns, coverage strategy, sound design —
  and defend a reading against someone who saw it differently.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Deleuze, *Cinema 1: The Movement-Image* and
  *Cinema 2: The Time-Image*; Metz, *Film Language*; Bordwell, *On the
  History of Film Style*; Miriam Hansen, *Babel and Babylon*; Schechner,
  *Performance Theory*; Carlson, *Performance: A Critical Introduction*.
- *Frontier:* media archaeology; platform and streaming studies; global and
  diasporic cinemas displacing the national-cinema frame; performance studies
  expanding well past theater; archival restoration; and the ethics and
  aesthetics of synthetic images.
- *Contribution looks like:* scholarship — a monograph, archival recovery, a
  restoration that changes what a film is understood to be. Or creative
  production: films, productions, plays. In the creative channel festivals
  and commissioning theaters function as peer review, and they are not
  softer than journals.
- *Community:* the Society for Cinema and Media Studies, ATHE, ASTR; FIAF for
  archives; Cannes, Berlinale, Sundance and Rotterdam for the creative track,
  plus new-play development programs for the stage.

## Rhetoric & writing (as an art)

**The question it asks.** How does language move people — and can persuasion
be an ethical art rather than a bag of tricks? Aristotle's framing is still
the working one: rhetoric is the faculty of observing, in any given case, the
available means of persuasion. Underneath sits a fight two and a half
millennia old and unresolved, between Plato's charge that rhetoric flatters
in place of truth and the Isocratean-Ciceronian answer that a civilization
without trained public speech has no way of deciding anything together.

**Big ideas to walk away with.**
- The rhetorical situation: audience, purpose, occasion (*kairos*),
  constraints. Nothing is well written in the abstract.
- Ethos, pathos and logos — and the five canons: invention, arrangement,
  style, memory, delivery. Modern writing advice covers the third and ignores
  the first two, which is why it stalls.
- Stasis theory: identifying what kind of question is actually in dispute —
  fact, definition, quality, or procedure. Most arguments fail here.
- *Topoi* as invention machinery: systematic ways to find something to say.
- Style at sentence level: characters as subjects, actions as verbs; the
  known-new contract; periodic vs cumulative construction; the standard
  figures (parallelism, antithesis, chiasmus) with their effects.
- Structure for readers, not writers: point-first movement, and Gopen &
  Swan's reader-expectation account of where readers look for what.
- Revision as the actual writing — the shift from writer-based to
  reader-based prose is the whole discipline.
- The dark mirror: propaganda, euphemism, and manipulation. Klemperer's *LTI*
  and Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" (flawed as linguistics,
  indispensable as ethics).

**What outsiders get wrong.**
- Believing clarity is innate talent. It is a small set of learnable
  sentence-level moves plus ruthless cutting, and it is measurable.
- Treating Strunk & White as law. It is a period style guide containing real
  errors of grammatical description — take its ethic of concision, leave its
  rules, and read Pullum's critique once for calibration.
- Equating rhetoric with dishonesty. It is the study of how persuasion
  operates; you cannot reliably detect manipulation you have no name for.

**T3 — Literacy (~150 hrs)**
- *Start here:* Joseph M. Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*.
  The door because it is the only book that reliably improves prose on a
  single pass — it gives you diagnosable patterns to find and fix rather than
  taste to acquire.
- *Survey:* Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style*, for the modern linguistic
  account; William Zinsser, *On Writing Well*, for nonfiction craft and
  temperament; Ward Farnsworth's *Classical English Rhetoric* for the figures
  demonstrated on real sentences rather than defined in a glossary.
- *Canon:* Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, Books I–II (George Kennedy's *On Rhetoric*
  is the standard translation); Plato's *Gorgias* for the prosecution;
  Cicero, *De Oratore*; Longinus, *On the Sublime*. Then the speeches and
  essays themselves, which are the field's real canon: Pericles' Funeral
  Oration in Thucydides; Lincoln's Gettysburg Address and Second Inaugural;
  Frederick Douglass, "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?"; Martin
  Luther King Jr., "Letter from Birmingham Jail"; Montaigne's *Essays*
  (Donald Frame's translation); Orwell's essays; Baldwin, *Notes of a Native
  Son*; Didion, *Slouching Towards Bethlehem*. Read each twice — once for
  what it says, once for how it arranges what it says.
- *Course:* say it plainly — no course substitutes for an editor who will
  cut your work. That said, two free things are genuinely worth the hours:
  Larry McEnerney's *The Craft of Writing Effectively* lecture (University of
  Chicago Writing Program, eighty minutes, on YouTube), and Kristin Sainani's
  *Writing in the Sciences* (Stanford, free on Coursera) even if you never
  write science. Gopen & Swan's "The Science of Scientific Writing"
  (*American Scientist*, 1990) is free and worth an afternoon.
- *Artifact:* take 1,000 words of your own older writing and cut it to 600
  without losing content. Publish both versions plus a paragraph naming each
  *class* of cut you made. The taxonomy is the proof, not the shorter draft.

**T2 — Working depth**
- *Spine:* *The Chicago Manual of Style* and *Garner's Modern English Usage*
  as references; Bizzell & Herzberg, *The Rhetorical Tradition*, as the
  field's anthology; Corbett & Connors, *Classical Rhetoric for the Modern
  Student*, which is a textbook with actual exercises.
- *Practice:* imitation, which is how this was taught for two thousand years.
  Copy passages by hand from writers you admire, then write your own on the
  same armature. Work the *progymnasmata* — the classical exercise sequence,
  fable through thesis — in order; Kennedy's translation gives you the
  originals, Corbett the modern version. Convert ten nominalizations to verbs
  before breakfast. Rewrite one paragraph three ways: periodic, cumulative,
  plain. Read everything aloud. Deliver a memorized ten-minute speech to real
  people. Keep a file of every change an editor makes to your work and
  pattern-match your own tics until you can pre-empt them.
- *Primary literature:* *College Composition and Communication*, *Rhetoric
  Society Quarterly*, *Rhetorica*, *Written Communication*. Landmarks:
  Kenneth Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*; Perelman & Olbrechts-Tyteca, *The
  New Rhetoric*; Richard Lanham, *Analyzing Prose*; Lloyd Bitzer, "The
  Rhetorical Situation."
- *You've arrived when:* you can take someone else's tangled draft, make it
  clearly better while keeping their voice, and justify every single change
  as a principle rather than a preference.

**T1 — Mastery**
- *Graduate texts/monographs:* Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*; Perelman;
  Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument*; George Kennedy, *A New History of
  Classical Rhetoric*; Halliday's functional grammar for the linguistic
  underpinning of style claims.
- *Frontier:* digital and algorithmic rhetoric; misinformation and
  platform-scale persuasion; comparative rhetoric beyond the Greco-Roman line
  (Chinese, Arabic, and African oral traditions); writing-transfer research;
  the rhetoric of science and of data visualization.
- *Contribution looks like:* scholarship — rhetorical criticism, histories of
  the tradition, empirical composition research. Or the art itself: a body of
  essays, books or speeches that changes how other people write. This field
  is unusual in that its own heroes are frequently practitioners, and nobody
  in it thinks the practitioner track is lesser.
- *Community:* the Rhetoric Society of America, CCCC, and the International
  Society for the History of Rhetoric. For the craft channel: magazines,
  working editors, and a workshop staffed by people willing to hurt your
  feelings on schedule.
