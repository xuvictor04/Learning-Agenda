# The Frontier — A Mobile Slot for What Doesn't Exist Yet

`02-map.md` lists 44 domains and `03-spine.md` sequences them across thirty
years. Both files are wrong about the year 2060, and there is no version of
them that isn't. This file is the part of the system that moves.

---

## 1. Why a fixed map goes stale

The map's failure mode is not that it's badly drawn. It's that the territory
moves under it, in four distinct ways.

**Fields get invented.** Molecular biology did not exist as a discipline
when Watson and Crick published in 1953; by 1966 the genetic code was
solved, by 1973 you could splice genes, and by 1983 PCR made it routine. A
biologist who took a degree in 1950 watched an entire field appear, mature,
and become the center of their science inside one working life — and it had
no slot on anyone's map. Computer science departments barely existed before
1962. Neuroscience got its own society in 1969. Cognitive science,
bioinformatics, and ancient DNA arrived the same way: not as new rooms in
the house, but as new houses.

**Existing domains get transformed from underneath.** Archaeology was about
pottery typologies and stratigraphy until radiocarbon dating arrived in
1949, and then it was a domain with absolute chronology. Seventy years
later, ancient DNA did it again — human prehistory has been substantially
rewritten since about 2010, by an instrument invented in genetics. The
domain name on the map is unchanged. Nearly everything under it is
different.

**Domains merge, split, and die.** Natural philosophy split into physics and
chemistry; physical chemistry and molecular biology are merges; phrenology
and phlogiston chemistry are dead. Some entry on your current ledger will
be, by 2060, either three entries or none.

**And principle 5 will lie to you.** `01-principles.md` says tools with
short half-lives are learned just-in-time and never scheduled years ahead,
and that is correct and load-bearing. But every paradigm shift arrives
disguised as a tool. The microscope was a curiosity for two decades before
Hooke and Leeuwenhoek turned it into cell biology. Deep learning was a
technique that had failed twice before 2012. If you apply the half-life rule
mechanically, you will file the next revolution under "tooling, learn on
demand" and sleep through it.

The asymmetry is what makes this tractable. **Missing a fad costs you
nothing** — zero hours spent, zero years lost. **Missing a genuine shift
costs a decade**, because you find out at 45 that the field you have depth
in has been reorganized around something you never learned, and the people
who did learn it started at 25. The costs aren't symmetric, so your decision
procedure shouldn't be either. The rest of this file pays a small fixed
premium against a large asymmetric risk — without having to be right in
advance.

## 2. The frontier slot

**A permanent, rolling allocation of roughly 5–10% of learning hours —
every year, for all seventy — reserved for things not on the map.**

At the spine's ten hours a week that's 25–50 hours a year, taken off the
ledger: one T3 domain a year runs slightly lean, or one year in ten does 1.5
domains instead of 2. When the ledger completes around year 30 and its 250
hours come free, the frontier slot grows rather than shrinks — that's the
whole point of it.

It is not the curiosity budget. The curiosity budget (`02-map.md`) is for
pull and pleasure: off-ledger, no tier, no artifact, and it must stay that
way or the joy leaks out of the system. **The frontier slot is deliberate
reconnaissance with an output.** Every year it produces at minimum a written
list of what you looked at, what you concluded, and what changed since last
year. That list goes in your annual review.

It's called *mobile* because what fills it changes continuously while the
slot itself never moves. The slot is a fixed piece of infrastructure. Its
contents in 2031 and 2061 should have almost nothing in common.

## 3. Triage: telling a shift from a fad

This is the part that does the work. Apply these to any candidate — a
technology, an emerging field, a method, an instrument.

**1. Does it change what can be *asked*, or only how existing answers get
produced?** This is the strongest single test. Instrument revolutions change
the questions. Before the telescope, "are there other moons?" was not an
answerable question; after 1610 it was a Tuesday. Before sequencing, "how
closely related are these two species?" was a morphology argument; after
Sanger in 1977 it was a computation. Cheaper answers to old questions are
useful. New askable questions are a field.

**2. Is it crossing domains, or contained in one?** A thing that shows up
independently in three unrelated fields at once is usually real. Statistics
appeared in agriculture, genetics, and industrial quality control. Bayesian
methods surfaced in ecology, epidemiology, and machine translation. If a
thing is only discussed by the people selling it, it isn't crossing
anything.

**3. Are serious practitioners in adjacent fields changing their methods?**
Not their opinions — their methods. Structural biologists rebuilt their labs
around cryo-EM after roughly 2013, because the resolution revolution made it
better than crystallography for problems they already had. Watch what labs
buy and what methods sections say, not what keynotes say.

**4. Does it have a falsifiable core, or only a narrative and a market?**
Ask what observation would prove it doesn't work. Cold fusion in 1989 had a
falsifiable core, which is exactly why it died within months when
replication failed. A technology whose promise is always five years out, and
which has no experiment that could embarrass it, is a market, not a field.

**5. Cost curve.** Is it getting cheaper by orders of magnitude?
Transistors; sequencing (roughly $100M per genome in 2001, about $1,000 a
decade later — faster than Moore's law); solar modules (around $100/W in the
mid-1970s, well under $1/W now); lithium-ion packs (down ~90% since 2010);
launch cost. Order-of-magnitude declines change *who* can ask questions,
which is how instruments become fields. A flat cost curve after ten years of
investment is diagnostic.

**6. Who is against it, and are their objections technical or economic?**
If the objection is "the physics doesn't work," take it seriously — those
objections usually hold. If the objection is "it's too expensive" or "it
would obsolete our equipment," the thing usually happens on the cost curve's
schedule. Sequencing's skeptics in 1990 were making an economic argument;
cold fusion's were making a technical one. Only one of those fields exists.

**7. The five-year test.** Would a competent practitioner five years from
now be *unable to work* without it? A molecular biologist without PCR in
1995, an astronomer without CCDs in 1990, a structural biologist without
cryo-EM in 2022 — not disadvantaged, unable. If you can't imagine that
sentence being true, it's a watch item.

### The false positives, honestly

Several things pass three or four of these tests and still don't matter, or
don't matter for twenty years.

- **Graphene** (Nobel 2010): real physics, real cost curve, cross-domain
  interest. The application timeline was off by decades.
- **Consumer 3D printing** around 2012 passed the cost-curve and
  cross-domain tests. Additive manufacturing became genuinely important in
  aerospace and prototyping; the printer in every home did not happen.
- **Gene therapy** in the 1990s was right about everything except the
  decade — it became real around 2017. Being early is indistinguishable from
  being wrong, at the time.
- **Blockchain beyond currency:** fifteen years, enormous capital,
  cross-domain claims, and a persistently thin falsifiable core outside its
  original use. Narrative and market, mostly.
- **Fifth-generation computing** (Japan, 1982–1992) had a national budget, a
  clear technical program, and serious practitioners. It produced an AI
  winter.

The correct response to most candidates is **watch, don't invest.** You
aren't trying to be right about each one. You're making sure that when one
is real, you notice within a couple of years rather than a couple of
decades.

## 4. The three responses

Every triaged candidate gets exactly one of these. No fourth option, and
nothing sits untriaged.

**Watch** — a few hours a year. One feed or one survey, an annual check-in
during the review, and a line in the frontier list saying what changed. This
is the default and should hold 80% of candidates, permanently. Some things
are watched for twenty years and then promoted; that's a success, not a
failure of nerve.

**Literacy pass** — the standard T3 treatment from `02-map.md`: one survey
work, one primary source, one artifact you write and publish. Treat it as a
provisional new ledger domain. **Trigger:** it passes test 1 (changes the
questions) plus either test 2 (crossing domains) or test 3 (practitioners
changing methods). That's 100–300 hours, which the frontier slot funds over
two to three years, or one year if you pull from the ledger.

**Promotion to the map** — it becomes a real domain in `02-map.md` with a
tier, and takes a place in `03-spine.md` with a prerequisite argument like
everything else. **Trigger:** the literacy artifact exists, the five-year
test now reads as obviously true rather than plausible, and it has survived
two annual reviews.

**Promotion happens at an annual review, never in the moment of
excitement.** `02-map.md`'s guardrail — everything enters at T3, nothing
skips the line on enthusiasm — applies with double force here, because
excitement is the precise emotion a hype cycle is engineered to produce.
Add the frontier list to `templates/annual-review.md` as a standing item
alongside the map check.

## 5. Keeping watch without drowning

Reconnaissance is cheap if you buy it in the right form. The rule: **buy
compression, not volume.**

- **Survey and review papers over news.** *Annual Review of* — anything —
  exists for exactly this, and one 30-page review substitutes for two years
  of headlines.
- **The "what changed this year" pieces.** Good journals publish them; read
  the ones for domains you hold at T2 or better.
- **Preprint servers scanned by title only.** arXiv, bioRxiv, SSRN. Ten
  minutes a week, titles and nothing else. You're looking for words you
  don't recognize appearing repeatedly — that's the signal.
- **One or two trusted practitioners per area, not general feeds.** Someone
  who works in the field and writes honestly about it is worth a hundred
  aggregators. General feeds optimize for engagement, which selects for
  hype — the exact quantity you're trying to filter out.
- **Conference programs, read as a diff.** What's on the schedule this year
  that wasn't three years ago?

`05-current.md` handles the ongoing-awareness half, and the two files do
different jobs. **Current awareness is breadth maintenance** — not falling
behind in domains you already hold, keeping literacy from decaying into a
memory of literacy. **The frontier slot is about structural change** —
noticing when the shape of the map is wrong, not when a fact in it is out
of date. Different cadences, sources, and outputs. Don't let one absorb the
other; the frontier slot loses that fight every time, because current news
is always more urgent and never more important.

## 6. Re-foundations: when a domain you already own goes stale

The other half of the mobility problem. A field you brought to literacy at
24 will have moved substantially by 54 — not because you forgot it, but
because it changed. Someone who learned geology in 1955 learned it without
plate tectonics. Someone who learned genetics in 1970 learned it without
sequencing. They weren't wrong; they were early.

**Run a re-foundation pass every 15–20 years per domain**, or sooner on
either trigger: you can no longer follow a current talk in a field you once
knew, or the field's methods sections read like a foreign language.

A re-foundation is not a re-read of your old notes. It's a survey work
published in the last three years, a current primary source, and a new
artifact — the T3 treatment again at maybe half the hours, because the
scaffolding is still there. Budget 50–150 hours. Three domains a year on a
rolling basis covers all 44 on a fifteen-year cycle, about 200 hours a year.

**This is why the spine's later decades aren't empty even though the ledger
completes around year 30.** Years 31–70 are the frontier slot, the
re-foundation cycle, the synthesis and stewardship tracks, and the spikes.
There is more than enough to do; the composition shifts from acquisition to
maintenance and reconnaissance.

Say this part plainly, because it's where people fail: **being taught the
new version of your own field by someone half your age is the normal and
correct experience.** Every biologist who learned recombinant DNA in the
mid-1970s learned it from a postdoc. The alternative to being taught by
someone younger is not being taught. Principle 7 in `01-principles.md` asks
you to review the system itself; this is the part that most often needs it,
because the obstacle is ego rather than time.

## 7. The seventy-year view

You are 20 in 2026. If you make 90, you will have lived through a span of
change comparable to 1955–2025. Take that comparison seriously for a moment.

In 1955: the structure of DNA was two years old and the genetic code
unsolved. Continental drift was a fringe idea respectable geologists mocked;
plate tectonics wouldn't be accepted for another decade. No integrated
circuit, no microprocessor, no personal computer, no internet, no CT scan,
no PCR, no confirmed exoplanet, no evidence for dark energy, and quarks not
yet proposed. Nearly every tool a working scientist uses daily in 2025 did
not exist, and several of the fields they work in had no names.

That is your span. Stated as a threat it's paralyzing: most of what will
matter to you hasn't been invented, and no plan written in 2026 can contain
it. Stated correctly it's the best feature of the whole agenda. **You are
guaranteed to witness the birth of fields that have no name yet** — and few
people get to watch that and understand what they're seeing. The system's
whole job here is to make sure you notice, and that when you do you can
still join: fundamentals enough to read the new thing, breadth enough to
recognize it as new, and 25 to 50 hours a year already budgeted.

This file predicts nothing about what those fields will be, and neither
should you — that's precisely the mistake it's designed to prevent. Keeping
a list of current candidates as *illustrations* is fine, so long as you
state plainly that any such list, reread in 2046, will look quaint in a way
you cannot presently detect. The 1985 version would have said "expert
systems" and "fifth-generation computing," and would not have said "the
web."

The list is disposable. The slot is not.

## 8. Failure modes

**Chasing every new thing.** The slot has a hard cap for a reason. Without
it, reconnaissance eats the ledger and you end up with encyclopedic
awareness of things that turned out not to matter, and depth in nothing. The
cap is the difference between reconnaissance and distraction.

**Dismissing everything as hype.** The mirror error, and the more common one
after 40. It feels like wisdom, it's cheap, and it's right most of the time
— which is exactly what makes it dangerous, because being right nine times
costs less than being wrong once. Metcalfe predicted the internet's collapse
in 1996. Deep learning was correctly dismissed twice before 2012 and
disastrously dismissed the third time. If your frontier list has produced
zero literacy passes in a decade, your filter is broken.

**Mistaking a tool for a field.** Both directions. Learning a product with a
version number as though it were a domain violates principle 5 and
`02-map.md`'s guardrail. Filing a genuine paradigm shift under "tooling"
because it first appeared as a tool is the failure this file exists to
prevent. Test 1 — does it change what can be asked? — separates them, and
it's worth applying slowly.

**Letting the slot get colonized by whatever is loudest.** The candidates
that reach you unbidden are, by construction, the ones with the largest
marketing budgets. Seed the frontier list deliberately from sources with no
commercial interest: review journals, conference diffs, people whose careers
don't depend on the answer. If your list looks like the technology news
cycle, it isn't reconnaissance — it's absorption.

**The seniority trap.** Expertise in the old insulates you from the new, and
pleasantly. You get consulted about what you already know, rewarded for the
depth you have, and the questions you're asked stop being ones you can't
answer. The countermeasure is structural, not attitudinal: **reverse
mentoring.** Arrange to be taught by someone twenty or thirty years younger,
in a domain where they're ahead of you — GE ran this as formal policy from
1999 for exactly this reason. One a year from your forties on, on the
calendar, as a student rather than a senior person graciously listening.
