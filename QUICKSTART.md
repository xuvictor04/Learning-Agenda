# Quickstart — Your First 90 Days

This repo is roughly 13,000 lines long and covers seventy years. That is a
good reason to admire it and never begin. This page exists so you can begin
today, without reading any of it.

A plan that isn't started is worth nothing. Everything below is small enough
to do and specific enough that you can't hide behind planning.

---

## 1. Before anything else — 30 minutes, today

Four things. None is optional, all four fit in half an hour, and the total
cost is zero.

- [ ] **Install Anki. Make one deck. Add three cards.** Take them from
      something you already know — a fact from your job, a word you looked
      up last week, anything. The point is not a good deck. The point is
      that the habit has a start date, and that date is today. Principle 2
      calls this the highest-leverage habit in the agenda; the version of it
      that matters is the one running tomorrow morning.
- [ ] **Get a library card.** Free, and `resources/kit.md` calls it the
      highest-leverage item in the whole file by an enormous margin — books,
      interlibrary loan reaching almost anything, and the part most people
      never discover: remote access to JSTOR, newspaper archives, often
      O'Reilly.
- [ ] **Create your two log files.** Copy `templates/annual-plan.md` to
      `log/2026-plan.md` and `templates/weekly-log.md` to
      `log/2026-weekly.md`. Leave the plan mostly blank for now — you'll
      fill it in on day 7, when you know what you're actually committing to.
- [ ] **Pick where you publish, and publish 300 words today.** A blog, a
      newsletter, a public repo, a forum you post under your own name — the
      platform is irrelevant and choosing one is not a decision worth an
      evening. Then write 300 words about anything and put them up.

**The first piece being bad is load-bearing.** You are not publishing to be
read this week. You are destroying the standard that would otherwise stop
you in week 6, when the piece is half-finished on Sunday night and the
tempting move is to skip a week rather than ship something mediocre. If your
public record already opens with something bad, there is nothing left to
protect. Make the first one bad on purpose and you have removed the most
common cause of death for this entire agenda.

## 2. Week 1

Seven days, 60–90 minutes each. Do them in order — the order is the argument.

- [ ] **Day 1 — Read `01-principles.md`, all of it, once.** Eight
      principles, and every later file assumes them. As you read, write
      three Anki cards from it in your own words. That's your first day of
      review load and your first pass at the habit that carries everything.
- [ ] **Day 2 — Read `02-map.md`, then fill in the fundamentals table
      honestly.** Rate yourself novice / working / strong on all eight rows.
      Honestly is the whole instruction: a flattering self-rating routes you
      around the exact weakness that will tax every one of the 44 domains.
      If you're unsure on a row, open `resources/fundamentals.md` and read
      that row's "what strong actually looks like" — the statements are
      written to be testable rather than kind. Check off any ledger box you
      could genuinely pass the literacy bar in today.
- [ ] **Day 3 — Watch Stat 110 lecture 1 (Joe Blitzstein, Harvard, free on
      YouTube).** Take notes by hand. Write four cards. Do not watch lecture
      2 today — the failure mode in `resources/fundamentals.md` is watching
      lectures, and you beat it by pairing every lecture with problems, not
      by accumulating lectures.
- [ ] **Day 4 — Read Plato's *Euthyphro*.** Grube translation, in Hackett's
      *The Trial and Death of Socrates*. It's about twenty pages, your
      library has it, and it is free in older translations if it doesn't.
      Read it for what Socrates is *doing* to Euthyphro, not for the
      conclusion — there isn't one, and that's the lesson. Write the
      Euthyphro dilemma out in your own words when you finish.
- [ ] **Day 5 — Start Adler & Van Doren, *How to Read a Book*.** Read the
      chapters on inspectional reading, then immediately inspect one book
      you own in 45 minutes and write a five-sentence verdict: thesis,
      structure, quality of evidence, who it's for, read or skip. You've now
      used the method once, which beats finishing the book.
- [ ] **Day 6 — Draft this week's piece.** 500 words on one thing you
      learned this week — the Euthyphro dilemma is right there, and so is
      whatever confused you in lecture 1. Draft it, leave it overnight.
- [ ] **Day 7 — Cut it by a quarter, publish it, then set up the ritual.**
      Pick the fixed weekly log time now — Sunday evening works, and
      `templates/weekly-log.md` says so — put it in your calendar as a
      recurring event, and write week 1's row. Five minutes, a few words per
      column, not paragraphs. Then finish `log/2026-plan.md`: your two big
      investments are Statistics & probability and Philosophy, your artifacts
      are the two literacy pieces, and your weekly hours are the table in
      section 3 below.

## 3. Month 1 — building the three habits

Content is not the job this month. The three habits are, because they are
what carry the other sixty-nine years. A month of perfect statistics with no
habits is worth less than a month of mediocre statistics with three habits
running.

**Daily Anki, 15 minutes.** Every day, including travel days. The rule that
makes it work: **cards come from your own study, in your own words, written
while you read** — not in a later session that never happens, and never from
a downloaded deck. Downloaded decks encode someone else's compression of a
book you didn't read; you'll pass the card and fail the concept. Keep the
intake rate low enough that reviews stay under 20 minutes. Any card you fail
three times is a writing problem — rewrite it smaller instead of grinding it.

**Weekly publishing, 500+ words, with your name on it.** Draft Saturday,
sleep on it, cut a quarter Sunday, publish. The honest part:
`resources/fundamentals.md` is blunt that **feedback is the bottleneck**, and
publishing into silence for ten years produces a practiced writer with all
the original bad habits intact. So go get some. In month 1, do one of these:
find one reader who owes you honesty and will say what's wrong; join or start
a writing group with a standing schedule; read every piece aloud before
posting, which catches rhythm problems your eye skips. The brutal one, worth
more than the rest: ask a reader to summarize your piece back to you, and
treat every distortion as your fault, not theirs. Later in the year, a paid
editor once on a real piece is the fastest money you can spend on writing.

**The weekly log, 5 minutes, fixed time.** Its only job is to make drift
visible in weeks instead of years. An "empty week" row is a valid entry and
you should write it rather than skip it — the skipped rows are the data.

### The weekly rhythm

Ten focused hours, which is what the whole agenda assumes. Here is where
they go in the first 90 days:

| Day | What happens | Hours |
|-----|--------------|-------|
| Mon | Anki · Stat 110 lecture + handwritten notes + cards | 1.25 |
| Tue | Anki · probability problems, solutions covered | 1.25 |
| Wed | Anki · philosophy reading (primary text, not summary) | 1.25 |
| Thu | Anki · probability problems or statistics reading | 1.25 |
| Fri | Anki · fundamentals — Adler, then Williams' *Style* | 1.0 |
| Sat | Anki · draft the week's piece | 2.0 |
| Sun | Anki · cut 25%, publish, weekly log at fixed time | 1.5 |
|     | **Total** | **9.5** |

The remaining half hour is slack, and slack is load-bearing too. Anki is on
every row on purpose: it is fifteen minutes attached to something you
already do, not a session you schedule.

## 4. Months 2–3 — the first literacy pass

Now run one domain end to end. Use **Statistics & probability**, because
`03-spine.md` puts it first for a reason: it gates the honest reading of
every empirical claim in the other 43 domains, and half the ledger is closed
without it. Philosophy waits until month 4. Concentrating beats splitting —
the spine says six weeks of intensity beats ten months of thirty-minute
sessions, so give statistics the ledger hours and let philosophy be your
Wednesday reading only.

The sequence, in order:

- [ ] **Weeks 5–6: David Spiegelhalter, *The Art of Statistics*.** The whole
      book. It's the door because it teaches the reasoning through real
      cases and postpones formulas until you want them. Cards as you go.
- [ ] **One evening in week 5: Darrell Huff, *How to Lie with Statistics*.**
      Ninety minutes, written in 1954, and it inoculates you permanently
      against a large fraction of everything you will ever be shown.
- [ ] **Weeks 5–12: Stat 110 lectures 1–12, one or two a week**, with
      Blitzstein & Hwang's *Introduction to Probability* (free PDF)
      chapters 1–6 read alongside — read the chapter that matches the
      lecture, not ahead of it.
- [ ] **Problems, every week, non-negotiable.** 8–12 exercises per chapter,
      worked with the solutions covered, then checked. Target 60+ worked
      problems by day 90. Reading math is not learning math, and the
      feedback here is unusually clean: an answer is right or wrong, and a
      ten-line simulation settles any probability argument you're having
      with yourself.
- [ ] **Week 9: pick the artifact's target.** Find a statistical claim in
      the news and get the underlying paper — your new library card is how
      you get past the paywall.
- [ ] **Week 10: reconstruct what was actually done.** Sample and how it was
      drawn, comparison group, effect size in absolute rather than relative
      terms, the interval, the assumptions, and how many comparisons were
      really made.
- [ ] **Week 11: draft 2,000 words** on what the study does and does not
      support.
- [ ] **Week 12: cut it by a quarter, fix the ten worst sentences, publish
      it.** This is the week's piece, not extra work.

**What "done" means.** The artifact is public, dated, has your name on it,
and a reader could restate its argument back to you. That is the day-90 bar
and you should hit it. It is not yet a checked box in `02-map.md`: a full T3
pass is one survey work plus one canonical work plus the artifact, so
sometime later in year 1 you still owe the canon — Fisher's *The Design of
Experiments*, chapter 2, the lady tasting tea, fifteen pages. Check the box
then. And note the rule while you're there: promotion to T2 happens at an
annual review, never on enthusiasm.

## 5. The 90-day review

By day 90, this should exist:

- [ ] An SRS streak that is unbroken, or close enough that you know exactly
      which days you missed
- [ ] ~12 published pieces, each 500+ words, in public, under your name
- [ ] 12 rows in `log/2026-weekly.md`, including the honest empty ones
- [ ] One literacy artifact — the 2,000-word statistics piece
- [ ] An honest fundamentals self-rating in `02-map.md`, written on day 2
      and worth rereading now

### If you've missed most of it

You have not failed at a seventy-year plan in ninety days. That isn't a
consolation, it's arithmetic. But diagnose it now, because the same cause
will eat month 4. Three causes account for nearly all of it:

**Too much at once.** You tried to run statistics, philosophy, three habits,
and a reading list simultaneously and nothing got traction. *Fix:* cut to
one habit and one domain. Daily Anki and the weekly log — 20 minutes a day
total — then add publishing back at month 5. Ten hours a week is the design
load, not the entry load.

**No fixed time.** The work was scheduled as "this week" rather than
"Tuesday at 8pm," and a week has no edges to hold anything against. *Fix:*
put every row of the section 3 table in your calendar as a recurring event
with a real start time. The log ritual especially — a fixed hour is the
entire mechanism.

**No feedback.** You published into silence, watched lectures without doing
problems, and the whole thing stopped feeling like it connected to anything.
*Fix:* work problems with the answers covered, which gives you feedback in
seconds; and get one human reader who will tell you what's wrong. Principle
1 is explicit that practice without feedback is exposure, and exposure does
not compound.

**The rule.** A lapsed habit restarts at the next weekly log, at half size
if necessary — that's straight out of `templates/weekly-log.md`, and half
size is a real option, not a face-saving one. Seven cards a day and 250
words a week is a running system. Zero is not. You do not owe the missed
weeks anything, and you may not spend this week repaying them.

## 6. What NOT to do in the first 90 days

- **Don't buy anything beyond the year-1 list.** `03-spine.md` allows a
  library card, a notebook system, and Anki — under $50, and the restraint
  is the point. Gear bought before the habit exists is gear that goes
  unused, and every item you buy now is a decision you'd make better in
  month 10.
- **Don't try to pick your mastery spikes yet.** The T1 and T2 choices are
  the highest-stakes calls in the repo and you have no evidence. Forty-four
  literacy passes are what tell you which fields actually pull. Everything
  enters at T3.
- **Don't read ahead into `resources/` for domains years away.** Neuroscience
  is year 12. Reading its entry now costs an evening and buys nothing you'll
  still remember, and it is the most seductive form of not-starting available
  in this repo.
- **Don't optimize the note-taking system instead of taking notes.** No new
  app, no tagging scheme, no vault architecture. A plain text file and Anki
  are sufficient for ten years. Reorganizing notes produces a strong feeling
  of processing and near-zero learning.
- **Don't start a second language.** It begins in year 3 for good reasons —
  it's a T2-cost track that runs for years, and starting it before the three
  habits hold means it becomes the thing that breaks and takes the habits
  down with it.
- **Don't reorganize this repo before you've used it.** Restructuring
  someone's plan is the most comfortable way to avoid executing it. Change
  it at the annual review, with a year of evidence, which is what annual
  reviews are for.

## 7. After 90 days

Four files, in this order:

- **`03-spine.md`, Year 1** — the rest of your year. Philosophy is the
  second domain and starts now: Plato's *Euthyphro / Apology / Crito*, then
  Kenny's *A New History of Western Philosophy* as the survey, with the free
  will artifact by December. Year 1's December bar is two literacy artifacts,
  forty-plus weeks of public writing, an unbroken SRS streak, and the
  fundamentals self-rating done honestly.
- **`TIMELINE.md`** — all seventy years on one page. Worth one read now that
  you have three months of evidence that you'll actually do this, and worth
  a second read for the variants at the bottom: pre-built forks for four
  hours a week, for starting late, and for the interrupted stretch you will
  eventually need. Branch from it rather than obeying it.
- **`phases/decade-1-foundations.md`** — what this whole decade is for, and
  the failure modes specific to it.
- **`templates/annual-review.md`** — copy it to `log/2026-review.md` in
  December. That's where you decide whether statistics earns promotion,
  what to drop, and what year 2 looks like.

Once the three habits are genuinely automatic — call it month 6 — spend an
hour each on `05-current.md` and `04-frontier.md`. Both are set up once and
left running, and both are capped on purpose.

Consistency beats intensity. The agenda only asks that you never fully stop.
